Decomposition ledger

The foundational theoretical debate within the field of galaxy evolution focuses entirely on whether the observed descent of galaxies from the star-forming main sequence (SFMS) into the quiescent red sequence is driven mechanically by the physical exhaustion, expulsion, or starvation of molecular fuel (f

However, precisely attributing the relative weights of these two parameters across transitioning galaxy populations is severely complicated by instrumental limitations, tracer selection, and profound methodological assumptions embedded in the conversion of observable luminosity to physical mass.

Studies utilizing spatially resolved millimeter interferometry, such as those derived from the ALMaQUEST survey , approach this mathematical decomposition by isolating sub-galactic regions (individual spaxels) and mapping their localized properties onto resolved scaling relations. Lin et al. SFR-Tracer Timescale Mismatch:
Decomposing ΔsSFR requires a highly accurate measure of the current, instantaneous star formation rate. However, the literature routinely cautions that different multi-wavelength SFR tracers operate on inherently non-commensurable temporal scales, creating a severe chronological mismatch when divided by instantaneous millimeter gas masses. H$\alpha$ emission fundamentally traces star formation over the past ∼5−10 Myr (tracking massive, short-lived O and early B stars), while ultraviolet (UV) continuum traces activity over a much longer ∼100−300 Myr window, and infrared (IR) emission relies on bulk dust heating which can be heavily contaminated by older, non-star-forming stellar populations.

In post-starburst galaxies, where star formation has violently and recently ceased, the specific choice of tracer entirely dictates the resulting denominator of the SFE calculation. French et al. (2015)  explicitly note that the high prevalence of Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions (LINERs) in aging post-starburst systems severely contaminates H$\alpha$ fluxes. This requires complex, model-dependent spectral subtractions that propagate massive uncertainties directly into the SFE determination. If an SFR tracer with a long temporal memory (such as integrated UV or broadband IR) is utilized shortly after a violent quenching event, the SFR is vastly overestimated. This chronological ghosting masks the true, instantaneous suppression of SFE and artificially inflates the perceived ongoing depletion rate of the residual gas.   

Aperture Effects and Spatial Extrapolation:
The physical aperture of the observing instrument heavily biases the resulting mathematical decomposition. Single-dish radio observations (e.g., the IRAM 30m telescope utilized extensively in the xCOLD GASS survey  and by French et al. 2015 ) capture the total global gas reservoir but completely blur out small-scale spatial variations, yielding a single, homogenized data point for the entire galaxy. Conversely, modern ALMA interferometry frequently captures only the central few kiloparsecs of a galaxy, risking the total omission of extended, low-surface-brightness molecular halos. If central f

As a prominent central stellar bulge grows in mass and density, it fundamentally alters the local gravitational potential. Central vs global

A critical, highly contested vector of inquiry in the published literature involves determining whether the cessation of star formation manifests as a localized phenomenon (e.g., the violent clearing or stabilization of the central kiloparsec) or a global, monolithic collapse of the galaxy's entire extended gas reservoir. Resolving the precise spatial distribution of both the retained molecular gas and the residual ongoing star formation provides a mechanism to test competing, scale-dependent quenching models, such as central Active Galactic Nucleus (AGN) feedback, internal bar-driven gas funneling, inside-out morphological quenching, and outside-in environmental ram pressure stripping.

AGN association tests

The theoretical potential for Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN) to serve as the primary physical catalyst for either the direct mechanical expulsion of gas (reservoir depletion) or the continuous injection of turbulence and thermal energy (suppressed SFE) is a recurring, dominant theme throughout the collected literature. The corpus reports numerous observational tests attempting to link these distinct quenching scenarios directly to AGN presence or historical activity, though the exact causal nature of the relationship remains a subject of intense and open investigation. It is vital to state explicitly that observational correlations derived from physical telescopes are non-commensurable with theoretical predictions derived from numerical cosmological simulations.

Setton et al. (2025)  examine the exceptionally massive quiescent galaxy RUBIES-UDS-QG-z7 at high redshift, which, despite its complete lack of optical star formation, retains a highly significant cold gas fraction (f
gas
	​

≳20%). They report the detection of a distinct, blueshifted velocity offset in the extended [CII] halo gas. The authors claim this kinematic signature is structurally consistent with a tentative, high-velocity gas outflow detected previously in local MgII absorption lines. The study explicitly claims that this specific kinematic signature suggests a past, violent episode of AGN activity that successfully expelled molecular gas from the inner galactic disk out into the circumgalactic halo. Crucially, in this specific published claim, the AGN is associated not with the total physical depletion of the gas from the overarching dark matter halo, but rather strictly with its spatial displacement. This mechanism results in a galactic system that appears completely centrally depleted (mimicking exhaustion) but in reality retains a massive, highly inefficient global gas reservoir trapped in the halo.   

French et al. (2015)  and Yesuf et al. (2017)  extensively investigate the complex intersection of aging post-starburst systems and low-luminosity AGN activity. French et al. formally report that almost all of their CO-detected post-starburst galaxies—which, by definition, host substantial, retained molecular gas reservoirs but exhibit heavily suppressed SFEs—display optical emission line ratios strictly consistent with LINERs (Low-Ionization Nuclear Emission-line Regions). Similarly, Yesuf et al. focus their study explicitly on Seyfert-classified post-starbursts. These linked studies claim that the unexpectedly high prevalence of AGN and LINER optical signatures in these definitively gas-rich transition galaxies suggests that AGN feedback may not be solely or even primarily responsible for violently expelling all cold gas out of the potential well, as standard monolithic depletion models often suggest. Instead, these authors claim that the central AGN might primarily serve to inject thermal heat or mechanical turbulence into the ISM, effectively stabilizing the retained molecular reservoir against standard gravitational collapse and suppressing the SFE without requiring the total physical expulsion of the fuel.   

In a non-commensurable theoretical approach, Piotrowska et al. (2022)  utilize a machine-learning Random Forest architecture to extract broad theoretical predictions from flagship cosmological hydrodynamical simulations (specifically EAGLE, Illustris, and IllustrisTNG), subsequently classifying simulation medians against observational SDSS data. They claim that their numerical models establish a direct, predictive link between the integrated, long-term history of AGN feedback (traced by total central black hole mass) and the eventual descent of massive galaxies from the main sequence. According to their explicit theoretical analysis, integrated AGN feedback in the simulations acts universally to simultaneously drive down both the global gas fraction and the star formation efficiency in tandem, creating a highly mixed-mode quenching pathway that closely mirrors the statistical observational proxies they derive from local SDSS central galaxies.   

What would discriminate

The literature explicitly identifies and proposes several critical observational advancements, necessary instrumentation upgrades, and profound methodological shifts required to successfully navigate the lingering ambiguities between reservoir depletion and suppressed efficiency in transitioning galaxies.

High-Resolution, Multi-Phase Gas Mapping:
Studies such as Pan et al. (2024)  and Zabel et al. (2023)  emphasize repeatedly that unresolved, global gas measurements fundamentally obscure the localized physics of quenching. The literature proposes that the deployment of highly spatially resolved interferometry (specifically utilizing extended baselines on ALMA and the future deployment of the Square Kilometre Array) capable of simultaneously mapping atomic (HI), diffuse molecular (CO), and highly dense molecular (HCN, HCO+) gas across the entire galactic disk and out into the circumgalactic medium is strictly necessary. Only by precisely mapping the exact spatial distribution and kinematic state of the gas relative to the highly localized star formation rate surface density (Σ
SFR
	​

) can observers determine if gas is physically missing (depleted via inside-out AGN clearing or outside-in environmental stripping) or merely rendered inert (suppressed SFE via morphological stabilization or injected turbulence).   

Deeper Integration for High-Redshift Quiescent Systems:
Williams et al. (2021)  and Spilker et al. (2018)  explicitly note that the current understanding of molecular gas fractions in high-redshift (z>1) quiescent galaxies is severely limited by a heavy reliance on non-detections. The literature proposes that heavily allocating highly contested observing time to achieve massive increases in depth (factors of 10 or more) is absolutely required to transform current 3$\sigma$ upper limits into actual, verifiable detections. Until the exact physical mass of the residual gas reservoirs in these early universe quiescent systems is measured, the literature states it is functionally impossible to discriminate definitively between theoretical models predicting total, violent gas expulsion versus those predicting the quiet retention of a small, highly inefficient gas fraction. The stated feasibility limit for this advancement is the extreme, often prohibitive cost of ALMA integration time required to reach these necessary depths for statistically significant, volume-limited sample sizes.
