Definition drift register
E1: Quenched fraction at fixed stellar mass

The characterization of what constitutes a "quenched" or "quiescent" galaxy undergoes continuous definitional drift across the astronomical literature, fundamentally complicating efforts to map commensurable redshift evolution across cosmic time. The literature routinely alternates between, and occasionally conflates, methodologies based on pure photometric color space and those based on specific star formation rate (sSFR) thresholds, generating a chronological dissonance in the reported evolution of the stellar mass function.

One of the most utilized paradigms for defining quiescence relies on the rest-frame UVJ color-color diagram, mapping (U-V) against (V-J) colors. This methodology, famously employed by the Muzzin et al. (2013) analysis within the COSMOS/UltraVISTA fields, is prized for its ability to break the pervasive degeneracy between galaxies that are genuinely dominated by older, cooler stellar populations and those that are heavily dust-obscured but actively star-forming. By defining a specific threshold line in this parameter space (typically U−V>1.3 and V−J<1.5), the literature successfully isolates a population whose spectral energy distributions (SEDs) reflect a sustained absence of newly formed massive O and B stars. However, this metric is fundamentally historical; a galaxy relies on the passive aging of its stellar population to migrate into the UVJ quiescent wedge, a process that can require hundreds of millions of years following the actual physical cessation of star formation.

In stark contrast, analytical frameworks heavily integrated with hydrodynamical simulations (such as the IllustrisTNG suite) or targeted spectroscopic surveys frequently define quiescence via an instantaneous sSFR cut, or as a set physical distance below the redshift-dependent Star-Forming Main Sequence (SFMS). An sSFR threshold, such as sSFR<10

Definition drift becomes highly problematic when the overarching narrative of galaxy evolution attempts to seamlessly stitch together a low-redshift fraction derived from sSFR measurements with a high-redshift fraction derived from UVJ color selection. Because the physical mechanisms tracked by these two tracers operate on vastly different temporal scales—one tracking immediate truncation via feedback or starvation, the other tracking the eventual cooling of the composite stellar population—comparing them inherently injects a chronological lag into the dataset. A galaxy might simultaneously qualify as "quenched" under an sSFR cut but "star-forming" under a UVJ cut because it has not yet completed its color migration. Consequently, synthesizing these disparate definitions creates a synthetic evolutionary slope that misrepresents the true assembly history of the quiescent galaxy population.

E4: AGN-driven outflow incidence at matched selection

The demographic quantification of galactic outflows, and particularly the incidence rates separating star-formation-driven winds from those driven by Active Galactic Nuclei (AGN), suffers from definition drift tied to the spectroscopic architecture of the observing facilities and the analytical thresholds used to define an "outflow."

In comprehensive integral field unit (IFU) surveys such as KMOS3D (Förster Schreiber et al. 2019), outflow signatures are meticulously isolated through multi-component Gaussian modeling of rest-optical nebular emission lines, primarily H-alpha and [NII]. Within this framework, an outflow is formally defined by the statistical necessity of a "broad" underlying velocity component to accurately fit the emission line profile beneath the narrow systemic emission representing the host galaxy's rotating disk. The literature drifts significantly when comparing IFU-derived incidence rates with those obtained from traditional single-fiber or long-slit spectroscopic surveys. An IFU provides spatially resolved kinematics, allowing researchers to physically separate a high-velocity, nuclear AGN outflow (often exhibiting Full Width at Half Maximum velocities in excess of 1000 to 2000 km/s) from extended, star-formation-driven winds (typically characterized by FWHM velocities of ~400-500 km/s) dispersed across the star-forming disk.

A single-fiber observation indiscriminately integrates the total light from the galaxy. In this scenario, a spatially compact, high-velocity AGN outflow can easily be completely diluted by the overwhelming surface brightness of the narrow H-alpha emission from the extended star-forming disk, particularly in lower signal-to-noise (S/N) regimes. When the literature stitches together single-fiber surveys at low redshift with IFU surveys at cosmic noon, it alters the estimand from "spatially resolved multi-component kinematics" to "integrated line-profile asymmetry." This mismatch artificially modulates the calculated incidence rate of outflows, generally suppressing the detection of modest AGN winds in single-fiber studies, and corrupting any claims of redshift evolution in the feedback demographic.

E5: Radio-AGN fraction at fixed stellar/halo mass

The fraction of the massive galaxy population hosting radio-loud AGN is a crucial metric for evaluating the efficacy of "maintenance mode" (or jet-mode) feedback in preventing the catastrophic cooling of hot halo gas. The definitional drift surrounding this metric primarily centers on the criteria used to identify "radio excess" and the sub-classification of the accretion mechanism.

The standard methodology for calculating the radio-AGN fraction involves cross-referencing radio continuum surveys with optical/near-infrared catalogs to identify sources whose radio luminosity vastly exceeds the theoretical emission expected from their ongoing star formation. Because star formation generates radio emission via synchrotron radiation from supernova remnants, isolating the AGN contribution requires a precise understanding of the host galaxy's underlying star formation rate (SFR) via the tight infrared-radio correlation. The definitional drift emerges when attempting to link deep, multi-wavelength selected high-redshift surveys (such as the VLA-COSMOS 3GHz program utilized by Smolčić et al. 2017) with local, spectroscopically complete benchmarks (such as the SDSS/NVSS cross-matches analyzed by Best et al. 2005 and 2014).

Local studies benefit from high-resolution optical spectroscopy, allowing the use of BPT diagnostic diagrams and velocity dispersion measurements to securely separate true Low-Excitation Radio Galaxies (LERGs) and High-Excitation Radio Galaxies (HERGs) from heavily obscured starburst interlopers. High-redshift studies, lacking deep rest-optical spectroscopy for their full statistical samples, must frequently rely on broad-band Spectral Energy Distribution (SED) fitting to estimate the host galaxy's SFR. If the SED templates systematically underestimate the true, deeply obscured SFR of a high-redshift dusty star-forming galaxy, the calculated radio excess will be artificially inflated. This results in the misclassification of extreme starbursts as radio AGN, artificially boosting the radio-AGN fraction at high redshift and mimicking a false evolutionary trend that suggests jet-mode feedback was more prevalent in the early universe, when it may simply be an artifact of SED fitting limitations.

E6: Metallicity-relation (MZR/FMR) normalization and scatter

The study of the Mass-Metallicity Relation (MZR) and the Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR, which incorporates the modulating effect of the SFR) exhibits some of the most profound definitional drift in extragalactic astrophysics, deeply rooted in the calibration mechanisms used to estimate gas-phase oxygen abundances.

The literature is heavily fractured between the utilization of empirical "strong-line" calibrations and the physically direct "electron temperature" (T

The direct method relies on the detection of highly temperature-sensitive auroral emission lines (most notably [O III] λ4363) to accurately measure the electron temperature of the ionized HII regions, yielding a robust, physical derivation of the oxygen abundance. However, these auroral lines are intrinsically faint and typically only detectable in local surveys, extreme starbursts, or heavily magnified gravitationally lensed systems. Consequently, major high-redshift statistical surveys, such as the MOSFIRE Deep Evolution Field (MOSDEF; Sanders et al. 2021), must rely on strong-line ratios (e.g., [N II]/H-alpha, O3N2, or R23) to infer metallicities. These strong-line diagnostics are calibrated either empirically against local direct-method samples or theoretically via photoionization models.

The definitional drift severely compromises evolutionary claims when the literature stitches together local direct-method baselines with high-redshift strong-line data, or worse, mixes different strong-line calibrations across redshift bins. High-redshift galaxies are observationally confirmed to exhibit fundamentally different physical conditions within their star-forming regions compared to local analogs, including harder ionizing radiation fields, elevated ionization parameters, and distinct nitrogen-to-oxygen (N/O) abundance ratios. Applying a strong-line calibration anchored in local universe physics to a z∼2 galaxy systematically biases the derived oxygen abundance. Therefore, any claimed redshift evolution in the MZR normalization that crosses calibration frameworks does not reflect isolated cosmic chemical evolution, but rather a complex convolution of chemical evolution with the systemic divergence of the calibrations themselves.

Metallicity Calibration Discrepancies

Published mixed-estimand claim: Early and ongoing characterizations of the redshift evolution of the Mass-Metallicity Relation (MZR) frequently compare the overall metallicity normalization of high-redshift galaxy populations (often derived via theoretical strong-line photoionization models) against the local z∼0 SDSS benchmark (typically derived via empirical strong-line calibrations). This cross-calibration comparison has historically led to claims of dramatic, rapid chemical depletion and extremely steep MZR slopes in the early universe.
Criticism and Reason: Sanders et al. (2021) and Topping et al. (2021) systematically critiqued the practice of mixing metallicity estimands across redshift bins. Their analysis demonstrated that applying different calibrations to the exact same high-redshift dataset can shift the overall normalization by several tenths of a dex. Furthermore, they highlighted that when the non-Gaussian metallicity uncertainties inherent to strong-line calibrations are not properly forward-modeled, the derived MZR normalization is artificially lowered by ~0.2 dex and the slope is flattened by ~20%. Claims of MZR evolution are strictly non-commensurable unless the local and high-redshift samples are analyzed using an identical suite of strong-line indicators or a universally anchored direct-method approach.

UVJ versus sSFR Quiescent Fraction Evolution

Published mixed-estimand claim: Numerous cosmological simulation papers (including early comparative analyses within the IllustrisTNG framework) have benchmarked their internal, sSFR-defined instantaneous quenched fractions directly against observational datasets (such as Muzzin et al. 2013) that relied exclusively on photometric UVJ color-color cuts to define quiescence. This direct comparison is often used to validate the feedback efficiency tuned within the simulation.
Criticism and Reason: Donnari et al. (2019, 2020) and Dickey et al. (2021) specifically identified the physical hazards of this comparative practice. They highlighted that the timescale for a simulated galaxy to cross a strict sSFR threshold into quiescence is nearly instantaneous following a major feedback or gas-stripping event. However, the timescale required for that same galaxy's stellar population to age, cool, and redden sufficiently to cross the observational UVJ threshold can span hundreds of millions of years. Stitching these fractions together creates a fundamental temporal offset, artificially suppressing the theoretical early-universe quenched fraction when compared to observational UVJ data, and penalizing simulations for a lag that is merely an artifact of the color-space estimand.
