Galaxy Evolution

*[Written from a galactic astronomy perspective by Gemini]*

Overview

Galaxy evolution studies how dark matter halos, gas supply, star formation, feedback, environment, and cosmic time combine to build the observed galaxy population. The useful modern picture is not a single ladder from blue spirals to red ellipticals. It is a regulated baryon cycle: halos assemble and acquire gas, galaxies convert only part of that gas into stars, feedback returns energy and enriched material to surrounding reservoirs, and quenching processes eventually reduce or shut down star formation.

A practical way to think about the field is through a budget. Halo mass sets the depth of the potential well, gas inflow sets the fuel supply, star formation sets the consumption rate, and outflows plus recycling set how much gas stays available later. A simple bookkeeping identity is often written as $\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{gas}}/\mathrm{d}t = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{in}} - \mathrm{SFR} - \dot{M}_{\mathrm{out}} + \dot{M}_{\mathrm{recycle}}$, which is useful because it separates supply, consumption, loss, and return. The red sequence, green valley, and blue cloud remain useful observational landmarks, but they are outcomes rather than mechanisms.

A physical synthesis therefore starts with causal structure: what sets the baryon supply, what regulates star formation, what shuts galaxies down, and which observations separate internal feedback from external processing. AGN-related observations indicate scoped, context-dependent mechanisms rather than a universal quenching explanation.

Discovery & History

Modern galaxy evolution emerged from several overlapping ideas. Early work organized galaxies by morphology, most visibly in the Hubble sequence, which sorted systems by appearance rather than by physical cause. Later work added star-formation diagnostics, color bimodality, and gas measurements, showing that color and morphology do not map one-to-one onto a single evolutionary path.

The field then shifted toward a physical view of galaxy growth. Dark matter simulations established the halo framework, while surveys of stellar populations, metallicity, and gas content showed that baryons are processed unevenly inside halos. The idea of a regulated baryon cycle followed naturally: inflow brings in fresh gas, star formation consumes part of it, outflows move energy and metals outward, and some expelled gas returns later. Integral-field spectroscopy, deep imaging, and multiwavelength surveys now make it possible to test these links directly rather than infer them from color alone.

Physical Properties

Galaxy evolution is controlled by a small set of physical reservoirs and observables. Halo mass and assembly history set the gravitational environment. Typical galaxy halos span roughly $10^{11}$ to $10^{13}\,M_\odot$ for ordinary systems, while group and cluster halos extend to $\sim10^{14}$ to $10^{15}\,M_\odot$. Stellar mass and star formation rate measure how much baryonic material has already been converted into stars and how fast the current conversion is proceeding. Gas fractions, metallicity, dust, and molecular and atomic gas tracers show how much fuel remains and how processed that fuel has become.

The same galaxy can look different depending on which phase is being measured. Ionized gas, molecular gas, neutral gas, hot halo gas, and stars do not respond to feedback in the same way or on the same timescale. Cold star-forming gas is often near $10^2$ to $10^4$ K, the warm circumgalactic medium is commonly traced around $10^4$ to $10^6$ K, and group atmospheres and hot halos can reach $10^6$ to $10^7$ K. That is why a single label such as "quenched" can hide important differences between temporary suppression, long-lived shutdown, and rapid gas removal. Structural properties such as size, bulge fraction, bar strength, and merger disturbance add another layer because they can either trigger central inflow or reflect earlier dynamical change.

At low mass, shallow potential wells make galaxies sensitive to feedback and to whether outflowing gas actually escapes. At high mass, hot halos, black-hole growth, and long cooling times matter more, so the relevant questions become whether inflow is prevented, whether the central gas supply is exhausted, and whether feedback keeps hot gas from cooling back onto the galaxy. A useful timescale comparison is the depletion time, $t_{\mathrm{dep}} = M_{\mathrm{gas}}/\mathrm{SFR}$, because it separates a galaxy that is simply using fuel quickly from one that has lost or stabilized its fuel reservoir.

Circumgalactic gas is especially important for this picture, but it is also one of the hardest reservoirs to measure. It is faint, often seen only through absorption against background sources or through low-surface-brightness emission, and different tracers probe different temperatures and densities. That means the same halo can appear gas-rich in one tracer and depleted in another. For that reason, recycling claims should be tied to the phase actually observed, the spatial scale of the measurement, and the completeness of the background-source geometry. In practice, the best tests compare ionized-gas kinematics, molecular depletion times, neutral-gas columns, and CGM absorption in the same halo rather than relying on a single tracer.

Current Research

The current literature emphasizes that quenching is not a single event. It can be rapid or slow, central or environmental, preventive or ejective, leaving distinct signatures in stellar populations, gas reservoirs, molecular excitation, hot atmospheres, and morphology. AGN feedback is a real but scoped pathway, with strong constraints against turning local or model-bounded evidence into a universal law.

Consequently, AGN feedback is best treated as a localized mechanism that can regulate some hosts and some phases, rather than as a guaranteed universal driver of host-galaxy quenching.

Gas cycling is the clearest bridge between local star formation and galaxy-scale regulation. Inflows feed the interstellar medium, the interstellar medium forms stars, and feedback sends some material back into the circumgalactic medium. Some outflows are temporary, some are recycled, and some may escape if the halo is shallow enough or the coupling is strong enough.

<!--claim:2945-->Gas-removal claims are constrained by gas recycling and host mass: empirical models indicate substantial fallback of outflowing material, and low-redshift, low-mass systems have shallow potential wells where stellar winds are often insufficient for permanent gas escape.<!--/claim:2945--> <!--cite:28066,28075-->

Not every outflow empties a galaxy. Some gas can leave the star-forming disk but later return, and some feedback regulates turbulence without depleting the reservoir. In specific cases, such as M51, observations show localized compressive or positive feedback rather than host-wide quenching. <!--cite:28060-->

Environment also matters, but not in a single uniform way. Groups and clusters can strip gas, starve inflow, perturb disks, or accelerate mergers. The same observed quiescent state can therefore arise from different channels, and the right interpretation depends on the gas phase, the surrounding halo, and the galaxy's recent assembly history.

Chemical enrichment provides a long-timescale record of these processes. Metallicity scaling relations encode how much gas has been processed into stars, how much enriched material has been lost, and how much low-metallicity gas has diluted the interstellar medium. At high redshift, the same observables become harder to interpret because dust, AGN light, stellar-population assumptions, and spectroscopic completeness can all bias inferred masses and star-formation histories.

Open Questions

The main open questions are mechanism-level rather than taxonomic. Which galaxies truly lose gas to the halo or beyond, and which ones mostly recycle it? How strongly do radio jets couple to cold and warm gas in different environments? Where is the transition between stellar-feedback-dominated regulation and AGN-linked regulation? When does environment merely change the timing of quenching, and when does it supply the dominant quenching channel?

The most useful present-day tensions line up with those questions. Massive galaxies at early times still stress model assumptions, cold streams may survive longer than simple hot-halo pictures predict, feedback efficiency changes with numerical resolution, and stripping must be separated from starvation when dense environments are involved. Empirical constraints from the SDSS DR17 pilot show that broad optical AGN selection correlates with lower catalog sSFR in matched control samples, but that association by itself does not establish causal quenching.

Those tensions should be resolved with observables that map to physical mechanisms. X-ray cavities and radio jets test hot-gas heating, molecular and ionized outflows test gas removal or redistribution, neutral-gas surveys test reservoir depletion, and wide-area environment surveys test whether the same host state appears in clusters, groups, or the field. The most decisive measurements are the ones that can distinguish three outcomes: gas is expelled, gas is delayed and recycled, or gas stays bound but is heated above the star-forming threshold. The cleanest near-term comparisons are the ones that pair ionized-gas kinematics with molecular depletion times and then check whether the same systems also show CGM absorption or hot-halo structure.
