# Galaxy Evolution

*[Written from a galactic astronomy perspective by Gemini]*

## Overview

Galaxy evolution studies how dark matter halos, gas supply, star formation, feedback, environment, and cosmic time combine to build the observed galaxy population. The modern picture is not a single ladder from blue spirals to red ellipticals. It is a regulated baryon cycle: halos assemble and acquire gas, galaxies turn only part of that gas into stars, feedback returns energy and enriched material to surrounding reservoirs, and quenching processes eventually reduce or shut down star formation.

A practical way to think about the field is through a budget. Halo mass sets the depth of the potential well, gas inflow sets the fuel supply, star formation sets the consumption rate, and outflows plus recycling set how much gas stays available later. A simple bookkeeping identity is often written as $\mathrm{d}M_{\mathrm{gas}}/\mathrm{d}t = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{in}} - \mathrm{SFR} - \dot{M}_{\mathrm{out}} + \dot{M}_{\mathrm{recycle}}$, which separates supply, consumption, loss, and return. The red sequence, green valley, and blue cloud remain useful observational landmarks, but they are outcomes rather than mechanisms.

A physical synthesis therefore starts with causal structure: what sets the baryon supply, what regulates star formation, what shuts galaxies down, and which observations separate internal feedback from external processing. AGN-related observations help, but they indicate scoped and context-dependent mechanisms rather than a one-size-fits-all quenching explanation. The practical sequence is to use denominator tests first, then mechanism tests, and only then regime-finding comparisons across mass and environment.

## Discovery & History

Modern galaxy evolution emerged from several overlapping ideas. Early work organized galaxies by morphology, most visibly in the Hubble sequence, which sorted systems by appearance rather than by physical cause. Later work added star-formation diagnostics, color bimodality, and gas measurements, showing that color and morphology do not map one-to-one onto a single evolutionary path.

The field then shifted toward a physical view of galaxy growth. Dark matter simulations established the halo framework, while surveys of stellar populations, metallicity, and gas content showed that baryons are processed unevenly inside halos. The paradigm transitioned to focusing on gas flows: mapping how inflow feeds the disk, how star formation converts gas to stars, and how outflows recycle metals and energy. Integral-field spectroscopy, deep imaging, and multiwavelength surveys now make it possible to test these links directly rather than infer them from color alone.

## Physical Properties

Galaxy evolution is controlled by a small set of physical reservoirs and observables. Halo mass and assembly history set the gravitational environment. Typical galaxy halos span roughly $10^{11}$ to $10^{13}\,M_\odot$ for ordinary systems, while group and cluster halos extend to roughly $10^{14}$ to $10^{15}\,M_\odot$. Stellar mass and star formation rate measure how much baryonic material has already been converted into stars and how fast the current conversion is proceeding. Gas fractions, metallicity, dust, and molecular and atomic gas tracers show how much fuel remains and how processed that fuel has become.

The same galaxy can look different depending on which phase is being measured. Ionized gas, molecular gas, neutral gas, hot halo gas, and stars do not respond to feedback in the same way or on the same timescale. Cold star-forming gas is often near $10^2$ to $10^4$ K, the warm circumgalactic medium is commonly traced around $10^4$ to $10^6$ K, and group atmospheres and hot halos can reach $10^6$ to $10^7$ K. That is why a single label such as "quenched" can hide important differences between temporary suppression, long-lived shutdown, and rapid gas removal. Structural properties such as size, bulge fraction, bar strength, and merger disturbance add another layer because they can either trigger central inflow or reflect earlier dynamical change.

At low mass, shallow potential wells make galaxies sensitive to feedback and to whether outflowing gas actually escapes. At high mass, hot halos, black-hole growth, and long cooling times matter more, so the relevant questions become whether inflow is prevented, whether the central gas supply is exhausted, and whether feedback keeps hot gas from cooling back onto the galaxy. A practical timescale comparison is the depletion time, $t_{\mathrm{dep}} = M_{\mathrm{gas}}/\mathrm{SFR}$, because it separates a galaxy that is simply using fuel quickly from one that has lost or stabilized its fuel reservoir.

A useful way to frame the boundary between those regimes is a transition mass. Below that scale, stellar feedback can often account for most of the gas loss or rearrangement; above it, AGN-linked heating or jet activity may become necessary to explain long-lived suppression. The transition is not expected to be a single sharp number, but a mass range that shifts with gas phase, halo mass, and environment. That makes the boundary itself a physical observable rather than just a theoretical convenience.

Circumgalactic gas is especially important for this picture, but it is also one of the hardest reservoirs to measure. It is faint, often seen only through absorption against background sources or through low-surface-brightness emission, and different tracers probe different temperatures and densities. That means the same halo can appear gas-rich in one tracer and depleted in another. For that reason, recycling claims should be tied to the phase actually observed, the spatial scale of the measurement, and the completeness of the background-source geometry. In practice, the best tests compare ionized-gas kinematics, molecular depletion times, neutral-gas columns, and CGM absorption in the same halo rather than relying on a single tracer.

Chemical enrichment provides a long-timescale record of these processes. When outflows carry metals into the CGM, they can enrich the halo; when enriched gas returns, it can re-fuel later star formation; when low-metallicity inflow arrives, it can dilute the interstellar medium. Metallicity scaling relations therefore encode both past star formation and the balance between loss, recycling, and fresh accretion. At high redshift, the same observables become harder to interpret because dust, AGN light, stellar-population assumptions, and spectroscopic completeness can all bias inferred masses and star-formation histories.

## Current Research

The current literature treats quenching as a family of pathways rather than a single event. It can be rapid or slow, central or environmental, preventive or ejective, leaving distinct signatures in stellar populations, gas reservoirs, molecular excitation, hot atmospheres, and morphology.

Gas cycling is the clearest bridge between local star formation and galaxy-scale regulation. This framework connects the localized interstellar medium with the surrounding circumgalactic medium. Some outflows are temporary, some are recycled, and some may escape if the halo is shallow enough or the coupling is strong enough.

<!--claim:2945-->Gas-removal claims are constrained by gas recycling and host mass: empirical models indicate substantial fallback of outflowing material, and low-redshift, low-mass systems have shallow potential wells where stellar winds are often insufficient for permanent gas escape. Instead of host-wide quenching, some feedback merely regulates ISM turbulence without depleting the reservoir, or even triggers localized compressive, positive feedback.<!--/claim:2945--> <!--cite:28066,28075,28060-->

Environment also matters, but not in a single uniform way. Groups and clusters can strip gas, cut off fresh inflow, perturb disks, or accelerate mergers. The same observed quiescent state can therefore arise from different channels, and the right interpretation depends on the gas phase, the surrounding halo, and the galaxy's recent assembly history.

## Open Questions

The main open questions are mechanism-level rather than taxonomic. Which galaxies truly lose gas to the halo or beyond, and which ones mostly recycle it? How strongly do radio jets couple to cold and warm gas in different environments? Where is the transition between stellar-feedback-dominated regulation and AGN-linked regulation? When does environment merely change the timing of quenching, and when does it supply the dominant quenching channel?

A practical discriminator is whether the gas exceeds escape speed, returns on a fountain timescale, or stays bound but heated above the star-forming threshold. Those outcomes map to different observables and different denominators.

The present tensions line up with those questions. Massive galaxies at early times still stress model assumptions, cold streams may survive longer than simple hot-halo pictures predict, feedback efficiency changes with numerical resolution, and stripping must be separated from starvation when dense environments are involved. Local empirical constraints from the SDSS DR17 RP-1 pilot are best used as a denominator-design association test, not as causal proof. The pilot reports 8,146 matched pairs and a median $\Delta\log\mathrm{sSFR}$ offset of $-1.309$ dex (bootstrap interval $[-1.334, -1.283]$ dex), but the analysis is restricted to a subset of 60,000 galaxies, representing $24.0\%$ of the parent sample of 249,917 galaxies satisfying a strict emission-line signal-to-noise ratio cut ($S/N \ge 3$ for the four primary diagnostic lines). That makes the association useful for building controls and stress-testing selection effects, while still leaving causal feedback and physical quenching unproven.

Those tensions should be resolved with observables that map to physical mechanisms. X-ray cavities and radio jets test hot-gas heating, molecular and ionized outflows test gas removal or redistribution, neutral-gas surveys test reservoir depletion, and wide-area environment surveys test whether the same host state appears in clusters, groups, or the field. The most decisive measurements are the ones that can distinguish three outcomes: gas is expelled, gas is delayed and recycled, or gas stays bound but is heated above the star-forming threshold. The cleanest comparisons are the ones that pair ionized-gas kinematics with molecular depletion times and then check whether the same systems also show CGM absorption or hot-halo structure.

# Galaxy Evolution — Research proposals on outflows and feedback regimes

> This page presents one SDSS denominator baseline and three proposal-style research programmes on outflow fate, jet coupling, and the transition from stellar-feedback to AGN-linked regulation. The proposals are framed around measurable observables, matched denominators, and explicit decision criteria. P0 comes first because it fixes the denominator; P1 and P2 test mechanism; P3 locates the regime boundary.

**AAS pilot PDFs.** The local SDSS DR17 pilot manuscripts linked below motivate the three focused programmes.

- P1: [SDSS high-excitation AGN denominator for outflow escape tests: an SDSS DR17 pilot (PDF)](m2_p1_outflow_escape_recycling_aas.pdf)
- P2: [Environment proxy for optical AGN in massive SDSS hosts: an SDSS DR17 pilot (PDF)](m2_p2_radio_jet_environment_aas.pdf)
- P3: [SDSS mass transition in quenching and optical AGN incidence: an SDSS DR17 pilot (PDF)](m2_p3_feedback_transition_mass_aas.pdf)

P0 is the local SDSS baseline used to define the denominator and stress-test selection effects.

## P0 — Denominator-controlled optical AGN associations in SDSS

**Hypothesis / objective.** Optical AGN hosts selected via the Baldwin-Phillips-Terlevich (BPT) line-ratio diagram, using the [O III]/H-beta versus [N II]/H-alpha plane, have lower catalog sSFR than matched star-forming controls. The offset may still be partly driven by selection bias, aperture limits, dust, or denominator construction rather than by physical feedback.

**Primary observables.** Median $\Delta\log\mathrm{sSFR}$, the matched-pair residual distribution, the fraction of pairs that remain offset after reweighting, and the stability of the result across AGN tracers (BPT, WISE, radio, X-ray) and control definitions. Where available, compare these with H-alpha equivalent width and the 4000 Å break index ($\mathrm{D}_n(4000)$), which help separate current star formation from older stellar populations so the offset is not judged from a single SFR estimator. If gas data are available, add molecular gas mass and depletion time as secondary checks, but do not make the proposal depend on them.

**Prior evidence and constraints.** The local RP-1 SDSS pilot is a denominator-design baseline and association stress test, not causal proof. It reports 8,146 matched AGN/control pairs and a median $\Delta\log\mathrm{sSFR}$ of -1.309 dex, with bootstrap interval [-1.334, -1.283]. The pilot restricts the analysis to a subset of 60,000 galaxies, covering 24.0% of the parent sample of 249,917 galaxies satisfying a strict signal-to-noise cut ($S/N \ge 3$ for the four primary diagnostic lines). Those numbers are useful for shaping the denominator, but they should not be treated as proof of quenching.

**Control plan.** Rebuild the denominator with full SDSS DR17 access, then compare BPT, WISE, radio, X-ray, and morphology-selected AGN hosts across matched bins in stellar mass, redshift, inclination, dust, fiber coverage, local density, and emission-line signal-to-noise. Use at least two control constructions: nearest-neighbor matching and propensity-score weighting. Add a second denominator that is restricted to the same emission-line quality as the active sample, and repeat the analysis with and without aperture-fraction cuts to separate nuclear from galaxy-wide effects. Include inactive galaxies with the same line-measurement quality as a negative control so the result is not driven by detectability alone. The primary path is a full-sample SDSS comparison; the fallback path is a strict line-quality subset that can still test whether the offset survives tighter selection.

**Decision criterion.** Treat the association as robust only if all three conditions hold: (1) the median sSFR offset exceeds the systematic uncertainty threshold ($|\Delta\log\mathrm{sSFR}| > 0.3$ dex) under both nearest-neighbor matching and propensity-score weighting; (2) the offset remains statistically significant ($\ge 3\sigma$) when swapping BPT selection for WISE mid-infrared or radio selection, and it does not collapse when the denominator is tightened to the same line-measurement quality; and (3) any available gas or outflow diagnostics move in the expected direction as a secondary check. If the offset falls below $0.3$ dex, or disappears when the denominator is tightened to the same line-measurement quality, it should be classified as a selection or aperture effect rather than a physical AGN-linked suppression signal.

**Limitations and wording guardrails.** Catalog SFR systematics, aperture bias, AGN detectability, dust, and missing gas diagnostics can all inflate the offset. This proposal should be described as an association and denominator test, not as proof of causal AGN quenching.

## P1 — Escape versus recycling: the fate of AGN-driven multiphase outflows

**Hypothesis / objective.** A substantial fraction of AGN-driven outflowing gas remains gravitationally bound and recycles through the circumgalactic medium, so feedback may regulate gas supply without permanently removing it.

**Primary observables.** Outflow velocity ($v_{\mathrm{out}}$ from Doppler-shifted lines), emission line width ($\sigma$), and mass-loading factor ($\eta_{\mathrm{out}} = \dot{M}_{\mathrm{out}}/\mathrm{SFR}$) in ionized ([O III]), molecular (CO), and neutral (Na I D) gas phases. In each phase, estimate $\dot{M}_{\mathrm{out}}$ from a fixed thin-shell or biconical geometry using outflow radius, velocity, gas density or column density, and covering fraction so the conversion is reproducible. Compare $v_{\mathrm{out}}$ with halo escape speed ($v_{\mathrm{esc}}$) derived from a stellar-mass-scaled NFW halo model. Use CGM column densities ($N(\mathrm{H\,I})$) and line ratios such as $\mathrm{O\,VI}/\mathrm{Mg\,II}$ at matched impact parameters as a secondary check for recycling. The core observable is whether each phase is kinematically bound or unbound, not whether an outflow is simply detected.

**Prior evidence and constraints.** Quasar observations show AGN feedback acting on molecular gas reservoirs at high redshift, but those detections do not determine whether gas escapes the halo. Stellar-feedback work on baryon deficiency in low-mass galaxies provides a boundary condition for non-AGN removal channels that must not be confused with AGN-driven escape. Simulated feedback constraints show that gas removal, recycling, and observable baryon fractions depend on halo scale.

**Control plan.** Assemble AGN hosts and inactive controls matched in stellar mass, halo mass, redshift, inclination, star-formation rate, and merger stage. Use MUSE and MaNGA for ionized-gas kinematics, ALMA CO and [C II] for cold gas, JWST/NIRSpec for $z > 2$ outflow tracers, and CGM absorption where available to follow recycling. Apply a shared escape-speed estimator, fixed aperture definitions, and the same outflow-mass conversion assumptions to every phase. When possible, compare inner-galaxy outflow tracers with background-sightline CGM tracers in the same halo so that escape and return are not inferred from one dataset alone. The primary path is a matched multi-phase sample; the fallback path is a phase-limited analysis that still uses the same escape-speed model across all objects.

**Decision criterion.** Permanent removal is favored if the median inferred escaping fraction is $> 50\%$ across the matched active sample and the CGM at large impact parameters ($R > 100\ \text{kpc}$) shows elevated metal column densities without comparable returning or infalling components. Recycling-limited regulation is favored if $> 70\%$ of the outflowing mass has $v_{\mathrm{out}} < v_{\mathrm{esc}}$ and background sightlines reveal returning or infalling gas kinematics that are consistent with the host rotation. If the escaping and bound fractions overlap strongly after matching, the safer conclusion is that the data do not distinguish escape from recycling.

**Limitations and wording guardrails.** Projection effects, phase conversion, uncertain halo potentials, incomplete time baselines, and sample censoring can dominate the escaped-fraction error budget. The proposal should therefore be framed as a bound on fate, not as a direct census of all expelled mass.

## P2 — Environmental dependence of radio-jet coupling efficiency in galaxy gas

**Hypothesis / objective.** The fraction of radio-jet mechanical power deposited into the multiphase interstellar or circumgalactic medium varies systematically with ambient gas density and host environment.

**Primary observables.** Cavity enthalpy ($4PV$, where $P$ is pressure and $V$ is volume from Chandra X-ray imaging) divided by a buoyancy or sound-crossing age $t_{\mathrm{age}}$ to define cavity power $P_{\mathrm{cav}} = 4PV / t_{\mathrm{age}}$, shock temperature jumps, disturbed line widths and velocity offsets of molecular (CO) and ionized ([O III]) gas, and jet power ($P_{\mathrm{jet}}$) estimated from radio luminosity and spectral aging. The mechanical coupling efficiency parameter is defined as $\eta = P_{\mathrm{cav}} / P_{\mathrm{jet}}$. A useful secondary observable is whether the same host shows both a hot-gas cavity and cold-gas disturbance, because that helps separate coupling from simple jet presence.

**Prior evidence and constraints.** Young radio galaxies show AGN-driven outflows and provide an empirical route to estimating feedback efficiency. Local M51-scale NOEMA data show how jet or AGN activity can affect molecular ISM structure at high spatial resolution, motivating a broader population test. Galaxy-group feedback studies identify the group regime as a strong testbed for coupling between radio activity and hot gas.

**Control plan.** Use VLA, LOFAR, and MeerKAT for jet morphology and radio spectral ages; Chandra for cavities, shocks, and hot gas; MUSE and MaNGA for ionized-gas shock diagnostics; ALMA CO for molecular disturbances; and group catalogues for environment. Match on jet age, jet power, stellar mass, halo mass, gas fraction, and local density, then compare field, group, and cluster subsamples with the same selection cuts. Separate sources by host inclination and compact-versus-extended radio morphology so that projection does not masquerade as coupling. The primary path is a cavity-plus-jet sample with X-ray coverage; the fallback path is a radio-plus-optical/CO sample that still tests whether disturbed gas tracks environment.

**Decision criterion.** A robust environmental dependence is present if the coupling efficiency parameter $\eta$ is significantly higher in group or cluster cores compared to matched field environments by a factor of $\ge 2$, with a statistical significance of $\ge 3\sigma$ after controlling for jet morphology, radio-power calibration, and aperture effects. If the difference is $< 1\sigma$ or non-systematic, treat the result as no evidence for environment dependence under these controls.

**Limitations and wording guardrails.** Radio-to-jet-power conversions are uncertain; cavity detectability, viewing angle, and phase mixing can also blur the coupling signal. The analysis should be read as a test of coupling efficiency, not as a direct measure of total feedback energy.

## P3 — Locating the transition from stellar-feedback to AGN-feedback regulation

**Hypothesis / objective.** There is a mass regime where stellar-feedback momentum and energy budgets no longer explain gas loss or quiescence, and AGN-linked observables become necessary to reproduce the observed quenched fraction and gas depletion pattern. The transition mass should be inferred from the data, not fixed in advance.

**Primary observables.** Gas fraction ($f_{\mathrm{gas}} = M_{\mathrm{gas}}/M_*$), H I and CO depletion times ($t_{\mathrm{dep}} = M_{\mathrm{gas}}/\mathrm{SFR}$), quenched fraction ($f_{\mathrm{q}}$), star-formation efficiency, halo-gas X-ray luminosity, central velocity dispersion, and AGN incidence as a function of stellar mass. The key output is the mass at which these observables change slope or break from the low-mass trend. Compare the same observables in the field, in groups, and in clusters so that environment is not absorbed into the mass trend.

**Prior evidence and constraints.** Low-mass systems are more vulnerable to stellar feedback and gas escape, while massive systems are more likely to retain hot gas and host long-lived central heating. Existing SDSS pilot work on optical AGN and sSFR offset is useful as a denominator baseline, but it does not by itself identify the transition mass. The goal here is to locate the point where the data stop being explainable by stellar feedback alone.

**Control plan.** Assemble a stellar-mass-binned sample with matched redshift, inclination, dust, morphology, local density, and emission-line quality. In each bin, compare AGN hosts, non-AGN controls, and quiescent galaxies using the same SFR estimator and the same gas-mass calibration. Use two denominators: one matched on stellar mass alone and one matched on stellar mass plus local environment so that the transition mass is not biased by overdensity. Fit the break mass with change-point methods or segmented regression, then repeat under alternate SFR tracers and gas tracers to test stability. The primary path is a full binned analysis; the fallback path is a narrower mass range around the expected break that still has enough objects for a comparison.

**Decision criterion.** The transition is supported if the low-mass bins can be described without an AGN term, but a higher-mass break appears where the quenched fraction and depletion times change in a way that cannot be reproduced by stellar feedback alone. Operationally, treat the transition as robust only if a break in at least two observables lands within the same mass range to within $\pm 0.3$ dex and the result persists after environment matching. If no stable break is found, the safer conclusion is that regulation changes smoothly with mass rather than at a single threshold.

**Limitations and wording guardrails.** Stellar mass and halo mass are correlated but not identical, so the break can shift depending on which one is used. Selection effects, incomplete gas measurements, and AGN detectability can also move the apparent transition. This proposal should therefore be described as a search for a regime boundary, not as a fixed universal threshold.

## Cross-program logic

P0 establishes the denominator. P1 asks whether outflows escape or recycle. P2 asks whether jets couple differently in dense environments. P3 asks where the mass scale changes from stellar-feedback-dominated regulation to AGN-linked regulation. Together, the four programmes separate association from mechanism and mechanism from regime.

The common failure mode across all four is the same: a signal can disappear once the denominator is tightened, the tracer is changed, or the host environment is matched. That is why each proposal includes a fallback path, a control plan, and a decision criterion that can return a negative result without becoming ambiguous.

## Implementation notes

- Keep the same SFR estimator within each comparison class.
- Match on mass, redshift, inclination, dust, and local density before interpreting offsets.
- Treat gas-phase observables as phase-specific, not interchangeable.
- Prefer a negative control whenever the active sample is selected by a detection threshold.
- If the result depends on one tracer only, label it provisional rather than general.
