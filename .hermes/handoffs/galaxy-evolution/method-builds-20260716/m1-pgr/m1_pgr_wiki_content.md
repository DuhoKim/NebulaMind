# Galaxy Evolution — A Packet-Gated Wiki (Method 1: PGR)

**Method note.** This wiki was assembled by Packet-Gated Paper-to-wiki Reconciliation (PGR). Every claim below is backed by a *packet*: a specific finding traced to a specific paper (or set of papers) with a trust judgment carried over from DR-verified review packets. Claims that multiple independent papers support are marked **established**; claims where the source packets record genuine, unresolved disagreement are recorded as **debates** — no winner is chosen. The source base is a set of DR-verified reviews of galaxy evolution (Kennicutt & Evans 2012; Madau & Dickinson 2014; Somerville & Davé 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019; Péroux & Howk 2020; Tumlinson, Peeples & Werk 2017) together with topical packets on the mass–metallicity relation, chemical evolution, and gas depletion. Nothing here is asserted beyond what those packets support; temporal and methodological boundaries recorded in the packets (IMF choice, tracer, aperture, redshift, calibration, resolution) are retained as caveats.

## 1. Star-Formation Laws

The relationship between gas content and the rate of star formation is one of the most heavily tested empirical scalings in galaxy evolution, but the source packets are emphatic that it is not a single scale-free law. Disk-averaged measurements spanning normal spirals and starbursts yield a **super-linear global Schmidt–Kennicutt relation with an index near 1.4** (Kennicutt 1998), a result reproduced as the standard subgrid anchor across the modeling and structural reviews (Somerville & Davé 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017; Kennicutt & Evans 2012). Yet when the same gas is resolved on sub-kiloparsec scales in nearby disks, the star-formation-rate surface density correlates closely with **CO-traced molecular gas and is largely uncorrelated with atomic hydrogen** (Bigiel 2008; Leroy 2008). In that molecule-dominated regime the relation is closer to linear, with a narrow **molecular depletion time of order 1–2 Gyr** (Leroy 2008; Bigiel 2008). Atomic hydrogen, by contrast, **saturates near 9–10 solar masses per square parsec**, and star-formation efficiency drops sharply in the HI-dominated outer disks (Bigiel 2008; Leroy 2008).

At the dense end, integrated infrared luminosity tracks **dense-gas (HCN) luminosity approximately linearly** across galaxies, a relation that extends down to massive Milky Way clumps (Gao & Solomon 2004; Wu 2005; Lada 2012). Within individual nearby clouds, star formation is **inefficient relative to the free-fall time** and is concentrated toward high-extinction, high-column-density material (Evans 2009; Lada 2010; Heiderman 2010). Crucially, the CO-to-H2 conversion factor that underlies most molecular-gas masses is **environment-dependent**, varying with metallicity, radiation field, temperature, and dynamics (Kennicutt & Evans 2012; Bolatto 2013; Sandstrom 2013), and the otherwise tight kpc-scale molecular relation **develops large scatter at individual cloud and star-forming-region scales** (Schruba 2011; Leroy 2021).

Several tensions remain open. Whether high-redshift disks and extreme starbursts occupy **two offset star-formation sequences or a single continuous law** is unresolved, because gas masses, excitation, and conversion factors covary (Genzel 2010; Daddi 2010; Tacconi 2018). Relatedly, the field disputes whether star formation obeys **one global power law or scale- and phase-dependent relations** (Kennicutt 1998; Bigiel 2008), and whether the CO-to-H2 factor is better described by a **two-value (Galactic/starburst) prescription or continuous environmental variation** (Bolatto 2013; Sandstrom 2013). At a physical level, the observed SFR–H2 correlation leaves open whether **molecular hydrogen is causally required for collapse or merely co-locates with shielded cold gas** (Bigiel 2008; Glover 2010), whether **star-formation efficiency per free-fall time is constant or environment-dependent** (Krumholz 2012; Evans 2009), and whether outer-disk suppression reflects a **chemical-shielding failure or a gravitational/dynamical threshold** (Bigiel 2010; Krumholz 2012).

## 2. The Star-Forming Main Sequence and Quenching

Most star-forming galaxies occupy a **relatively narrow sequence in the SFR–stellar-mass plane** (scatter of order 0.3 dex) out to at least z~2.5, making sustained gas supply and regulation a central benchmark for models (Noeske 2007; Wuyts 2011; Somerville & Davé 2015). The broader galaxy population is **bimodal**, separating into a star-forming blue cloud and a quiescent red sequence (Baldry 2004; Bell 2004). Reproducing the massive, quenched end requires **preventive suppression of cooling and star formation in massive halos**, over and above the stellar feedback that regulates low-mass systems (Bower 2006; Croton 2006). That stellar feedback itself — driving galactic outflows — is required to **lower the efficiency of low-mass galaxies and transport metals** out of star-forming disks (Dekel & Silk 1986; Somerville & Davé 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017). Consistent with this regulation picture, the global molecular depletion time is **not a single constant** but correlates with specific SFR and offset from the main sequence (Saintonge 2017; Tacconi 2018).

The *mechanism* of quenching is contested. The source packets record disagreement over whether massive-galaxy quenching is set by **virial heating and black-hole (radio-mode) feedback or by overlapping environmental and gas-supply processes** (Bower 2006; Croton 2006; Somerville & Davé 2015), and over whether quenching in general proceeds by **reduced supply/exhaustion, suppressed star-formation efficiency at fixed gas, or rapid gas removal** (Martig 2009; Ellison 2020; Saintonge 2017). Resolved data further fuel a debate over whether scatter about the main sequence is driven primarily by **reservoir mass or by molecular star-formation efficiency** (Saintonge 2017; Tacconi 2018; Ellison 2020), and whether **stellar-metallicity offsets between passive and star-forming galaxies pin down a strangulation/starvation channel** (Peng 2015; Trussler 2020).

## 3. Gas Content, Depletion, and Accretion

The cold-gas contents of galaxies evolve strongly with cosmic time. **Molecular-gas fractions rise steeply from the local Universe to cosmic noon**, while the molecular depletion time evolves more weakly (Tacconi 2018; Genzel 2015). At low redshift, atomic and molecular fractions **vary systematically with stellar mass, color/specific SFR, and surface density**, but with different dependences and scatter for the two phases (Catinella 2018; Saintonge 2017). A key inference is that **the observed growth of stellar mass cannot be supplied by the declining HI+H2 reservoirs alone**, so continued net accretion of gas onto galaxies is required (Walter 2020). Gas accretion itself separates usefully into **rapidly cooling cold/filamentary and shock-heated hot-halo pathways**, with the balance set by halo mass, redshift, and environment (Kereš 2005; Birnboim & Dekel 2003).

Open questions include whether **cold filamentary accretion or hot-mode shocked cooling dominates galaxy growth** across the relevant mass range (Kereš 2005; Birnboim & Dekel 2003; Somerville & Davé 2015), the **absolute normalization and peak redshift of the cosmic molecular-gas density** (Decarli 2020; Riechers 2019), and whether outflowing gas is **permanently ejected or substantially recycled through galactic fountains and the circumgalactic medium** (Marinacci 2011; Werk 2014; Naab & Ostriker 2017).

## 4. Cosmic Star-Formation History

The synthesis of ultraviolet and infrared surveys shows that the **cosmic star-formation-rate density rises from the present to a broad maximum near z~2 and declines toward higher redshift** (Madau & Dickinson 2014). Integrating that history under standard recycling assumptions implies that **roughly half of today's stellar mass had formed by z~1.3 and about a quarter above z~2** (Madau & Dickinson 2014), with **less than about one percent of present-day stellar mass formed at z>6** (Madau & Dickinson 2014). A central established result is that **dust-obscured, infrared-emitting star formation is a major — and near cosmic noon dominant — component of the total budget** (Madau & Dickinson 2014). The **cosmic molecular-gas density traces this rise-and-fall**, peaking near cosmic noon and declining afterward (Péroux & Howk 2020; Decarli 2020). The observed **stellar-mass-density history is broadly consistent with the time integral of the SFR density** once stellar mass return is included, though a residual offset persists (Madau & Dickinson 2014). All of these conversions depend on the assumed stellar initial mass function: **Salpeter- and Chabrier-like IMFs imply materially different SFR normalizations, mass-return fractions, and yields** (Chabrier 2003; Salpeter 1955).

Debates carried in the packet concern whether the **decline of the SFR density at z>6 is largely physical or amplified by galaxies below detection limits** (Bouwens 2011; Bouwens 2012), whether a **universal IMF is justified for cross-epoch comparison** (Chabrier 2003; Salpeter 1955), the **origin of the residual mismatch between the integrated SFR density and the measured stellar-mass density** (Dickinson 2003; Madau & Dickinson 2014), and whether **locally calibrated UV-slope (IRX–β) dust corrections apply at high redshift** (Meurer 1999; Calzetti 2000).

## 5. The Mass–Metallicity Relation

The correlation between galaxy stellar mass and gas-phase metallicity is one of the most robust scaling relations in the field. The **local gas-phase mass–metallicity relation rises with stellar mass and flattens above a turnover near ~10^10.5 solar masses**, with scatter of order 0.1 dex (Tremonti 2004; Zahid 2014; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019). A parallel and distinct relation holds for **stellar metallicity, which rises with stellar mass as an integrated-history record** rather than a snapshot of the present ISM (Gallazzi 2005). The relation evolves: the **gas-phase MZR normalization declines toward higher redshift out to at least z~3.3** (Erb 2006; Maiolino 2008; Sanders 2021). Interpreting these data is complicated by calibration: **strong-line metallicity methods disagree by up to ~0.7 dex in absolute O/H**, even where they recover consistent relative trends (Kewley & Ellison 2008; Curti 2020). Two further established results connect the MZR to the baryon cycle: **effective yields in low-mass galaxies fall below closed-box expectations**, pointing to inflow and/or preferential metal loss (Edmunds 1990; Peeples & Shankar 2011), and **most local non-interacting star-forming disks show negative radial abundance gradients** (Sánchez 2014; Belfiore 2017). Environment leaves only a small imprint: at fixed mass, **overdense environments enhance metallicity by no more than ~0.04–0.05 dex** (Cooper 2008; Ellison 2009).

The packets record several live debates. The most prominent is the **reality, strength, and redshift-invariance of a "fundamental metallicity relation" (FMR)** linking mass, SFR, and metallicity — a real universal surface versus a residual manufactured by selection, aperture, and calibration effects (Mannucci 2010; Lara-López 2010; Salim 2014). Others concern **what physically sets the MZR** — mass-dependent outflow, inflow dilution, star-formation efficiency, or retention (Zahid 2014; Lilly 2013; Finlator & Davé 2008); **which abundance scale can be transferred to high redshift** given harder ionizing spectra (Kewley & Ellison 2008; Curti 2020; Nakajima 2023); and **whether the low-mass MZR slope evolves** into the JWST regime (Sanders 2021; Curti 2024; Langeroodi 2023).

## 6. Chemical Evolution and Enrichment

Chemical evolution links the star-formation history of a galaxy to its abundance patterns through stellar nucleosynthesis and gas flows. The **closed-box model is a useful null hypothesis but fails the G-dwarf problem**, requiring gas infall (and finite stellar delays) to match observed metallicity distributions (Tinsley 1980; Chiappini 1997; Lilly 2013). The enrichment is time-structured: **core-collapse supernovae deliver prompt alpha-element enrichment while Type Ia supernovae add iron-peak elements over a broad delay-time distribution**, making abundance ratios sensitive to the star-formation timeline (Nomoto 2013; Maoz & Brandt 2012). Consequently, **[alpha/Fe] encodes the duration and timing of star formation**, with high values indicating rapid early formation before delayed iron enrichment (Thomas 2005; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019). **Low- and intermediate-mass stars return carbon, nitrogen, and s-process elements through the AGB phase on mass-dependent delays** (Karakas & Lattanzio 2014; Henry 2000), and **N/O shows a low-metallicity plateau that rises at higher O/H**, consistent with primary nitrogen plus delayed secondary production (Vincenzo 2016; Henry 2000). At the population level, **gas-regulator models cast metallicity as a balance among inflow, star formation, recycling, yields, and outflow** rather than a monotonic clock (Lilly 2013; Bouché 2010; Davé 2012). Within galaxies, **stellar radial migration can flatten and broaden stellar abundance gradients**, so a star's present radius need not equal its birth radius (Sellwood & Binney 2002).

Open debates include the **progenitor channels and delay-time distribution of Type Ia supernovae** (the near-t^-1 DTD shape is more secure than the channel mixture; Maoz & Brandt 2012; Maoz & Nelemans 2014), the **dominant driver of [alpha/Fe]** — pure time-delay versus IMF variation or metallicity-dependent yields (Thomas 2005; Nomoto 2013; Johnson & Weinberg 2020), the **primary origin of nitrogen** between massive and intermediate-mass stars (Henry 2000; Izotov & Thuan 1999), and the **evolution of radial abundance gradients at z~1–3**, where beam smearing and inflow can mimic flat or inverted profiles (Stott 2014; Wuyts 2016; Sánchez 2014).

## 7. The Circumgalactic Medium

The circumgalactic medium (CGM) — **gas outside a galaxy's stellar disk and ISM but within its virial radius — is multiphase in ionization and complex in kinematics** (Tumlinson, Peeples & Werk 2017; Werk 2013; Tripp 2008). Absorption surveys establish that **cool, HI-bearing gas is common around both star-forming and passive low-redshift galaxies** (Thom 2012; Tumlinson 2013), and photoionization modeling implies a **large cool-CGM baryonic reservoir of order 10^10–10^11 solar masses around L* galaxies** (Werk 2014; Prochaska 2017). The warm/hot tracer O VI is **strong around star-forming L* galaxies and much weaker around passive ones** (Tumlinson 2011; Oppenheimer 2016). A key budget result is that **a substantial fraction of the metals produced by a galaxy resides outside its stellar disk** (Peeples 2014; Tumlinson 2011). The spatial distribution of absorption is not random: **low-ionization gas depends on galaxy orientation, with enhanced incidence along the minor axis** consistent with biconical outflows in statistical samples (Bordoloi 2011; Kacprzak 2012). Taken together, the CGM functions as a **reservoir and transfer medium for accretion, feedback, and recycling that regulates the galaxy's gas supply** (Kereš 2005; Fraternali & Binney 2008).

The physics of this gas is actively debated: whether **small cool clouds are rapidly shredded by the hot halo or survive and regenerate** through cooling, conduction suppression, or magnetic fields (Armillotta 2017; McCourt 2012); whether **O VI arises in collisionally ionized ~10^5.5 K gas or in photoionized/non-equilibrium interfaces** (Oppenheimer 2016; Tripp 2008); **whether the detected cool and warm phases close the CGM baryon budget or large hot/low-density reservoirs remain unmeasured** (Werk 2014; Anderson & Bregman 2010); and whether **metallicity reliably tags gas origin** (fresh accretion versus recycled outflow), given mixing and ionization overlap (Lehner 2013; Prochaska 2017; Ford 2014).

## 8. The Cosmic Baryon and Metal Census

At the largest scale, the source packets support a broadly closed — though contested in detail — accounting of baryons and metals. **More than 90 percent of cosmic baryons remain gaseous rather than locked in stars at the present epoch** (Planck Collaboration 2016; Driver 2018; Péroux & Howk 2020), and **Big-Bang-nucleosynthesis and CMB estimates give concordant total baryon-density normalizations** (Cooke 2018; Planck Collaboration 2016). The neutral-atomic-gas density **evolves only modestly from z~5 to the present** (Péroux 2003; Bird 2017). For metals, the 2020 census finds that **at z>2.5 the metal mass in cold gas can account for nearly all metals expected from the integrated star-formation history, while at z<1 stars are the largest identified reservoir** (Becker 2019; Driver 2018; Péroux & Howk 2020) — with little evidence for a severe global missing-metals problem. The **dust-to-metals ratio is not universal and decreases toward low metallicity** (Aller 2014; Berg 2016).

Reconciliation across packets nonetheless leaves debates: whether the **metal census is genuinely closed or a residual population hides in poorly measured warm-hot phases** (Bouché 2007; Péroux & Howk 2020); how much **ionization corrections in CGM and Lyman-limit gas bias inferred total masses** under an uncertain radiation field (Chen 2017; Wotta 2019); and whether **simulations that match global totals nonetheless assign baryons and metals to different temperatures, ionization states, or halo radii** than observed (Bird 2015; Churchill 2015).

## 9. Galaxy-Formation Models and Feedback

Modern galaxy-formation theory rests on a shared architecture: **both semi-analytic models and cosmological hydrodynamic simulations begin from hierarchical dark-matter assembly and add baryonic cooling, star formation, feedback, enrichment, and structural evolution** (White & Rees 1978; White & Frenk 1991; Somerville & Davé 2015). Comparison with data drives two robust inferences. The **shallow abundance of low-mass galaxies relative to the halo mass function requires strongly mass-dependent suppression of baryon conversion** in shallow potential wells (Moster 2013; Behroozi 2013), and **abundance matching places peak stellar-conversion efficiency near Milky-Way-scale (~10^12 solar-mass) halos**, with lower efficiency on both sides (Moster 2013; Behroozi 2013). Because **cosmological volumes cannot resolve the multiphase ISM, both model families rely on subgrid star-formation and feedback prescriptions** (Springel & Hernquist 2003; Somerville & Davé 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017). At the resolution of these boxes, **depositing supernova thermal energy directly leads to numerical overcooling** unless mitigated (Dalla Vecchia & Schaye 2008; Kim & Ostriker 2015), and the underlying **supernova-remnant physics — free expansion, Sedov–Taylor, radiative shell formation, and a final momentum-conserving phase — sets the mechanical budget** that subgrid models must capture (Cioffi 1988; Kim & Ostriker 2015).

The reviews foreground several methodological debates. Whether **agreement between semi-analytic and hydrodynamic models reflects physical convergence or shared calibration and analogous subgrid assumptions** is unresolved (Somerville & Davé 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017). Different **wind implementations — kinetic kicks, thermal deposition, or effective equations of state — reproduce the required regulation through different paths** (Dalla Vecchia & Schaye 2008; Schaye 2015), raising the broader **subgrid-degeneracy concern that numerical convergence is not physical validation** (Somerville & Davé 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017). The **dominant stellar-feedback channel** — supernovae versus radiation, photoionization, and winds that condition the birth environment — is contested (Agertz 2013; Walch & Naab 2015), as is the **dynamical importance of cosmic-ray and magnetic nonthermal pressure** (Boulares & Cox 1990; Salem & Bryan 2014).

## 10. Structural and Morphological Evolution

The structural transformation of galaxies is driven by multiple, overlapping channels. **Mergers, dissipative gas dynamics, and internal disk instabilities can all build bulges, compactness, and spheroids** (Barnes 1988; Ceverino 2010; Somerville & Davé 2015). Disk sizes have a physical baseline in **halo angular momentum and the retention or loss of baryons** (Fall & Efstathiou 1980; Mo 1998). Observationally, **star-forming and quiescent populations follow different size–mass evolution out to z~3** (van der Wel 2014). The history of the field also carries a cautionary established result: **early cooling-only simulations overproduced central stellar mass and lost too much baryonic angular momentum**, which motivated both stronger regulation and improved numerical treatment (Katz & Gunn 1991; Navarro & Steinmetz 1997; Naab & Ostriker 2017).

The relative weights of these channels remain debated. The packets record disagreement over whether **major and minor mergers or internal secular and violent-disk-instability processes dominate spheroid and compact-galaxy growth** (Barnes 1988; Ceverino 2010; Somerville & Davé 2015), and over whether **galaxy sizes chiefly reflect halo spin with approximate angular-momentum conservation or are set by feedback-selective loss, torques, and accretion history** (Fall & Efstathiou 1980; Mo 1998).
