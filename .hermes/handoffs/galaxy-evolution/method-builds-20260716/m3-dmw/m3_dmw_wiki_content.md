# Galaxy Evolution: A Debate Map

**Method: DMW (Debate-Map-to-Wiki).** This wiki is built around the field's *open questions*. Rather than presenting galaxy evolution as a settled narrative, each section below is framed by a central tension and separates what is genuinely **settled** (consensus across the source reviews) from what remains **contested** (competing positions and the evidence each side musters). The organizing principle is the map of scientific debate itself.

Everything here is grounded in a fixed base of eight peer-reviewed review syntheses and their DR-verified primary sources: Kennicutt & Evans (2012) on star-formation laws; Madau & Dickinson (2014) on cosmic star-formation history; Somerville & Davé (2015) on galaxy-formation models; Naab & Ostriker (2017) on the theory of feedback and the interstellar medium; Maiolino & Mannucci (2019) on chemical evolution and the mass–metallicity relation; Tacconi, Genzel & Sternberg (2020) on molecular gas; Péroux & Howk (2020) on the cosmic baryon and metal cycle; and Tumlinson, Peeples & Werk (2017) on the circumgalactic medium.

---

## The debate map at a glance

The live tensions in galaxy evolution cluster into a small number of recurring fights. Nearly every one turns on the same underlying difficulty: **an observed correlation or a calibrated model can be reproduced by more than one physical mechanism.** The recurring fault lines are:

1. **The slope of the star-formation law** — is it linear in molecular gas or super-linear in total gas, and is the correlation with H2 causal?
2. **What limits star-formation efficiency** — turbulence and gravity, or early stellar feedback?
3. **Disks versus starbursts** — two distinct star-formation sequences, or one continuum blurred by the CO-to-H2 conversion factor?
4. **What quenches galaxies** — gas removal and starvation, or suppression of efficiency in retained gas?
5. **The high-redshift edge of cosmic star formation** — physical decline or observational incompleteness?
6. **Whether models validate physics** — does matching tuned observables identify the true mechanism, or hide it?
7. **The reality and universality of the fundamental metallicity relation.**
8. **Where the missing baryons and metals reside**, and what the circumgalactic medium is actually doing.

Each section develops one of these clusters.

## Section 1 — How do galaxies convert gas into stars? The slope of the star-formation law

**Central tension:** There is a tight empirical relation between gas and star formation, but its functional form depends on which gas phase and what spatial scale you measure — and the field disagrees on which version is fundamental.

### Settled
Disk-averaged total (atomic plus molecular) gas and star-formation-rate surface densities follow a super-linear global Schmidt–Kennicutt law with a power-law index near 1.4 (Kennicutt 1998; Kennicutt & Evans 2012). On sub-kiloparsec scales in nearby normal disks, the star-formation-rate surface density correlates far more tightly with CO-traced molecular gas than with atomic hydrogen, and the resolved molecular relation is close to linear (Bigiel et al. 2008; Leroy et al. 2008). Atomic hydrogen saturates near 9–10 solar masses per square parsec; above this the medium becomes molecular-dominated and below it the star-formation efficiency per unit atomic gas falls sharply in outer disks and dwarfs (Bigiel et al. 2008; Leroy et al. 2008). Star formation is globally inefficient: the efficiency per free-fall time is only 1–2 percent, and nearby molecular clouds convert gas to stars far more slowly than their free-fall times would allow (Krumholz, Dekel & McKee 2012; Evans et al. 2009). Recent star formation concentrates in the highest-column, dense gas, and the global star-formation rate tracks dense-gas mass traced by HCN nearly linearly across normal galaxies and starbursts (Gao & Solomon 2004; Lada et al. 2010).

### Contested
**Linear versus super-linear.** Whether the star-formation law is intrinsically super-linear in total gas or strictly linear once the molecular phase is isolated is unresolved. Whole-galaxy total-gas data give a compact N≈1.4 law (Kennicutt 1998); resolved molecular maps give a near-linear slope, with the global super-linearity potentially an artifact of averaging over the atomic-to-molecular transition (Bigiel et al. 2008; Leroy et al. 2008). **Is H2 causal?** The observed correlation motivates treating molecular gas as a precursor requirement (Bigiel et al. 2008), but chemistry and turbulence models argue that molecule formation and gravitational collapse merely share the same shielded, cold, high-density environments — correlation need not be causation (Glover et al. 2010; Krumholz, Dekel & McKee 2012). A decisive test requires low-metallicity regimes where the molecule-formation and dynamical timescales separate. **Constant versus variable efficiency per free-fall time.** Unified models adopt a low, roughly constant efficiency per free-fall time (Krumholz, Dekel & McKee 2012), while cloud and galaxy measurements suggest it varies with density, tracer, or regime (Lada et al. 2010; Evans et al. 2009). **What suppresses star formation in outer disks** — failure to form shielded molecular gas (Bigiel et al. 2008) or large-scale disk stability and low midplane pressure (Krumholz, Dekel & McKee 2012) — is unresolved because column density, metallicity, stellar density, and pressure all covary.

## Section 2 — What regulates a galaxy's gas supply and its position on the main sequence?

**Central tension:** Star-forming galaxies form a remarkably tight sequence in the star-formation-rate–stellar-mass plane, which implies self-regulated, accretion-fed growth. But the depletion time that links gas to star formation is not a universal constant, and how it evolves is disputed.

### Settled
Most star-forming galaxies occupy a narrow "main sequence" with roughly 0.3 dex scatter that persists to at least z≈4, implying that secular, accretion-regulated growth — not mergers — builds the bulk of stellar mass (Speagle et al. 2014; Noeske et al. 2007). Galaxies at cosmic noon (z≈1–3) were far more gas-rich than local galaxies, with molecular gas fractions routinely 30–50 percent versus 5–10 percent today (Tacconi et al. 2010; Tacconi et al. 2018). The molecular gas depletion time is not a single universal number: it scales systematically with specific star-formation rate and offset from the main sequence, dropping toward ~100 Myr in starbursts (Saintonge et al. 2017; Genzel et al. 2015). Gas-regulator or "bathtub" models, in which star formation tracks a quasi-equilibrium set by inflow, require continuous large-scale cold accretion to sustain star formation over cosmic time (Bouché et al. 2010; Lilly et al. 2013). The volume-averaged molecular gas density peaks near z≈1.5–3 and declines afterward, tracking the cosmic star-formation-rate density, while the atomic hydrogen density stays comparatively flat (Decarli et al. 2019; Péroux & Howk 2020).

### Contested
**Two sequences or one continuum.** Whether disks and starbursts occupy two physically distinct depletion-time sequences (Daddi et al. 2010; Genzel et al. 2010) or a single relation varying smoothly with main-sequence offset (Saintonge et al. 2017; Tacconi et al. 2018) is unresolved, and the tension is bound up with the CO-to-H2 conversion factor: apparent bimodality can arise from covarying conversion factors and dynamical state. **The conversion factor itself.** The magnitude and drivers of variation in the CO-to-H2 factor are contested, some analyses applying near-uniform values while resolved dust and dynamical calibrations find continuous variation with metallicity, radiation field, and surface density (Bolatto et al. 2013; Accurso et al. 2017). **Depletion-time evolution.** At fixed main-sequence offset, whether the molecular depletion time is constant or declines mildly toward higher redshift, and whether it scales with (1+z) or with the Hubble time, are statistically indistinguishable within current scatter (Tacconi et al. 2018; Genzel et al. 2015). **Uniqueness of bathtub models.** Gas-regulator scaling matches are necessary but not sufficient to validate feedback physics, because the mass-loading factor and pristine-inflow fraction are strongly degenerate (Lilly et al. 2013; Bouché et al. 2010).

## Section 3 — Why do galaxies stop forming stars? The quenching debate

**Central tension:** Galaxies split into a star-forming sequence and a quiescent population, but the field does not agree on whether quenching removes the fuel or suppresses the efficiency of retained fuel — nor on what dominates at low versus high mass.

### Settled
The galaxy population is bimodal, split into a star-forming blue cloud and a quiescent red sequence, and the quiescent population has been growing since z≈2 (Baldry et al. 2004; Bell et al. 2004). Reproducing the shallow faint end and the sharp bright-end cutoff of the galaxy stellar mass function requires *two* regulation regimes: strong stellar-feedback-driven suppression in low-mass halos, and additional preventive suppression of cooling in massive halos (Somerville & Davé 2015; Bower et al. 2006). Stellar mass conversion efficiency peaks near Milky-Way-scale halos (~10^12 solar masses) and falls toward both lower and higher halo mass (Moster et al. 2013; Behroozi et al. 2013).

### Contested
**Removal versus suppression.** Whether quenching is driven primarily by exhaustion or removal of cold gas, or by kinematic and morphological suppression of star-formation efficiency in retained gas reservoirs, remains open, with different samples favoring different interpretations (Martig et al. 2009; Ellison et al. 2020). **The high-mass mechanism.** Successful population models invoke "radio-mode" black-hole feedback to quench cooling flows in massive halos (Bower et al. 2006; Croton et al. 2006), but this is a phenomenological galaxy-scale prescription, and virial shock heating and environmental channels can produce overlapping effects, so the bright-end cutoff does not uniquely identify one cause (Somerville & Davé 2015). **Evidence from stellar metallicities.** Offsets between the stellar metallicities of passive and star-forming galaxies have been read as favoring a prolonged reduced-inflow (starvation) phase over instantaneous gas removal (Peng, Maiolino & Cochrane 2015; Trussler et al. 2020), but the relative contribution of outflows and the dominant timescale remain debated.

## Section 4 — When did the Universe form its stars? The cosmic star-formation history

**Central tension:** The broad rise and fall of cosmic star formation is one of the field's most robust results — but its high-redshift edge, its dust budget, and the initial mass function underlying every conversion remain contested.

### Settled
A multi-survey ultraviolet-plus-infrared synthesis rises from the present to a broad maximum near z≈2 and declines toward higher redshift; the standard analytic fit peaks near z≈1.9 (Madau & Dickinson 2014). Under this history, roughly half of today's stellar mass had formed by z≈1.3 and about one quarter above z≈2 (Madau & Dickinson 2014). Dust-obscured star formation traced in the infrared is a major — and near cosmic noon, dominant — component of the total budget (Gruppioni et al. 2013; Madau & Dickinson 2014). The observed stellar-mass-density history is broadly consistent with the time integral of the preceding star-formation-rate density once stellar mass return is included, though a residual normalization offset remains (Madau & Dickinson 2014).

### Contested
**Physical decline versus incompleteness.** The observed drop in star formation toward z>6 may be largely physical or substantially amplified by galaxies below detection limits, because the faint-end turnover, completeness, and cosmic variance were not directly measured (Bouwens et al. 2012; Madau & Dickinson 2014). **Faint-end extrapolation.** With an ultraviolet luminosity-function faint-end slope near or steeper than −2 at z≈7, the integrated star-formation and ionizing-photon budget is extremely sensitive to the imposed faint limit (Bouwens et al. 2011; Madau & Dickinson 2014). **High-redshift dust corrections.** Whether locally calibrated ultraviolet-slope attenuation relations apply at high redshift, or whether dust geometry and composition differ, was not settled given inadequate far-infrared constraints for ordinary z>4 galaxies (Meurer et al. 1999; Madau & Dickinson 2014). **The reionization photon budget.** Whether galaxies supplied enough ionizing photons to reionize the Universe depends on an ionizing escape fraction that cannot be measured directly at z>6 and on the uncertain faint-galaxy population (Robertson et al. 2013; Madau & Dickinson 2014). **IMF universality.** Whether a universal Salpeter/Chabrier-like initial mass function is valid across epochs, or whether it is environment- or metallicity-dependent, is unresolved and propagates into every light-to-mass, light-to-star-formation, yield, and ionizing conversion (Chabrier 2003; Madau & Dickinson 2014).

## Section 5 — Do our galaxy-formation models tell us the physics? Subgrid feedback and the validation problem

**Central tension:** Semi-analytic and hydrodynamic simulations reproduce the observed galaxy population, but they do so through subgrid prescriptions calibrated to those same observables — so the deepest debate is whether matching the data validates the underlying physics at all.

### Settled
Both semi-analytic models and cosmological hydrodynamic simulations begin from hierarchical dark-matter assembly and add baryonic cooling, star formation, feedback, enrichment, and structural evolution (Somerville & Davé 2015; White & Frenk 1991). Because cosmological volumes cannot resolve the multiphase interstellar medium or individual star-forming clouds, both families require subgrid star-formation and feedback prescriptions (Somerville & Davé 2015). The shallow abundance of low-mass galaxies relative to the halo mass function robustly requires strongly mass-dependent suppression of baryon conversion in shallow potential wells, even though the exact mechanism is not uniquely inferred (Dekel & Silk 1986; Somerville & Davé 2015). A supernova remnant transfers energy and momentum through resolved evolutionary stages with a canonical explosion energy of order 10^51 erg, and depositing that energy at insufficient resolution lets it radiate away before doing mechanical work — numerical overcooling (Kim & Ostriker 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017). Distinct feedback implementations can reproduce overlapping galaxy-population statistics after calibration (Schaye et al. 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017).

### Contested
**Calibration is not validation.** Whether reproducing observed stellar masses and star-formation rates demonstrates physically adequate regulation, or whether tuned agreement hides incorrect mechanisms and wrong secondary predictions, is the field's central methodological dispute — different subgrid recipes match the same targets while implying divergent physics (Somerville & Davé 2015; Naab & Ostriker 2017). **The dominant feedback channel.** Supernovae may provide the main long-lived mechanical budget (Kim & Ostriker 2015), or pre-supernova radiation, photoionization, and winds may condition the birth environment enough to control the coupling (Agertz et al. 2013; Naab & Ostriker 2017). **Thermal versus kinetic injection.** Stochastic high-temperature thermal deposition and kinetic/momentum injection can both be calibrated to work, and both depend on resolution (Dalla Vecchia & Schaye 2008; Naab & Ostriker 2017). **Ejective versus recycled winds.** Whether strong winds permanently lower baryon-conversion efficiency (Dalla Vecchia & Schaye 2008) or much of the outflowing gas recycles through galactic fountains and the circumgalactic medium (Marinacci et al. 2011; Naab & Ostriker 2017) is unresolved; no universal mass-loading factor existed. **Cold versus hot accretion.** Whether filamentary cold flows dominate galaxy growth or stable virial shocks and cooling atmospheres control massive systems depends on mode definitions, mixing, and tracer observability (Kereš et al. 2005; Birnboim & Dekel 2003). **Cosmic rays and magnetic fields** may provide dynamically important nonthermal pressure that drives cool winds and supports disks, but uncertain transport limits their inferred impact (Salem & Bryan 2014; Naab & Ostriker 2017).

## Section 6 — How do galaxies build their structure? Mergers, instabilities, and sizes

**Central tension:** Galaxies transform their sizes and morphologies dramatically over cosmic time, but whether that transformation is driven mainly by mergers or by internal, secular processes — and what sets galaxy sizes — is unresolved.

### Settled
Mergers, dissipative gas dynamics, and internal disk instabilities can all alter bulges, compactness, and morphology; morphology alone does not identify which channel operated (Somerville & Davé 2015; Barnes 1992). Star-forming and quiescent galaxies follow different size–mass evolution, with compact quiescent galaxies already in place at z≈2 (van der Wel et al. 2014; Barro et al. 2013). Gas-rich disks at cosmic noon are turbulent, with intrinsic velocity dispersions of 40–80 km/s and rotation-to-dispersion ratios of 2–6, sustaining marginal Toomre stability, and their central regions are strongly baryon-dominated (Förster Schreiber et al. 2009; Genzel et al. 2017). Violent disk instabilities in gas-rich disks drive radial gas inflow, central compaction, and bulge growth (Zolotov et al. 2015; Bournaud et al. 2007). Halo angular momentum and baryonic retention provide a baseline connection between halo properties and disk sizes (Mo, Mao & White 1998; Somerville & Davé 2015).

### Contested
**Mergers versus internal evolution.** Whether major and minor mergers dominate spheroid and compact-galaxy growth, or whether violent disk instability and secular evolution contribute substantially, is unresolved because observed morphology and simulations cannot uniquely reconstruct assembly histories (Somerville & Davé 2015; Ceverino et al. 2010). **What sets sizes.** Whether galaxy sizes largely reflect halo spin with approximate angular-momentum retention, or feedback-selective angular-momentum loss, torques, and accretion history, is unresolved because direct baryon-angular-momentum accounting across gas phases is incomplete (Somerville & Davé 2015). **Clump survival.** Whether giant star-forming clumps in high-redshift disks survive feedback long enough to migrate inward and build bulges, or are rapidly disrupted, depends sensitively on how aggressively subgrid stellar feedback is implemented (Bournaud et al. 2007; Genzel et al. 2011). **The origin of disk turbulence** — sustained by stellar feedback or by gravitational instability and accretion — is unresolved because both inject sufficient energy analytically (Krumholz & Burkert 2010; Elmegreen et al. 2009).

## Section 7 — What sets a galaxy's metal content? The mass–metallicity and fundamental relations

**Central tension:** Galaxy metallicity correlates tightly with stellar mass, but the absolute abundance scale is uncertain at the 0.7-dex level, and whether a deeper, redshift-invariant mass–star-formation–metallicity relation exists is genuinely disputed.

### Settled
Local star-forming galaxies show a positive gas-phase mass–metallicity relation that steepens below about 10^10.5 solar masses, flattens above it, and has roughly 0.1 dex scatter (Tremonti et al. 2004; Curti et al. 2020). Mean stellar metallicity also increases with stellar mass, forming an integrated-history relation distinct from the gas-phase one (Gallazzi et al. 2005). At fixed stellar mass, gas-phase metallicity is associated with star-formation rate in local samples, so a mass–star-formation-rate–metallicity surface reduces residual scatter (Mannucci et al. 2010; Curti et al. 2020). Consistently calibrated samples show lower gas-phase abundance at fixed stellar mass toward higher redshift (Sanders et al. 2021; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019), and JWST/NIRSpec has extended the relation to low-mass galaxies at z≈4–10, which lie below local samples (Nakajima et al. 2023; Curti et al. 2024). Low-mass systems have lower effective yields than closed-box expectations, consistent with inflow and/or preferential metal loss (Maiolino & Mannucci 2019; Peeples & Shankar 2011).

### Contested
**The abundance scale.** Electron-temperature, recombination-line, and photoionization-model calibrations can shift inferred oxygen abundances by as much as 0.7 dex and do not share one settled absolute scale, so mass–metallicity normalizations are comparable only when the underlying scale is matched (Kewley & Ellison 2008; Curti et al. 2017). **FMR reality and invariance.** The existence, strength, and redshift invariance of a universal mass–star-formation-rate–metallicity relation are disputed: some matched analyses find no evolution to z≈3.3 while other data and simulations indicate substantial offsets and an evolving secondary star-formation dependence at higher redshift (Sanders et al. 2021; Curti et al. 2024; Garcia et al. 2024). **The physical driver of the MZR.** The relation alone cannot separate mass-dependent outflow, inflow dilution, star-formation efficiency, recycling, and enriched reaccretion — several regulator and simulation parameter combinations reproduce it (Finlator & Davé 2008; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019). **The high-redshift scale.** Which metallicity scale transfers to early galaxies is unresolved, as local calibrations disagree in normalization and applying some of them to early galaxies can bias inferred oxygen abundance (Kewley & Ellison 2008; Hirschmann et al. 2023).

## Section 8 — How do galaxies assemble their chemical elements? Chemical evolution

**Central tension:** Chemical enrichment is well described as a balance of inflow, star formation, and outflow rather than a closed box, but the nucleosynthetic sources, timescales, and gradient evolution behind the elements remain contested — sharply so in the early Universe.

### Settled
Cosmic chemical enrichment is a long-term balance among pristine inflow, star-formation efficiency, and metal-loaded outflows; classical closed-box models fail because they cannot reproduce the metallicity distribution of long-lived low-mass stars (the G-dwarf problem) (Tinsley 1980; Lilly et al. 2013). The Type Ia supernova delay-time distribution follows a declining power law of about t^−1.07 over billions of years, making Type Ia supernovae the dominant source of delayed iron (Maoz, Mannucci & Brandt 2012; Maoz, Mannucci & Nelemans 2014). Low- and intermediate-mass stars on the asymptotic giant branch are major sources of carbon, secondary nitrogen, and slow-neutron-capture elements (Karakas & Lattanzio 2014). The nitrogen-to-oxygen ratio shows a low-abundance plateau (primary nitrogen) rising steeply at high abundance (secondary nitrogen) (Vincenzo et al. 2016; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019). Local star-forming disks generally show negative radial gas-phase gradients consistent with inside-out assembly, with a characteristic slope of order −0.1 dex per effective radius (Belfiore et al. 2017; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019). Stellar radial migration can flatten and blur stellar metallicity gradients over time (Sellwood & Binney 2002).

### Contested
**[α/Fe] as a star-formation clock.** Whether the [α/Fe] ratio cleanly measures star-formation timescale is debated, since a top-heavy initial mass function or metallicity-dependent yields could raise it independently of the star-formation history (Tinsley 1980; Maiolino & Mannucci 2019). **Type Ia progenitors.** The single-degenerate channel readily produces prompt explosions but struggles with the late-time delay tail, while the double-degenerate merger channel yields the tail but has difficulty producing enough prompt events (Maoz, Mannucci & Nelemans 2014). **Early-Universe nitrogen.** The JWST detection of a supersolar N/O ratio in GN-z11 at z≈10.6 is in tension with standard delayed nitrogen enrichment and has prompted competing explanations invoking Wolf–Rayet winds, tidal disruption events, or dense stellar clusters (Cameron et al. 2023; Vincenzo et al. 2016). **High-redshift gradients.** Whether high-redshift disks have steeper, flat, or inverted radial gradients is debated, as observed z≈1–3 gradients are diverse and may reflect genuine cold-flow accretion and feedback or observational beam-smearing (Cresci et al. 2010; Stott et al. 2013).

## Section 9 — Where are the baryons and metals? The cosmic census and baryon cycle

**Central tension:** The total baryon and metal budgets of the Universe are fixed, but locating them across gas phases is not — and whether the census closes depends on poorly measured warm-hot gas.

### Settled
Big-Bang nucleosynthesis and cosmic-microwave-background estimates give concordant total baryon densities, but this fixes the total, not where baryons later reside (Cooke et al. 2018; Péroux & Howk 2020). More than 90 percent of present-day baryons remain gaseous rather than locked in stars (Driver et al. 2018; Péroux & Howk 2020). The comoving atomic-gas density evolves only modestly from z≈5 to today, while the molecular-gas density rises toward cosmic noon and falls afterward, tracking the star-formation-rate density (Péroux & Howk 2020; Decarli et al. 2019). At z>2.5 the metal mass inferred in cold gas can account for nearly all metals expected from the integrated star-formation history, while at z<1 stars are the largest identified metal reservoir (Becker et al. 2019; Driver et al. 2018). The dust-to-metals ratio is not universal and decreases toward low metallicity (Péroux & Howk 2020; De Cia et al. 2016).

### Contested
**Metal-census closure.** Whether adding stars, cold gas, dust, and ionized gas brings the cosmic metal census near closure (Bouché et al. 2007; Péroux & Howk 2020) or whether residual metals hide in poorly measured warm-hot phases so closure is not secure (Péroux & Howk 2020) turns on uncertain yields and ionization corrections. **Atomic-gas evolution versus selection.** The weak evolution of the atomic-gas density could be biased by absorber column thresholds, the rarity of high-column systems, and dust-obscured sightlines missing from optical quasar samples (Péroux & Howk 2020). **The molecular census.** Whether CO luminosity functions directly yield the molecular-gas density, or whether uncertain excitation and metallicity-dependent conversion undermine those masses, is unresolved (Decarli et al. 2019; Bolatto et al. 2013). **Metal yields and the IMF.** Different initial mass functions, solar abundance scales, and nucleosynthetic yields shift the expected metal budget that every closure calculation is compared against (Asplund et al. 2009; Péroux & Howk 2020).

## Section 10 — What is the circumgalactic medium doing? The halo gas reservoir

**Central tension:** A galaxy's halo holds a large, multiphase gas reservoir that mediates accretion, feedback, and recycling — but its total mass, ionization state, origin, and flow direction are all contested, and a single ion column rarely constrains any of them uniquely.

### Settled
The circumgalactic medium — gas outside the stellar disk and inside the virial radius — is multiphase in ionization and complex in kinematics, so line-of-sight velocity does not uniquely map to radius (Tumlinson et al. 2017; Werk et al. 2013). Cool, neutral-hydrogen-bearing gas is common around both star-forming and passive low-redshift L-star galaxies (Tumlinson et al. 2017; Thom et al. 2012). Strong O VI is widespread around star-forming L-star galaxies and much weaker around passive ones (Tumlinson et al. 2011; Oppenheimer et al. 2016). Photoionization modeling implies a large cool-circumgalactic reservoir of order 10^10–10^11 solar masses around L-star galaxies (Werk et al. 2014; Prochaska et al. 2017), and roughly 75–80 percent of the metals produced by L-star galaxies reside outside their stellar disks (Peeples et al. 2014; Tumlinson et al. 2011). Low-ion absorption depends on galaxy orientation, with enhanced minor-axis incidence consistent with biconical outflows (Bordoloi et al. 2011; Kacprzak et al. 2012).

### Contested
**CGM baryon closure.** Whether large cool and warm reservoirs account for the halo baryon budget (Werk et al. 2014; Prochaska et al. 2017), or whether a hot, low-density phase remains unmeasured so closure is not demonstrated (Anderson & Bregman 2010; Tumlinson et al. 2017), depends decisively on radial extrapolation, metallicity, and X-ray density-profile assumptions. **The origin of O VI.** Whether O VI arises in collisionally ionized gas near 10^5.3–10^5.6 K (Tumlinson et al. 2011) or in photoionized or non-equilibrium interfaces and cooling flows (Faerman, Sternberg & McKee 2017; Tripp et al. 2008) is unresolved because one O VI column does not fix density, temperature, path length, or mass. **Cool-cloud survival.** Whether cool clouds are rapidly shredded by hydrodynamic instabilities in the hot halo, or survive and grow aided by cooling, suppressed conduction, or magnetic fields, is unresolved because cloud sizes and interfaces are unresolved observationally and numerically (Armillotta et al. 2017; McCourt et al. 2012). **Accretion versus recycled winds.** Whether low-metallicity cool gas marks fresh accretion and high-metallicity gas marks recycled outflow — supported by a claimed bimodal metallicity distribution (Lehner, Howk & Tripp 2013; Prochaska et al. 2017) — or whether mixing and ionization blend the origins so metallicity alone cannot reconstruct a trajectory (Ford et al. 2014; Christensen et al. 2016), is unresolved. **Inflow versus outflow identification** at the level of individual absorbers is degenerate because projection and recycling blur the geometric clues (Bouché et al. 2013; Kacprzak et al. 2012). **Feedback-model dependence.** Different wind loading, velocity, and recycling prescriptions yield different circumgalactic columns and phase masses, and galaxy-integrated calibration does not uniquely constrain halo-scale gas histories (Muratov et al. 2015; Tumlinson et al. 2017).

## Recurring meta-tension

Across all ten sections one structural problem recurs: **a measured scaling relation, ion column, or calibrated simulation is consistent with multiple physical mechanisms.** The star-formation law, the mass–metallicity relation, the depletion-time sequence, the circumgalactic ion inventory, and the galaxy stellar mass function are each reproduced by more than one physical story. This is why so much of galaxy evolution remains, in the strict sense, *contested* — not for lack of data, but because the available observables under-determine the physics. Progress in each debate depends on breaking a specific degeneracy: separating molecule formation from collapse at low metallicity, pinning the absolute abundance scale, measuring wind mass-loading directly, resolving the temperature-resolved baryon census, and running same-initial-condition multi-code simulations that expose where calibration ends and prediction begins.
