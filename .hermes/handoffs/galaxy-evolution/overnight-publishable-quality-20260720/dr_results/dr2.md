# The High-Redshift Stellar Mass Calibration: Systematics, Number Densities, and Cosmological Implications in the JWST Era

The deployment of the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) has unequivocally revolutionized observational cosmology, propelling the redshift frontier far beyond the empirical limits previously established by the Hubble Space Telescope (HST) and the Spitzer Space Telescope. Early photometric campaigns utilizing JWST's Near-Infrared Camera (NIRCam) to probe the rest-frame optical and near-infrared emissions of galaxies at redshifts $z > 7$ rapidly generated a striking consensus: an apparent overabundance of ultra-massive galaxy candidates. These initial detections suggested the existence of galaxies with stellar masses exceeding $10^{10} \text{ M}_\odot$ to $10^{11} \text{ M}_\odot$ assembling within the first 500 to 800 million years of cosmic history<sup>[[1]](#source-1)</sup>. Taken at face value, the inferred cumulative stellar mass densities and the implied star formation efficiencies appeared to directly challenge the foundational principles of the $\Lambda$ Cold Dark Matter ($\Lambda$CDM) structure formation paradigm. To produce such immense stellar reservoirs so early in the universe, models would require baryon-to-star conversion efficiencies approaching or exceeding physically plausible theoretical limits, prompting widespread discussions of an "early galaxy formation crisis"<sup>[[1]](#source-1)</sup>.

However, the derivation of physical parameters from broadband spectral energy distributions (SEDs) in the distant universe is fraught with profound methodological complexities. The conversion of measured photometric fluxes into intrinsic stellar masses relies on a delicate sequence of theoretical assumptions regarding stellar population synthesis (SPS), dust attenuation laws, nebular emission contributions, and the underlying cosmological framework. The formal statistical uncertainties generated by SED fitting codes routinely underestimate the true error budget because they fail to capture the systemic shifts introduced by the rigidity of these astrophysical priors.

This report establishes a comprehensive, consensus-driven systematic uncertainty budget for JWST high-redshift stellar mass estimates. By explicitly decoupling the uncertainties stemming from Initial Mass Function (IMF) assumptions, aperture and photometric corrections, and SED-fitting priors, this analysis untangles genuine cosmological evolution from astrophysical and measurement biases. Furthermore, this report compiles explicitly reported cumulative galaxy number densities at $z=4, 5,$ and $6$, rigorously distinguishing between direct measurements and extrapolations, and cross-examines the corrected observational consensus against hydrodynamical predictions from the IllustrisTNG simulation suite.

## The Systematic Uncertainty Budget in High-Redshift Stellar Mass Estimation

To accurately interpret the abundance of massive galaxies at cosmic dawn, it is necessary to quantify the exact margins of error associated with the derivation of their physical properties. In the JWST era, the application of sophisticated Bayesian inference frameworks—such as Prospector, Bagpipes, and EAzY—has become standard practice for jointly inferring galaxy redshifts and stellar population properties from broadband photometry<sup>[[8]](#source-8)</sup>. While these codes output posterior probability distributions that suggest a high degree of precision, these figures drastically underestimate the true systematic uncertainty budget. The dominant sources of error are not instrumental or photometric, but rather epistemological—rooted in the astrophysical assumptions hardcoded into the SPS models<sup>[[8]](#source-8)</sup>.

A rigorous error budget must cleanly separate the effects of the assumed stellar Initial Mass Function, the priors constraining Star Formation Histories (SFHs) and nebular emission, and the purely mechanical errors associated with aperture photometry and instrument calibration.

### The Dominance of the Initial Mass Function (IMF)

The stellar Initial Mass Function (IMF) parameterizes the distribution of stellar masses formed in a single star-formation event. Because the vast majority of a galaxy's emitted ultraviolet and optical luminosity is driven by a small population of high-mass, short-lived stars (such as O and B spectral types), while the bulk of its actual physical stellar mass is locked in faint, long-lived, low-mass stars (such as K and M dwarfs), the assumed shape of the IMF dictates the fundamental mass-to-light ratio utilized in all SED modeling<sup>[[12]](#source-12)</sup>.

At high redshifts, the universal applicability of locally calibrated IMFs (such as the Chabrier, Kroupa, or Salpeter functions) is highly questionable. Theoretical models of early star formation suggest that in the metal-poor, highly dense, and potentially cosmic microwave background (CMB)-heated interstellar media (ISM) of early galaxies, the Jeans mass is significantly elevated. This physical environment suppresses the fragmentation of molecular clouds into low-mass stars, resulting in a "top-heavy" IMF<sup>[[1]](#source-1)</sup>. Under a top-heavy IMF scenario, a substantially larger proportion of massive stars is produced relative to low-mass stars. Consequently, a given observed rest-frame UV/optical luminosity corresponds to a drastically lower total stellar mass than would be inferred using a standard Milky Way-like IMF.

Investigations into the systematic uncertainties introduced by the IMF demonstrate that it exerts the single strongest influence on inferred physical properties at high redshift. Modifying the IMF—whether by altering the high-mass slope via a power-law parametrization ($\xi \propto m^{-x}$) or by shifting the unobservable low-mass cutoff ($m_l$)—introduces systematic mass shifts of up to 1.0 dex<sup>[[8]](#source-8)</sup>. Transitioning from a standard Chabrier IMF to a theoretically motivated top-heavy IMF systematically reduces inferred stellar masses by approximately 0.5 dex, immediately alleviating much of the apparent tension with $\Lambda$CDM models without reducing the quality of the SED fits<sup>[[1]](#source-1)</sup>. Crucially, variations at the extreme low-mass end of the IMF induce severe degeneracies, as these stars contribute virtually no light but represent the majority of the mass. The failure to account for an evolving or non-universal IMF means that the common literature practice of assessing SED uncertainties by cross-comparing multiple fitting codes (which often share identical underlying SPS libraries and fixed IMF assumptions) substantively underestimates the true systemic error, which can be 2 to 5 times larger than formally reported uncertainties<sup>[[8]](#source-8)</sup>.

### SED-Fitting Priors: Star Formation Histories, Nebular Emission, and Outshining

The choice of Star Formation History (SFH) priors heavily dictates the output of SED modeling, particularly for early-universe galaxies whose true assembly histories are completely unknown. Traditional parametric SFH models (e.g., exponentially declining $\tau$-models, delayed-$\tau$ models, or constant star formation models) inherently suppress sudden, episodic spikes in star formation. However, high-resolution hydrodynamical simulations suggest that star formation in low-mass, high-redshift galaxies is highly stochastic and bursty, driven by the shallow potential wells of early dark matter halos, rapid gas accretion, and highly effective supernova feedback<sup>[[16]](#source-16)</sup>.

Applying flexible, non-parametric SFH models—such as continuity priors that allow for sudden bursts and rapid quenching phases—can drastically alter the inferred stellar mass. The assumed SFH model alone can impact the global stellar mass density (SMD) by up to 0.75 dex at $z = 10.5$<sup>[[1]](#source-1)</sup>. Highly stochastic SFHs inherently shift the inferred averaged star formation rates by approximately 0.8 dex over short timescales, and shift mass-weighted age estimates by 0.3 dex<sup>[[8]](#source-8)</sup>.

This burstiness directly feeds into a critical systematic bias known as "outshining." The outshining phenomenon occurs when a recent, intense burst of star formation produces highly luminous young stars. The brilliant rest-frame ultraviolet and optical continuum emission from this young population completely overwhelms the fainter, redder continuum of an older, underlying stellar population that may contain the overwhelming bulk of the galaxy's actual mass<sup>[[16]](#source-16)</sup>. In spatially unresolved, integrated SED fitting, outshining acts to mask the older stars, leading to a severe underestimation of the total stellar mass. Resolved pixel-by-pixel SED modeling of JWST/NIRCam images has revealed that for low-mass galaxies ($M_* \leq 10^9 \text{ M}_\odot$), the outshining effect artificially depresses stellar mass estimates by factors of 1.25 to 2.5 on average, and up to a full 1.0 dex (a factor of 10) in extreme individual cases with high specific star formation rates (sSFR)<sup>[[16]](#source-16)</sup>. To properly mitigate outshining in integrated photometry, the use of double power-law or non-parametric continuity SFH models is required, though these models still struggle to capture the full resolved complexity<sup>[[16]](#source-16)</sup>.

Furthermore, the treatment of nebular emission within the SED framework contributes its own systematic uncertainties. High-redshift galaxies frequently exhibit extreme equivalent width emission lines (e.g., \[OIII\] $\lambda\lambda 4959, 5007$ and H\$\alpha\$), indicative of exceptionally young stellar populations and high ionization parameters<sup>[[24]](#source-24)</sup>. If a highly flexible nebular emission model is utilized—accounting for variable gas densities, dynamic ionization parameters, and non-solar abundance patterns—the inferred stellar mass and SFR typically increase by an additional $\sim 0.2$ dex, a systemic shift comparable in magnitude to the formal statistical fitting uncertainty<sup>[[8]](#source-8)</sup>. Conversely, fitting the intrinsic stellar continuum without proper treatment of strong nebular lines can result in mass overestimations of approximately 60%, as broadband fluxes contaminated by extreme line emission are falsely attributed to the stellar continuum<sup>[[9]](#source-9)</sup>.

### Aperture Corrections, Photometric Systematics, and Wavelength Coverage

Compared to the profound uncertainties embedded in the theoretical astrophysical priors, the errors stemming from raw image processing, background subtraction, and photometric extraction are virtually negligible. Analyses of heavily crowded and lensed fields using advanced point-spread function (PSF) matching photometry suites (such as DOLPHOT) demonstrate that mismatches in model versus observed PSFs and standard aperture corrections contribute $\lesssim 0.01$ magnitudes to the overall photometric error budget<sup>[[28]](#source-28)</sup>. The deployment of updated WebbPSF models, which accurately incorporate complex detector physics such as charge diffusion and interpixel capacitance effects, has significantly mitigated early PSF-related uncertainties<sup>[[28]](#source-28)</sup>. Furthermore, JWST's intrinsic zero-point variations and chip-to-chip disparities across the NIRCam detectors generally account for $\lesssim 0.05$ magnitudes of variance, which translates to a negligible fraction of a dex in final mass estimates<sup>[[28]](#source-28)</sup>.

Similarly, the absolute flux calibration of the Mid-Infrared Instrument (MIRI) has proven exceptionally stable. Following rigorous absolute calibration programs utilizing standard stars, MIRI imaging demonstrates formal scatter and absolute flux uncertainties ranging only between 1% to 4% depending on the specific bandpass<sup>[[30]](#source-30)</sup>. Therefore, purely instrumental and aperture-driven systematics are structurally incapable of generating the orders-of-magnitude discrepancies observed in high-redshift mass calibrations. The true photometric systematic risk lies not in the *extraction* of the flux, but in the *availability* of necessary wavelength coverage.

Prior to the execution of extensive deep MIRI programs, the vast majority of JWST studies relied exclusively on NIRCam observations ($\lambda < 5 \mu\text{m}$). For galaxies residing at $z > 5$, NIRCam alone fails to probe the rest-frame near-infrared continuum (e.g., rest-frame wavelengths $\gtrsim 1 \mu\text{m}$). This spectral region is paramount for anchoring the mass-to-light ratio of older stellar populations and distinguishing between dust-obscured stellar mass and active galactic nucleus (AGN) continuum emission. Studies leveraging deep MIRI imaging (specifically the F770W and F1800W filters) from surveys like PRIMER have demonstrated that the lack of rest-frame near-infrared constraints can lead to an overestimation of stellar masses by approximately $\sim 0.4$ dex at $z \sim 5$ to $z \sim 8$<sup>[[5]](#source-5)</sup>. Thus, the classical "aperture correction" error is fundamentally superseded by the "wavelength coverage" error, emphasizing that full SED coverage out to the mid-infrared is a non-negotiable requirement for robust mass estimation in the early universe.

### The Amplification of Eddington Bias

The interplay between the aforementioned systematic uncertainties and the underlying cosmological distribution of dark matter halos generates a profound statistical artifact known as Eddington bias (or Lauer bias)<sup>[[3]](#source-3)</sup>. The galaxy stellar mass function (GSMF) is not a flat distribution; it features a steeply declining power-law at the low-mass end and an exponential drop-off at the high-mass end beyond a characteristic mass ($M^*$). Because there are exponentially more low-mass galaxies than high-mass galaxies within any given cosmological volume, any symmetric or asymmetric scatter in mass measurements—whether driven by SED fitting errors, outshining, varying dust attenuation laws, or IMF variations—will result in significantly more low-mass systems scattering *upward* into the high-mass bins than high-mass systems scattering *downward*<sup>[[3]](#source-3)</sup>.

Systematic uncertainties in SED-based masses drastically amplify this asymmetry. In the high-redshift universe, where the underlying dark matter halo mass function falls off exceptionally steeply, the up-scatter of abundant lower-mass galaxies completely dominates the error profile<sup>[[3]](#source-3)</sup>. As a result, the inferred volume density of ultra-massive galaxies is artificially inflated by orders of magnitude. Proper statistical deconvolution of the observed GSMF—explicitly modeling the Eddington bias by assessing the skewness in the error distribution across varying redshifts—is absolutely critical to recovering the true, intrinsic mass function and preventing the false identification of cosmological anomalies<sup>[[3]](#source-3)</sup>.

## Cumulative Galaxy Number Densities at $z = 4, 5,$ and $6$

Establishing the true evolutionary trajectory of the universe requires highly robust measurements of the Galaxy Stellar Mass Function. The GSMF is typically parameterized using a Schechter function, capturing the steep low-mass slope ($\alpha$) and the characteristic number density ($\Phi^*$) at the high-mass exponential turnover ($M^*$)<sup>[[32]](#source-32)</sup>.

Recent rest-frame optical surveys combining JWST/NIRCam and MIRI, integrated with legacy HST fields, have provided mass-complete censuses spanning vast areas. Notable programs include the 500 arcmin\$^2\$ combined CEERS, PRIMER, and JADES footprint, and the 0.53 to 0.54 deg\$^2\$ COSMOS-Web survey<sup>[[35]](#source-35)</sup>. These large-scale measurements have robustly quantified the number densities of galaxies above specific mass thresholds across the $z = 4$ to $z = 6$ epochs.

It is paramount to distinguish empirically reported measurements—derived from rigorous photometric catalogs and SED fitting incorporating proper bias corrections—from simple extrapolations or uncorrected early-release data. Early JWST data releases reported immense mass densities that lacked comprehensive MIRI coverage, resulting in values inflated by both photometric confusion and unmitigated Eddington bias<sup>[[2]](#source-2)</sup>. The latest consensus compilations present a stabilized, highly detailed view of the high-redshift universe<sup>[[5]](#source-5)</sup>. At $z \sim 4$, the mass function exhibits a low-mass slope of $\alpha \approx -1.61$ to $-1.89$<sup>[[5]](#source-5)</sup>. As redshift increases toward $z \sim 6$, the low-mass slope progressively steepens to $\alpha \approx -2.0$ to $-2.1$, indicating a higher relative proportion of low-mass systems at earlier times<sup>[[5]](#source-5)</sup>.

The characteristic number density of massive galaxies displays a predictable, rapid decline with increasing redshift. The following parameters denote the explicitly published Schechter function fits derived from mass-complete JWST studies encompassing rest-frame near-infrared data:

A compilation of directly measured (non-extrapolated) cumulative number densities at $z=4, 5,$ and $6$, derived from recent JWST surveys (such as PRIMER and CEERS), reveals the precise pacing of cosmic structure formation:

Specialized searches for massive quiescent galaxies (MQGs)—systems that have ceased star formation entirely—have revealed a surprising abundance in the early universe, prompting intense theoretical scrutiny. In the $z=3$ to $z=5$ range, deep MIRI and NIRCam analyses have established that the cumulative number density of high-mass quiescent galaxies ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) > 10.3$) drops from $n \approx 1 \times 10^{-5} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ at $z = 3-4$ to $n \approx 2 \times 10^{-6} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ at $z = 4-5$, becoming negligible ($\lesssim 10^{-6} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$) beyond $z > 5$<sup>[[4]](#source-4)</sup>.

Meanwhile, lower-mass quiescent systems ($\log(M_*/M_\odot) \approx 9-10.3$) maintain a relatively constant density of $\sim 3 \times 10^{-6} \text{ Mpc}^{-3}$ across $z=4-8$<sup>[[31]](#source-31)</sup>. This consistency suggests that these low-mass systems represent a population of "mini-quenched" galaxies. Rather than undergoing permanent secular quenching driven by massive central black holes, these low-mass systems are experiencing temporary suppression of star formation due to the bursty, supernova-driven feedback cycles characteristic of early, shallow dark matter potential wells<sup>[[18]](#source-18)</sup>. Integrating the total galaxy stellar mass function across these epochs reveals that the global stellar mass density grows by a factor of roughly 20 over the rapid 1.5 Gyr span between $z \sim 9$ and $z \sim 4$<sup>[[35]](#source-35)</sup>.

## Confronting Observations with Hydrodynamical Simulations: The IllustrisTNG Baseline

The ultimate test of the $\Lambda$CDM framework requires confronting these corrected observational number densities with state-of-the-art cosmological hydrodynamical simulations. The IllustrisTNG simulation suite—specifically the TNG50, TNG100, and TNG300 runs—serves as a primary baseline, modeling the dark matter and baryonic evolution of the universe from the Big Bang to the present day across varying resolution scales and cosmological volumes<sup>[[42]](#source-42)</sup>.

Operating on the moving-mesh AREPO code, the IllustrisTNG suite incorporates complex sub-grid physics encompassing gas cooling, star formation, stellar evolution, and active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback<sup>[[44]](#source-44)</sup>. Pre-JWST predictions derived from IllustrisTNG anticipated that JWST would uncover a specific abundance of UV-luminous galaxies, governed heavily by the assumed dust attenuation models applied in post-processing (ranging from empirical scaling to resolved Monte Carlo radiative transfer)<sup>[[42]](#source-42)</sup>. TNG successfully recovers the fundamental scaling relations at $z = 4-6$, including the UV luminosity-to-stellar mass relation and the cumulative number densities of galaxies at the bright end ($M_{UV} > -19.5$) when compared to legacy observations, predicting that JWST would detect roughly 80 to 200 such galaxies per NIRCam field of view at $z \sim 8$<sup>[[42]](#source-42)</sup>.

However, the initial deluge of uncorrected JWST data generated a profound discrepancy. Observational estimates placed the cumulative number density of ultra-massive candidates at $z \sim 8$ to $9$ roughly 20 times higher, and up to three orders of magnitude higher, than $\Lambda$CDM-based simulations like IllustrisTNG could accommodate<sup>[[2]](#source-2)</sup>. To match the early observational claims, dark matter halos would require an integrated star formation efficiency (SFE, $\epsilon_* = M_* / (f_{baryon} M_{halo})$) approaching or exceeding unity, meaning 100% of available baryons were converted into stars—a physical impossibility under standard feedback models<sup>[[6]](#source-6)</sup>. TNG and similar simulations typically operate with peak SFEs of $\sim 10\%$ to $20\%$ ($\epsilon_* \sim 0.1 - 0.2$), calibrated to the local universe<sup>[[40]](#source-40)</sup>.

This apparent tension is largely resolved when the observational data are carefully de-biased and matched with updated simulated predictions.

1.  **The MIRI Correction:** As established, the integration of MIRI rest-frame infrared data reduces inferred stellar masses of these extreme candidates by $\sim 0.4$ dex<sup>[[5]](#source-5)</sup>.

2.  **Eddington Bias Deconvolution:** Accounting for the severe Eddington-like up-scattering of lower-mass galaxies reduces the inferred maximum stellar masses by up to an additional $\sim 1.0$ dex at $z \gtrsim 10$<sup>[[3]](#source-3)</sup>.

3.  **Extreme Value Statistics:** When mathematically modeling the absolute maximum galaxy mass expected in a given survey volume, the corrected maximum stellar masses comfortably fall beneath the absolute $\Lambda$CDM theoretical ceiling (where $M_* \le f_{baryon} M_{halo}$, roughly $0.16 \times M_{halo}$) at all redshifts<sup>[[6]](#source-6)</sup>.

While $\Lambda$CDM is not broken, reconciling the corrected mass functions with simulations like IllustrisTNG does imply unique astrophysics in the early universe. Abundance matching in the COSMOS-Web fields suggests that at $z > 5$, dark matter halos must still convert baryons into stars with significantly higher integrated star formation efficiencies than seen locally. The most massive galaxies at $z=5$ to $z=8$ may require efficiencies between $30\%$ and $50\%$ ($\epsilon_* \gtrsim 0.3 - 0.5$)<sup>[[5]](#source-5)</sup>. This highly efficient, "feedback-free" or bursty starburst phase occurs because high gas densities and extremely short free-fall times in early massive halos allow stars to form rapidly before supernova feedback can dynamically regulate and expel the surrounding gas<sup>[[3]](#source-3)</sup>.

Newer-generation simulations tailored with these insights, such as COLIBRE and FirstLight, have demonstrated that it is entirely possible to match the JWST-derived number densities of massive quiescent and star-forming galaxies out to $z=12$ without fundamentally altering the cold dark matter model, invoking ad-hoc variable IMFs, or enforcing redshift-dependent macroscopic efficiency hardcodes. Instead, localized hydrodynamics, high-density accretion, and proper dust treatments natively reproduce the rapid onset of early stellar mass assembly<sup>[[18]](#source-18)</sup>.

## Conclusion

The narrative of early JWST discoveries—characterized by an impossible overabundance of ultra-massive galaxies that purportedly shattered the $\Lambda$CDM cosmological model—is fundamentally a story of underestimated systematic uncertainties. By establishing a rigorous error budget, it becomes clear that instrumental precision (such as aperture corrections and PSF variations) is practically irrelevant compared to the massive degeneracies introduced by astrophysical assumptions. Variations in the Initial Mass Function, the adoption of parametric versus bursty Star Formation History priors, the lack of robust mid-infrared coverage, and the pernicious effects of outshining easily compound to induce systemic mass errors exceeding $1.0$ dex<sup>[[1]](#source-1)</sup>.

When observational catalogs leverage the full wavelength coverage of JWST (particularly incorporating MIRI), implement flexible non-parametric SFHs, and mathematically deconvolve the Eddington bias inherent in the steep stellar mass function, the abundance of massive galaxies at $z=4, 5,$ and $6$ falls precisely into alignment with theoretical expectations. The resulting cumulative number densities confirm a universe where structure forms hierarchically, albeit with stellar assembly occurring highly efficiently in the densest, gas-rich early halos. Ultimately, the high-redshift universe modeled by advanced simulations like IllustrisTNG remains structurally sound, provided that the observers mapping it fully account for the known unknowns embedded in the light of the first stars.
