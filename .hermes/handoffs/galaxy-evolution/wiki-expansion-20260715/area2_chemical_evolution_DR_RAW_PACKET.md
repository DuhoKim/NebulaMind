## Deep Research evidence map

Broad Non-AGN Galaxy Chemical Evolution: Enrichment History and Processes

The chemical evolution of galaxies represents the cumulative, macroscopic fossil record of microscopic nucleosynthetic processes, stellar life cycles, and the vast cosmological gas flows that dictate the baryonic assembly of the Universe. Unlike purely dynamical or morphological histories, the chemical composition of the interstellar medium (ISM) and the stellar populations within a galaxy provides an unbroken timeline of its star-formation history, the efficiency of its feedback mechanisms, and its interactions with the circumgalactic medium (CGM). This evidence map isolates the fundamental mechanisms of non-AGN chemical enrichment. It explicitly delineates gas-phase from stellar metallicities, maps the nucleosynthetic origins and delay times of key elemental abundance ratios, and synthesizes the evolutionary models that connect pristine accretion to metal-loaded galactic winds.

1. Established findings

The foundations of galactic chemical evolution rely on tracking the flow of baryons in and out of the ISM, combined with the delayed restitution of synthesized elements from distinct stellar mass regimes. The following established findings synthesize the prevailing observational evidence and theoretical consensus regarding this continuous cycle.

[CHEM-E01]

role: established

finding: Cosmic chemical enrichment is governed by a long-term regulatory balance between pristine gas inflow, star-formation efficiency, and metal-loaded outflows, universally necessitating gas-regulator (bathtub) models over classical closed-box approximations.

scope/boundary: Redshift z=0 to z∼3, global gas-phase and stellar metallicities, observational synthesis and analytic modeling.

confidence note: High; the classic closed-box model fails unequivocally to reproduce the observed stellar metallicity distribution functions, leading to the universally recognized "G-dwarf problem."

The foundational framework for understanding galactic chemical enrichment was originally posited through the "closed-box" model, which assumed a galaxy begins as a finite, isolated reservoir of pristine hydrogen and helium, continuously forming stars and enriching the remaining gas without any external mass exchange. Under the instantaneous recycling approximation (IRA)—which assumes massive stars die and return their nucleosynthetic yields to the ISM immediately—the closed-box model yields a clean, analytical prediction: the metallicity of the gas should scale logarithmically with the inverse of the gas fraction. However, when applied to the local Milky Way disk, this model critically overpredicts the number of long-lived, low-mass stars with low metallicities. This discrepancy, known as the classic "G-dwarf problem," mathematically breaks the closed-box assumption. To resolve the deficit of metal-poor stars, the system must either begin pre-enriched or undergo continuous infall of pristine cosmological gas over time, which dilutes the ISM and ensures that the bulk of star formation occurs at intermediate-to-high metallicities.   

Consequently, the field has universally adopted "gas-regulator" or "equilibrium" models. In these frameworks, the chemical evolution of a galaxy is dictated by an equilibrium state where the accretion of low-metallicity gas from the cosmic web is perfectly balanced by the consumption of gas via star formation and the expulsion of gas via supernova-driven, metal-loaded outflows. As mathematically formulated by Lilly et al. (2013), once a galaxy reaches this regulatory phase, its gas-phase metallicity is determined not by its total integrated history, but by the instantaneous effective yield (y

) modified by the mass-loading factor (η) of its outflows and the specific star-formation rate. This fundamental shift from isolated boxes to dynamic, continuous baryon cycling dictates that a galaxy's metallicity traces the depth of its gravitational potential well and the efficiency of its stellar feedback.

sources:Yates et al. (2022, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society) | DOI:10.1093/mnras/stac2205; ADS:2022MNRAS.516.1275Y | role=established | SDSS-IV MaNGA co-evolution of gas and stellar metallicities in spirals

In the study of chemical evolution, it is critical to distinguish between gas-phase metallicity, which provides an instantaneous snapshot of the current ISM enrichment, and stellar metallicity, which acts as a luminosity- or mass-weighted fossil record of all past enrichment epochs. Spatially resolved integral field unit (IFU) surveys, such as SDSS-IV MaNGA, have demonstrated that these two quantities do not evolve in a unified manner across all galaxy masses. By decomposing galaxy absorption-line spectra into single stellar populations of varying ages, researchers can archaeologically track the co-evolution of gas and stars.   

In lower-mass spiral galaxies, the stellar and gaseous metallicities increase together over cosmic time. These systems typically possess extended, continuous star-formation histories, allowing each successive generation of stars to form from an ISM that has been incrementally enriched by the previous generation. Conversely, high-mass spiral galaxies exhibit a profound decoupling. In these systems, the average stellar metallicity has not increased in step with the inferred gas metallicity. High-mass galaxies formed the vast majority of their stellar mass during a rapid, intense phase of star formation at cosmic noon (z∼1.5−2). Consequently, the global stellar metallicity is anchored to this early, less-enriched epoch. The present-day gas, however, continues to be enriched by late-time recycling and AGB mass loss, reaching a high-metallicity equilibrium that is disconnected from the bulk of the underlying stellar mass.

The primary diagnostic tool for measuring star-formation timescales in galaxies relies on the delayed enrichment of the iron-peak elements. While core-collapse supernovae (CCSN) deposit α-elements (such as O, Mg, and Si) into the ISM practically instantaneously following a starburst (within ∼3 to 30 Myr), Type Ia supernovae (SNe Ia) originate from thermonuclear explosions of white dwarfs in binary systems, introducing a significant temporal delay. Understanding the exact mathematical form of this delay—the delay-time distribution (DTD)—is essential for calibrating chemical evolution models.   

Extensive volumetric surveys and cluster integrations have firmly established that the cosmic DTD for SNe Ia follows a continuous, monotonically declining power law of the form t
−1.07±0.07

By integrating this DTD over a standard initial mass function (IMF), observations dictate a time-integrated production rate of approximately 0.00130±0.00015 SNe Ia per solar mass of formed stars. This fixed delayed iron injection rate is the absolute anchor for interpreting the [α/Fe] vs. [Fe/H] abundance ratio planes used in galactic archaeology.

[CHEM-E05]

role: established

finding: Nitrogen exhibits a dual enrichment origin: a primary component produced directly by massive stars yielding a flat [N/O] plateau at low metallicities, and a secondary component produced by intermediate-mass stars via the CNO cycle causing [N/O] to rise steeply at high metallicities.

scope/boundary: Local universe, SDSS and dwarf galaxy gas-phase strong-line diagnostics, 12+log(O/H) ranging from 7.0 to 9.0.

confidence note: High; the low-metallicity primary plateau at log(N/O)≈−1.6 is universally observed in unperturbed metal-poor environments and galaxies.

sources:Vincenzo et al. (2016, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society) | DOI:10.1093/mnras/stw532; ADS:2016MNRAS.458.3466V | role=established | Primary vs secondary nitrogen evolution and the low-metallicity N/O plateau

The interpretation of the nitrogen-to-oxygen (N/O) abundance ratio is a cornerstone diagnostic for chemical evolution, as it traces both nucleosynthetic origins and star-formation efficiency. Nucleosynthetically, elements are classified as "primary" if they can be synthesized directly from a pristine composition of hydrogen and helium (e.g., oxygen formed in massive stars). They are classified as "secondary" if their production requires pre-existing heavy elements to act as seed nuclei. Standard stellar evolution predicts that nitrogen is primarily a secondary element, formed as a byproduct of the CNO cycle operating in hydrogen-burning shells, where pre-existing carbon and oxygen act as catalysts. If nitrogen were purely secondary, the N/O ratio would scale linearly with the O/H ratio, meaning the relative abundance of nitrogen would plummet towards zero at extremely low metallicities.   

However, detailed gas-phase measurements of low-metallicity dwarf galaxies and large SDSS surveys unequivocally reveal a "plateau" in the N/O ratio at low oxygen abundances. Below an oxygen abundance of roughly 12+log(O/H)≈8.0, the N/O ratio remains flat at a constant value of log(N/O)≈−1.6 dex. This observational fact mandates that massive stars must produce a small but significant amount of primary nitrogen during their short lifespans. This primary production is generally theorized to result from rotational mixing in massive stars, which transports newly synthesized carbon from the helium-burning core into the hydrogen-burning shell, allowing the CNO cycle to operate without pre-existing seeds.   

Once a galaxy's metallicity surpasses the critical threshold, the delayed secondary nitrogen production from lower-mass AGB stars becomes dominant. At this point, the N/O ratio breaks from the plateau and rises steeply as a function of O/H. Consequently, the position of a galaxy on the N/O vs. O/H plane provides a powerful diagnostic of its star-formation efficiency: galaxies that form stars highly efficiently will rapidly increase their O/H via CCSN, stretching the primary plateau to higher metallicities before the delayed secondary N from AGB stars has time to enrich the ISM.

sources:Belfiore et al. (2017, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society) | DOI:10.1093/mnras/stx789; ADS:2017MNRAS.469..151B | role=established | Inside-out disk growth traced by MaNGA radial gas-phase metallicity gradients

The spatial distribution of heavy elements within a galaxy provides critical constraints on its mass assembly history and radial gas flows. With the advent of large-scale IFU surveys like SDSS-IV MaNGA, the CALIFA survey, and SAMI, astronomers have transitioned from measuring global, aperture-biased metallicities to mapping the complete 2D chemical topography of galactic disks.   

The overwhelming consensus from these resolved observations is that isolated, local star-forming spiral galaxies uniformly possess negative radial gas-phase metallicity gradients. The interstellar medium in the central cores of these galaxies is highly enriched, while the metallicity steadily declines as a function of galactocentric radius into the outskirts. This ubiquitous chemical structure strongly supports the "inside-out" paradigm of galactic disk formation. In this framework, the dense central regions of dark matter halos collapse and ignite star formation earliest and most efficiently, rapidly driving up the central metallicity. The outer disk regions, composed of higher-angular-momentum material, accrete and form stars on much longer, protracted timescales, leaving them relatively metal-poor at the present day.   

[CHEM-E07]

role: established

finding: Over cosmic time, stellar radial migration continuously flattens and blurs existing stellar metallicity gradients by efficiently churning star orbits via resonant interactions with transient spiral arms and central bars.

scope/boundary: Milky Way and generic local disk chemical-kinematic models, stellar population distributions, and N-body simulations.

confidence note: High; purely in-situ star-formation models cannot explain the extreme scatter in local stellar age-metallicity distributions; radial migration is dynamically unavoidable in structured disks.

sources:Sellwood & Binney (2002, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society) | DOI:10.1046/j.1365-8711.2002.05806.x; ADS:2002MNRAS.336..785S | role=established | Radial migration of stars flattening chemical gradients over time

While the gas-phase metallicity gradient traces the current state of inside-out star formation, the stellar metallicity gradient—the fossil record—does not perfectly preserve the gradient of the gas from which the stars were born. The assumption that stars remain locked at their original birth radii (often used in simple multi-zone chemical evolution models) has been thoroughly dismantled.   

As demonstrated canonically by Sellwood & Binney (2002), stars experience rapid and significant radial migration across the galactic disk due to resonant interactions with transient spiral density waves and galactic bars. This specific mechanism, known as "churning," allows stars at the corotation resonance of a spiral pattern to efficiently exchange angular momentum without significantly increasing their orbital eccentricity (heating). Consequently, stars on near-circular orbits can permanently migrate kiloparsecs inward or outward from their birth radii in a matter of a few hundred million years.   

This radial migration dramatically impacts galactic archaeology. It systematically flattens the native, steeper stellar metallicity gradients that were established at high redshift. Furthermore, because metal-rich stars from the inner disk migrate outward and metal-poor stars from the outer disk migrate inward, radial migration naturally produces a massive scatter in the age-metallicity relation at any given radius. The presence of super-solar metallicity stars in the solar neighborhood is a direct observational consequence of outward radial migration.

sources:Mannucci et al. (2010, Monthly Notices of the Royal Astronomical Society) | DOI:10.1111/j.1365-2966.2010.17291.x; arXiv:1005.0006; ADS:2010MNRAS.408.2115M | role=established | Definition of the Fundamental Metallicity Relation minimizing scatter via SFR

The correlation between a galaxy's stellar mass and its gas-phase metallicity (the MZR) is one of the tightest scaling relations in astrophysics. However, significant intrinsic scatter remains in the 2D relation. This scatter is largely resolved by introducing the star-formation rate (SFR) as a third axis, creating the Fundamental Metallicity Relation (FMR). The FMR dictates that, for galaxies of a fixed stellar mass, those with higher current SFRs will systematically exhibit lower gas-phase metallicities, and vice-versa.   

The physical mechanism driving the FMR represents the core of the baryonic cycle. The anti-correlation between SFR and metallicity diagnosing a transient state of gas flow. When a galaxy experiences an intense accretion event of pristine, metal-poor gas from the cosmic web, the immediate effect is a profound dilution of the local ISM metallicity. Simultaneously, this sudden influx of cold gas dramatically compresses and fuels a burst of star formation, spiking the specific SFR. Over the subsequent tens of millions of years, the newly formed massive stars will undergo core-collapse supernovae, releasing a massive payload of oxygen and metals back into the ISM. This feedback rapidly re-enriches the gas, pushing the galaxy back up to its equilibrium metallicity state while simultaneously driving mass-loaded outflows that quench the starburst. The FMR, therefore, does not just describe a static scaling law; it is a snapshot of galaxies caught in various stages of the dilution-starburst-enrichment cycle.   

2. Open debates and tensions

[CHEM-D01]

debate_topic: IMF and Yield Dependences versus [α/Fe] as a pure star-formation timescale diagnostic.

competing positions: The classical framework of galactic archaeology utilizes the [α/Fe] abundance ratio purely as a "chemical clock." Because α-elements are released promptly by CCSN (<30 Myr) and Fe is delayed via SNe Ia (hundreds of Myr to Gyr), a high [α/Fe] ratio is interpreted as evidence of a very short, truncated star-formation history that finished before SNe Ia could contribute. However, competing perspectives argue this is degenerate. Variations in the Initial Mass Function (IMF)—specifically a "top-heavy" IMF that produces a higher ratio of massive stars—would inherently overproduce α-elements relative to iron regardless of the timescale. Additionally, metallicity-dependent mass-loss rates in massive stars can radically alter their specific chemical yields before they explode, breaking the assumption of constant α-production.

why unresolved: Separating a truncated star-formation history from an anomalous IMF requires independent, high-precision measurements of low-mass stellar populations (which are hopelessly faint at high redshifts) or exquisite constraints on the exact stellar yields of massive stars across extreme metallicity ranges, neither of which is currently achievable.

source/sample/calibration boundaries: Stellar archaeology, Milky Way thick disk, and extragalactic integrated light spectroscopy across all metallicities.

sources:Tinsley (1980, Fundamentals of Cosmic Physics) | ADS:1980FCPh....5..287T | role=debate | Establishing the classic alpha-element time-delay framework

[CHEM-D02]

debate_topic: Extreme early Nitrogen-to-Oxygen (N/O) enrichment during the Epoch of Reionization.

competing positions: Standard chemical evolution strictly requires hundreds of millions of years for intermediate-mass stars to evolve into AGBs and enrich the ISM with secondary nitrogen, meaning highly primitive galaxies should be firmly located on the low-N/O primary plateau. However, breakthrough JWST observations of GN-z11 at z=10.6 directly contradict this, revealing a highly supersolar N/O ratio (log(N/O)>−0.25) just 440 Myr after the Big Bang, combined with very low total metallicity. Competing explanations invoke completely non-standard enrichment: either extreme, mass-loaded winds from dense clusters of Wolf-Rayet stars, tidal disruption events involving massive stars, runaway stellar collisions in proto-globular clusters, or highly anomalous yields from pristine Population III stars.

why unresolved: The required fine-tuning for standard AGB enrichment is mathematically untenable within the strict 440 Myr cosmological time limit, yet none of the exotic proposed mechanisms have yet been proven to dominate high-z galaxy populations broadly.

source/sample/calibration boundaries: Extremely high-redshift (z>10) rest-UV emission line spectra (N III], N IV], O III] transitions), dependent on precise ionization corrections.

[CHEM-D03]

debate_topic: The direction and driver of radial gas-phase metallicity gradient evolution over cosmic time.

competing positions: Fundamental models of inside-out disk growth predict that high-redshift galaxies should possess steeper negative metallicity gradients than local galaxies, as the dense central regions enrich rapidly while the extended disk remains pristine. These gradients should then slowly flatten over time. Conversely, IFU observations of z∼1−3 galaxies often reveal the exact opposite: an extreme diversity of gradients that are frequently flat or even dynamically inverted (positive, where centers are metal-poor). One camp argues this reflects reality, where extreme cosmic accretion (cold flows) directly feeds pristine gas to the core, or where violent stellar feedback preferentially ejects metal-rich gas from the shallow central potential wells. The opposing camp argues these flat/positive gradients are largely observational artifacts caused by extreme "beam-smearing" (where poor spatial resolution artificially blends the bright center with the dim outskirts) or merging interactions that funnel gas inward.

why unresolved: The spatial resolution achievable at high redshift (z>1) even with adaptive optics is frequently broader than the intrinsic half-light radius of the galaxy, making gradient recovery highly model-dependent. Furthermore, the strong dependence of gradients on the specific star formation rate indicates gradients are highly transient at high-z.

source/sample/calibration boundaries: z∼0.6−3 star-forming disks mapped with near-infrared IFUs (e.g., KMOS, SINFONI, OSIRIS).

[CHEM-D05]

debate_topic: Inflow versus Outflow dominance in driving the Fundamental Metallicity Relation.

competing positions: It is heavily debated whether the anti-correlation between SFR and metallicity at fixed mass (the FMR) is primarily an inflow-driven or outflow-driven phenomenon. The inflow camp argues the FMR is caused by stochastic accretion of pristine cosmological gas, which simultaneously dilutes the ISM and provides the fuel for a starburst. The outflow camp argues that the FMR is driven by differential metal ejection: phases of extreme starbursts drive highly mass-loaded, supernova-powered galactic winds that preferentially expel the newly synthesized metals from the galaxy entirely, lowering the effective yield.

why unresolved: Both mechanisms operate simultaneously in analytical bathtub models and hydrodynamical simulations. Distinguishing instantaneous inflow rates from outflow metal-loading factors observationally requires incredibly difficult CGM absorption line mapping and high-resolution kinematic mapping of galactic fountains, which cannot be routinely done for the massive samples used to define the FMR.

source/sample/calibration boundaries: Gas-regulator analytic models and large global volumetric abundance surveys.
