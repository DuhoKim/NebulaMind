## Deep Research review-base result

The Cosmic Baryon and Metal Cycles: An Exhaustive Source-Base Review

The evolution of galaxies across the vast expanse of cosmic time is fundamentally governed by the continuous flow and physical transformation of matter—a paradigm formally recognized in modern astrophysics as the cosmic baryon cycle. Within this overarching theoretical and observational framework, normal matter, or baryons, is not static; it dynamically accretes from the vast, filamentary reservoirs of the intergalactic medium (IGM) into the deepening gravitational potential wells of dark matter halos. Once captured within these halos, this diffuse gas must traverse multiple complex thermodynamic phases. It cools from hot, low-density coronal halos that make up the circumgalactic medium (CGM) into the cold, dense atomic and molecular clouds that constitute the interstellar medium (ISM) of galaxies. This cold ISM is the critical fuel reservoir for all subsequent star formation. As stars form, evolve, and ultimately die, their internal stellar nucleosynthesis generates elements heavier than helium—astrophysically termed "metals." These newly forged heavy elements do not remain locked within the stars or their immediate surroundings; rather, they are violently redistributed via powerful supernova explosions and energetic stellar wind feedback mechanisms. This feedback ejects enriched material back into the ISM, expels it outward into the extended CGM, and frequently drives it completely out of the galactic potential well, polluting the highly ionized IGM.   

Tracing the absolute cosmic inventories of these constituents—baryons, metals, and interstellar dust—across billions of years of cosmic time is paramount to constraining both hydrodynamic simulations and semi-analytic models of large-scale structure formation. A meticulous synthesis of contemporary observational censuses relies heavily on diverse multi-wavelength data. Quasar absorption-line spectroscopy remains the gold standard for probing diffuse foreground gas, relying on the imprints left by intervening clouds on the light of distant background sources. Concurrently, millimeter and sub-millimeter arrays map the molecular gas and dust continuum in emission, while the modern phenomenon of fast radio burst (FRB) dispersion measures offers a revolutionary method to quantify the highly ionized, low-density IGM that evades traditional optical detection. By synthesizing these empirical constraints, the 2020 review by Céline Péroux and J. Christopher Howk reveals a profoundly dynamic universe. Their synthesis demonstrates that the observed decline in the global star-formation rate density at low redshift is demonstrably driven by a diminished supply of molecular gas, a phenomenon fundamentally tied to declining cosmic accretion rates and the slowing growth of dark matter halos. The subsequent sections comprehensively extract the boundaries, established findings, open debates, and primary source literature that form the bedrock of our understanding of the cosmic baryon and metal cycles.   

1. Review Identity and Scope Map

Establishing the precise boundaries of the astrophysical censuses under consideration requires a strict mapping of the scientific territory covered by the core review, as well as an explicit acknowledgment of the subjects deliberately excluded to maintain focus on the evolution of normal matter in non-active galaxies.   

The core review utilized to build this source base is titled "The Cosmic Baryon and Metal Cycles," authored by Céline Péroux and J. Christopher Howk. It was published in the Annual Review of Astronomy and Astrophysics, Volume 58, spanning pages 363-406 in the year 2020. The document can be uniquely identified via its Digital Object Identifier (DOI) 10.1146/annurev-astro-021820-120014, its preprint server designation arXiv: 2011.01935, and its Astrophysics Data System (ADS) bibcode 2020ARA&A..58..363P.   

The authorized scientific territory of this review is expansive yet highly specific. It seeks to quantify the comoving mass density of normal matter across multiple distinct physical phases: stars, neutral atomic gas (HI), molecular gas (H2), and highly ionized gas situated within the circumgalactic medium, intracluster medium, and intergalactic medium. Furthermore, the scope includes rigorous assessments of cosmological metal yields, the phase partitioning of these metals (evaluating the fraction of metals residing in cold gas versus hot halos versus stars), and the evolution of interstellar dust. A major objective of the review is addressing the historical "missing metals" discrepancy, a fundamental tension where the integral of cosmic star formation historically predicted far more heavy elements than observational censuses could locate. 2. Established Findings

The overarching architecture of the cosmic baryon budget reveals a striking reality: galaxies are profoundly open systems. According to the foundational parameters established by Big Bang nucleosynthesis and the cosmic microwave background, normal matter makes up approximately 4% of the critical density of the Universe. Yet, even after billions of years of gravitational collapse and star formation, the vast majority of this matter has not been converted into stars. Over 90% of the Universe's baryons remain in a gaseous state, residing in the interstellar medium, diffuse circumgalactic halos, and vast intergalactic filaments.   

The quantification of these gaseous reservoirs relies heavily on understanding their specific physical phases. Neutral atomic hydrogen (HI), the raw precursor material for galaxy building, is primarily traced through Damped Lyman-α (DLA) systems in the spectra of distant quasars. These systems, characterized by immense column densities that produce distinct damping wings in their absorption profiles, contain the bulk of the Universe's neutral gas. Observations demonstrate that the cosmic density of molecular gas strongly mirrors the shape of the global cosmic star-formation rate density (SFRD). Both quantities rise from the early Universe, peak at "cosmic noon" around redshift z∼2, and subsequently undergo a steep decline toward the present epoch. By taking the ratio of the molecular gas density to the star-formation rate density, astrophysicists derive the cosmic molecular gas depletion timescale. This global, volume-averaged timescale is found to be roughly constant across cosmic time. The constancy of the depletion time indicates a universal physical relationship between available cold gas reservoirs and the efficiency of star formation. Consequently, the dramatic decline in the cosmic star-formation history over the last several billion years is fundamentally driven by a lack of molecular gas supply, which is ultimately caused by a drop in the net cosmological accretion rate as the growth of dark matter halos slows in a dark-energy-dominated Universe.

In the modern Universe at low redshift (z<1), the majority of the total observed metal mass density has become locked within the stellar populations of galaxies. Furthermore, deep surveys of the circumgalactic medium and the highly ionized warm-hot intergalactic medium (WHIM) have increasingly located heavily ionized metals (such as O VI and O VII) outside of galaxies. When combining these diverse reservoirs—stars, cold ISM gas, dusty sub-millimeter galaxies, and the multiphase CGM—the modern census achieves near closure. Therefore, the review synthesizes that there is little empirical evidence remaining for a severe "missing metals problem" in the contemporary Universe, resolving a long-standing tension in chemical evolution models.   

The cycling of metals is intimately tied to the presence of interstellar dust, which plays a crucial role in cooling the ISM and catalyzing the formation of molecular hydrogen. Observations tracking the dust content of neutral gas over cosmic time reveal that the cosmic dust-to-gas ratio scales directly with metallicity, falling significantly as one probes lower metallicity environments. Interestingly, the dust-to-metals ratio is not a fixed constant; it also drops with decreasing metallicity, indicating that the processes governing dust production and survival are less efficient in the pristine conditions of the early Universe.   

Perhaps the most profound recent finding regarding the cosmic baryon inventory stems from the study of Fast Radio Bursts (FRBs). For decades, mapping the highly diffuse, highly ionized gas in the IGM proved exceptionally difficult due to its low density and lack of easily observable emission or absorption lines. However, the transient, highly energetic radio pulses of FRBs undergo frequency-dependent dispersion as they travel through the intergalactic plasma. By measuring the dispersion measures of localized FRBs, astronomers can determine the total electron column density along cosmological sightlines. This technique essentially accounts for every ionized baryon between the observer and the burst source. The resultant measurements yield a late-universe cosmic baryon density that perfectly aligns with the requirements of Big Bang nucleosynthesis and the cosmic microwave background, definitively locating the vast majority of the Universe's normal matter in the diffuse, ionized intergalactic medium.

3. Open Debates and Tensions

Despite profound advancements in tracing the cosmic matter inventory, the aggregation of these multi-wavelength censuses is not without conflict. The 2020 review outlines several systematic uncertainties and ongoing debates regarding tracer fidelity, phase state corrections, and the physical interpretation of heavy-element distributions.   

A primary area of debate centers on the exact nature of census closure regarding the historical "missing metals" problem. While the aggregate census of metals across stars, cold gas, and hot halos largely matches the expected yields derived from the cosmic star-formation history, the specific phase partitioning remains contentious. In particular, the exact metal mass fraction hidden in the ultra-hot, million-degree Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) is notoriously difficult to constrain via X-ray absorption, leaving room for debate on whether the metal budget is perfectly closed or simply hiding within observational error margins.   

Tracer incompleteness also fuels significant tension. For instance, there are lingering disagreements regarding DLA selection biases in optical quasar surveys. Some researchers argue that optical sightlines systematically miss the most massive, dust-obscured neutral hydrogen systems because the background quasars are heavily reddened and thus drop out of magnitude-limited optical catalogs. 5. What Remained Unknown in 2020

The boundary of astrophysical knowledge in 2020, situated just prior to the deployment of next-generation observatories like the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) and the full Square Kilometre Array (SKA), left several specific gaps in the empirical tracing of the baryon cycle.   

A critical unknown was the direct, spatially resolved kinematics of CGM inflows versus outflows. While the decline of the cosmic star formation rate was mathematically linked to a drop in net accretion, directly observing and isolating the faint, low-metallicity cold streams of infalling gas from the highly enriched, energetic galactic winds proved exceptionally difficult. Resolving this required down-the-barrel, multi-phase spectroscopy of isolated halos to definitively verify the accretion starvation hypothesis. Furthermore, while aggregate censuses implied closure of the metal budget, the exact metal mass fraction hidden in the million-degree Warm-Hot Intergalactic Medium (WHIM) remained unmeasured. Constraining this required ultra-high-resolution X-ray absorption spectroscopy of distant background quasars, capabilities that were largely beyond the scope of 2020-era X-ray observatories.   

At the other end of the temperature spectrum, the distribution and total density of molecular hydrogen (H
2
	​

) at extreme redshifts (z>5) remained virtually uncharted. Understanding this molecular reservoir is crucial for determining the onset of the earliest epoch of star formation and the transition from pristine Population III stars to heavily enriched modern stellar populations. Similarly, the completeness of neutral gas censuses was questioned regarding heavily dust-obscured, metal-rich DLAs. A complete accounting using radio/mm-selected blind DLA surveys was needed to determine if optical surveys systematically undercounted the most chemically enriched phases of the high-z Universe.   

Finally, the physics of early-universe dust and stellar yields presented significant unknowns. The specific dust destruction rates and survival mechanisms within early supernova remnants dictate the origin of the first solid matter and the subsequent evolution of the dust-to-metals ratio. Additionally, the assumed universality of the Initial Mass Function (IMF) in extreme high-redshift or low-metallicity environments remained a persistent theoretical vulnerability; because the IMF dictates the expected yields in all "missing metals" calculations, any systematic variations in the IMF would require a recalculation of the entire cosmic metal census.
