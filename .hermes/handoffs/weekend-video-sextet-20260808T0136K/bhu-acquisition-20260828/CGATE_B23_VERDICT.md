## Facts

### Fact 1 — confirmed

The 2004 falsifier runs through the **strange-quark mass**, denoted by a glyph mangled in the text extraction but explicitly named in prose. Smolin introduces §4 by saying the test concerns “the dependence of the upper mass limit of neutron stars on the mass of the strange quark.” His chain is:

1. There is a critical strange-quark mass.
2. Below it, K⁻ condensation is claimed to yield a neutron-star upper mass near 1.5 M☉.
3. Above it, conventional equations of state give a higher upper limit, “almost certainly above 2.”
4. A sufficiently massive neutron star—certainly 2.5 M☉ in Smolin's wording—would place the actual strange-quark mass on the latter side and contradict the Bethe–Brown claim.

The quoted facts are faithful to the source.

### Fact 2 — confirmed; the absence claim meets the standard

`M_LC` is not the parameter of Smolin's 2004 falsifier. The complete text contains none of `Landau`, `M_LC`, or `M_{LC}`. More importantly, this is not merely a three-pattern inference: the whole falsifier section explicitly identifies the varied fundamental parameter as the strange-quark mass and follows it through the critical-value construction.

The phrase “upper mass limit for stable neutron stars” on p. 708 occurs in the fifth item of a list of conditions conducive to black-hole production. It names a desired **derived outcome**, not the independent parameter varied in §4. Likewise, the upper neutron-star mass limit in §4 is the response variable. It is not Rothman–Ellis's Landau–Chandrasekhar mass scale under an unrecognized alias.

This absence claim is therefore not another defect-1aa case. Its conclusion rests on an exhaustive short source, inspection of the plausible semantic alias, and—decisively—Smolin's affirmative identification of the actual parameter.

### Fact 3 — confirmed, with a finite-change qualification

Smolin says the heavy-neutron-star observation would refute S because a **decrease in the strange-quark mass** would lead to a lower neutron-star upper mass and therefore more black holes. That is indeed a local-maximum/unidirectionality-style falsifier: it identifies a parameter direction in which black-hole production would rise rather than fall.

The wording should not imply that every infinitesimal decrease has this effect. The stated mechanism requires a sufficient decrease to cross the hypothesized critical mass and switch the core/EoS regime. This is compatible with S's wording about the “first significant changes,” but it matters mechanically.

## Inference — refuted

The claim that the 2004 falsifier **is the defence** against Rothman–Ellis does not follow.

First, Smolin does not make that connection. In §3 he cites Rothman & Ellis [13] and Ellis [14] while answering the neutron-universe/star-formation objection; footnote 1 credits them on the unnecessary closed-universe assumption. In §4 he introduces the heavy-neutron-star argument as an answer to the objection that S is **not testable**, citing Silk [31] and Rees [32]. He neither cites Rothman–Ellis there nor says that the strange-quark construction answers their general unidirectionality criticism. The two arguments share a logical form, but textual responsiveness has not been shown.

Second, a falsification condition is not a defence of the proposition it may falsify. Rothman and Ellis argue that CNS requires parameter changes on both sides of the present value to reduce black-hole production and that it is easy to find directions that instead increase it or do nothing. Smolin's test says, conditionally, that a heavy neutron star would expose **another** direction that increases black-hole production and thereby refute S. That makes the criticism observable in one new parameter direction; it does not establish that no such direction exists. Before the bar fires, mere non-observation of a sufficiently heavy neutron star does not demonstrate that the present strange-quark mass is at a local maximum of black-hole production.

Third, one parameter cannot answer the general problem over parameter space. A successful calculation showing the required behavior on both sides of the strange-quark mass would defend S in that direction only. It would not neutralize Rothman–Ellis's explicit counterexamples involving α or the Landau–Chandrasekhar scale, nor establish the required behavior for all other significant parameters.

Fourth, even the one-parameter test is conditional on the kaon-condensation chain. Smolin concedes that the Bethe–Brown calculations use approximations and “may be sufficiently inaccurate.” He asserts reasonable confidence in a critical value, but does not independently derive the sign and magnitude of the neutron-star-mass response. If the proposed K⁻ condensate, transition, or EoS effect is wrong, a heavy neutron star refutes that instrument chain; the claimed parameter change yielding more black holes no longer follows, so the observation does not by this argument refute S. Footnote 6 separately concedes that parameter coupling could defeat the independent-variation premise, although Smolin properly rejects an unsupported ad hoc rescue.

The defensible narrower inference is: **Smolin 2004 converts the same kind of directional counterexample that concerns Rothman–Ellis into a proposed observational falsification route for a different parameter.** That is evidence that he understood how S could fail, and it strengthens S's testability. It is not a defense of S's local-maximum warrant and cannot close open question 4 in S's favor. Calling it a defence smuggles precisely such a verdict into the framing.
