## 1. Junction construction

The construction actually displayed here matches an interior, spatially flat FLRW region to an exterior Schwarzschild (SW) region. The paper first identifies the late-time FLRW interior with the trapped de Sitter regime: the de Sitter horizon is (r_*=1/H_\Lambda) (lines 56–67), observations make the FLRW expansion vacuum dominated, and the paper says the FLRW metric then describes the interior of the same trapped surface (lines 81–91). It next says explicitly that FLRW comoving coordinates are matched to SW proper coordinates, with the de Sitter-inside/SW-outside special case called BH.fv (lines 117–121).

Some matching work is reproduced in this paper. The junction is a timelike constant-χ hypersurface (lines 122–127); equality of the induced metrics and extrinsic curvatures is stated, but the reduction of those conditions is explicitly attributed to Gaztanaga (2021) (lines 129–134). The displayed result is (r=R=a\chi_\Sigma), Ṙ² = (R²H²=R_*/R), with (R_*=2GM) (lines 132–136). At the limiting horizon it gives (R_*=2GM=1/H_\Lambda) (lines 137–140). Thus this article gives a compact derivation/check, while relying on the earlier BHU paper for the reduced junction equations rather than deriving all Israel-condition components afresh.

The surface stress is not separately calculated or parameterized. Instead, after asserting equal extrinsic curvature on both sides, the article concludes that the joint metric solves Einstein's equations “with no surface terms in the junction” (lines 129–140). That is a zero-thin-shell claim, not a stated nonzero surface stress tensor.

“There could be a different universe outside” rests on two moves: the FLRW–SW junction just described and the claim that SW itself is a perturbation embedded in a larger FLRW background. On that basis the authors say that beyond (1/H_\Lambda) there is “another FLRW metric,” with nested BHUs possible (line 143). The existence/type and parameters of that outer universe are not obtained by solving an observational inverse problem here.

## 2. Incoming-photon imprint

The paper establishes only causal access geometrically. Equation (7) makes outgoing signals unable to escape, while the incoming radial null distance can exceed (r_*), so an interior observer can in principle see what occurred outside (lines 108–115); this conclusion is repeated after the particle-horizon discussion (lines 159–175). The figure discussion specifies that at last scattering both frozen and causally disconnected perturbations are observable (lines 177–182).

Every subsequent sentence that makes the imprint observationally specific says, in substance:

- At last scattering, χΣ subtends θ = χΣ/(d_A\lesssim1) rad ≈ 60°, so scales larger than χΣ can be observed despite being causally disconnected (line 184).
- This “could be related” to CMB anomalies or cross-scale/cross-time parameter tensions (line 185).
- A cited Planck temperature-anisotropy analysis finds three sky regions in best-fit ΩΛ (line 189); their radii are 40–70° and are said to agree with the causal-boundary scale (line 190).
- The cited work finds region size correlated with mean ΩΛ and calls that good agreement with BHU (line 191), while other fitted ΛCDM parameters show the same large anisotropic patterns (line 192).
- The paper assigns the anisotropy a Gaussian-isotropic fluctuation probability of order (10^{-9}), again from the cited analysis (lines 193–194).
- Its own summary says angular size versus fitted ΩΛ agrees with BHU, disconnected regions fit the same acoustic model, and their fitted backgrounds differ significantly (lines 196–200); it then infers a source operating beyond the causal universe (lines 201–202). Figure 2 is explicitly a map of partial-sky best-fit ΩΛ values, not a photon-transfer prediction (line 204).

There is no junction-derived temperature morphology, (C_\ell), amplitude, or sign. The only derived observable specification is the same causal angular scale of order 60°; “incoming photons” supplies a causal interpretation, and the reported sky structures are then compared with that scale.

## 3. Fossil-record analysis and the challenged claim

The fossil picture comes from Fosalba & Gaztañaga (2021), not a new map analysis performed in this paper: section 2.2 opens by citing that work for the Planck temperature-anisotropy result (lines 187–191), sends the reader back to its Figure 31 for the size agreement (lines 196–197), and identifies the study in the bibliography as *Explaining cosmological anisotropy: Evidence for causal horizons from CMB data* (lines 430–439). Camacho-Quevedo & Gaztañaga is not cited or named in this paper.

Entry 24 presents the prior measurement as agreement, not as a prospective test with a pre-stated rejection threshold. Its language is “in agreement” and “good agreement” after reporting 40–70° regions and correlations (lines 189–192), followed by a three-part retrospective summary (lines 196–200). It states no advance acceptance band, likelihood for a BHU template, or BHU-versus-alternative decision rule.

The abstract's “challenges” refers to the origin of the primordial universe (lines 14–17). The body makes this concrete: it calls the anisotropy a breakdown of the Big Bang model's large-scale-isotropy assumption (lines 193–194), says the inferred beyond-horizon source is in apparent tension with simple inflationary models, and proposes revisiting the origin of the primordial universe (lines 201–202). It is not a calibrated falsification criterion for BHU.

## 4. Calibratable additions

No new calibratable observable absent from the series is supplied. The numerical quantities are:

- (r_*=1/H_\Lambda), and the matched identity (2GM=1/H_\Lambda) (lines 62–65, 136–140);
- the standard-background particle horizon χO(1) ≈ 3.26 (c/H_0) for ΩΛ ≈ 0.7 (lines 165–168);
- χΣ ≈ 3.34 (c/H_0), explicitly reported as a prior Gaztañaga (2021) result obtained from the light-cone average relation ρΛ = ⟨ρm/2 + ρR⟩ for ΩΛ ≈ 0.7 (lines 172–175);
- the resulting ≲1 rad ≈ 60° angular scale (line 184);
- observed 40–70° region radii and a cited order-(10^{-9}) isotropic-Gaussian fluctuation probability (lines 189–194).

The 60° scale is directional/non-circular at the series level as disclosed in the brief, but this paper does not add an independently predicted amplitude or map statistic. The 40–70° radii, fitted ΩΛ maps, correlation, and (10^{-9}) figure are outputs of the data analysis being explained, not prior BHU calibration constants. The exterior is merely described as another FLRW region (line 143): no exterior mass, exterior Λ, density parameters, or other outer-universe parameters are inferred from observation. The only mass relation is the formal junction identity (2GM=1/H_\Lambda) (lines 136–140), fixing the inner BH representation from the interior horizon rather than measuring an exterior universe.

## 5. Tier consequence

**QUALITATIVE-DIRECTIONAL holds.** Entry 24 contains a real geometric direction: a causal boundary should appear at an angular scale of order 60°, and incoming null paths make beyond-boundary information visible (lines 108–115, 177–185). It does not reach CALIBRATED-FALSIFIER because it supplies no predicted temperature pattern, sign, amplitude, (C_\ell), likelihood, threshold, or prospective rejection rule. It also does not merit a separate stronger observable: section 2.2 imports an already-completed CMB map analysis and labels its 40–70° structures and parameter correlations agreement (lines 187–204). CONSISTENCY-ONLY would understate the derived directional scale, while PROSPECT would understate the presented comparison to actual data. Under A(a), a lane could own a missing decision threshold, but not the missing quantitative signal; here both the junction-to-CMB signal amplitude/template and a pre-stated threshold are absent, and data-derived region sizes cannot repair that calibration gap.

In plain language: this paper gives a compact argument that our FLRW region can be joined to a Schwarzschild exterior without a thin shell and that light from beyond the causal boundary could be visible to us. It then points to previously measured, very large CMB parameter patches and says their 40–70° sizes resemble the roughly 60° causal scale. It never calculates what those incoming photons should make the CMB look like or how strong, hot, cold, or statistically distributed the signal should be. So it supports the existing qualitative directional claim, but it does not add a new calibrated observable or falsifier.
