AUDIT_HOLDS_QUALITATIVE_DIRECTIONAL

## 1. The hypothesis, exactly

Smolin's two foundational postulates are: “Each final singularity is followed by an initial singularity” in a new spatially closed universe, with tunnelling from a black-hole interior to a new compact universe offered as an equivalent alternative (lines 140–148); and at each such initial singularity/tunnelling event the point `p` in the space of dimensionless parameters “changes by a small random amount” (lines 149–158). Both are assumptions, not results. He adds further assumptions: restrict the ensemble to compact, positive-pressure universes that recollapse and have at least one progeny (lines 159–188); take the mutation step to be sufficiently small (lines 155–158, 267–280); assume the late normalized distribution `A(p)` exists and is peaked near local maxima of the expected progeny count `R(p)` (lines 220–227); and assume ours is a typical universe in that ensemble (lines 227–230).

Conditional on all of those premises, selection supplies the consequence: our parameters should lie near a local maximum of expected black-hole production, so nearby parameter modifications should generally reduce `R(p)` (lines 231–238). The paper's abstract states the same direction: modifications “should tend to decrease the number of black holes” (lines 45–47). The local-maximum claim is therefore a conditional consequence of a model that includes an expressly assumed concentration property, not a theorem derived from the two bounce/mutation postulates alone.

## 2. Predictions and tests actually stated in the paper

1. **Headline local test — direction only.** “The parameters of particle physics are such that most changes in their values should lead to decreases in the expected number of black holes in the universe” (lines 231–238). No numerical tolerance, derivative, confidence level, or rejection boundary is given. Testing needs only local calculations of `R(p)` (lines 239–263), but those calculations are not supplied generally.

2. **Inflationary scalar coupling/fluctuations — schematic conditional prediction, not a calibrated result.** The paper notes the observational/model requirement that the scalar self-coupling be below roughly `10^-12` (lines 406–416), then constructs an assumed one-parameter toy model (lines 439–465). In that model `R(lambda)` has a maximum at the model-dependent `lambda_critical` (lines 477–493), and it “predicts” `lambda` near `lambda_critical` and fluctuations of order `delta rho/rho(lambda_critical)` (lines 517–521). Smolin immediately says, “This cannot yet be taken seriously,” and lists the missing realistic model and estimates (lines 519–526). The `10^-12` is an inflationary constraint cited from prior work, not a CNS falsification threshold calculated by this paper.

3. **Sign of the neutron–proton mass difference — directional.** Reversing the sign, so neutrons are lighter, is argued to make radiative cooling and condensation slower; hence `G(delta m < 0) < G(delta m > 0)` (lines 700–715). The abstract calls the decrease “large” (lines 50–54), but gives no quantitative black-hole reduction or decision threshold.

4. **Increasing the neutron–proton mass difference — explicitly unresolved, despite numerical examples.** The text gives nuclear-physics landmarks: helium would not bind above 14 MeV; probably no stable composite nuclei above “say, 18 MeV”; the observed difference is 1.29 MeV; and deuterium would fail if the difference doubled (lines 546–556). It hypothesizes a local maximum of galactic black-hole production at 1.29 MeV (lines 583–600). Yet its simple comparison of 18 MeV with 1.29 MeV yields more immediate black holes and says “our hypothesis is contradicted, given the assumptions” (lines 716–790); it then rejects the crucial assumption that the stellar birth-rate function is independent of the mass difference (lines 792–827). The paper concludes that sufficiently precise astrophysics did not yet exist (lines 828–837). Thus these are physical scales and a failed toy calculation, not a usable calibrated falsifier. The abstract accurately says whether raising the difference decreases or increases black-hole production is difficult to determine (lines 52–54).

5. **Cold/tepid big-bang nucleosynthesis — possibility, not a prediction.** A crude matter-dominated model gives energy scales of order 100 MeV and suggests that transfer of order 1 MeV per baryon could affect cosmic evolution (lines 944–961). Its expressly limited conclusion is only that it is “not impossible” for nucleosynthesis to alter the gross evolution (lines 962–967), while the bound-state premise still needs detailed calculation (lines 968–972). No direction for total black-hole production or rejection threshold is derived.

6. **Extra-fermion-generation proposal — qualitative conditional falsifier.** The speculative proposal that each universe adds a fermion generation predicts that a newly lightest second or third generation would yield no fusion, nucleosynthesis, stars, or supernovas (lines 974–1003). Smolin calls it “easily falsifiable”: finding a generation which, if lightest, permits processes increasing expected black-hole production would falsify that proposal (lines 1003–1006). This is no numeric threshold and tests an expressly ad hoc subproposal, not the core CNS local-maximum claim.

7. **Other constants mentioned in the audit prompt are not predictions made here.** The paper does not present tests involving the Fermi constant, variation of the strength of the strong interaction, or variation of the pion-mass scale. It identifies the cosmological-constant problem but says, “I have not been able to invent a selective mechanism” for it (lines 429–438), and merely returns to it as an outstanding problem (lines 1010–1014). Those items therefore cannot supply a 1992 threshold or direction.

## 3. The neutron-star point in 1992

The paper does **not** state an upper mass limit for neutron stars. Its only nearby numerical compact-object mass is a deliberately schematic **stellar progenitor cutoff**: it assumes all stars above `p_cutoff` become black holes and those below do not, reporting that Shapiro and Teukolsky “take `p_cutoff` to be 10 solar masses” (lines 646–660). That is not a maximum neutron-star mass and is not attributed as one. Elsewhere “final neutron star or black hole” appears only in explaining uncertain massive-star outcomes (lines 646–654). Therefore the 1992 text contains no neutron-star upper-mass number and no neutron-star falsifier threshold.

## 4. Warrant and objections

The reproduction story does motivate why high-`R` regions multiply: a random walk eventually enters a region with `R(p) > 1`, after which many descendants are created (lines 208–218). But concentration near local maxima is not derived. Smolin labels convergence of `A(p)` and peaking at local maxima “likely consequences,” says they should eventually be shown in appropriate models, and states that “for the moment it is sufficient to assume them” (lines 220–227). Typicality of our universe is another final assumption (lines 227–230). The paper derives local maximality only after inserting those assumptions.

The paper does recognize parameter covariance in limited form: the inflation model says a more general scalar potential has more than one parameter (lines 488–493), and the stellar calculation explicitly holds all other parameters fixed while varying the neutron–proton difference (lines 589–595). It does not, however, answer the objection that a coordinated change of several parameters could increase black-hole production; its headline statement is only that **most** changes should decrease production (lines 231–238), consistent with a local maximum under small mutations rather than every multidimensional displacement. It also does not analyze primordial black holes as a counterexample or alternative dominant production channel. Instead, the stellar section assumes that black-hole production over the universe's lifetime is by stellar collapse (lines 583–589), so primordial production remains an unaddressed warrant gap.

## 5. Tier consequence

**QUALITATIVE-DIRECTIONAL holds; A(a).** The core observable consequence is a sign/direction claim: most sufficiently small nearby changes should lower expected black-hole production. The paper supplies no neutron-star upper-mass value, no numeric CNS decision boundary, and no fully calculated local fitness landscape. Its numerical values (`10^-12`, 1.29/14/18 MeV, 1 MeV per baryon, and the assumed 10-solar-mass progenitor cutoff) are respectively borrowed model constraints, nuclear landmarks, order-of-magnitude illustrations, or schematic assumptions; none becomes a robust 1992 number-plus-threshold falsifier. CONSISTENCY-ONLY is too weak because the paper does articulate refutable directions, while PROSPECT is too weak because at least the sign-reversal neutron–proton claim is an actual qualitative consequence. A calibrated lane cannot manufacture the missing neutron-star number or turn an explicitly inconclusive toy model into a threshold.

In plain language: the paper's real test is that our constants should sit near a black-hole-production hilltop, so nudging most of them should make fewer black holes. It sketches why that might be true and gives one fairly clear directional example—the neutron should not become lighter than the proton—but it does not calculate a dependable numerical boundary. In particular, its 10-solar-mass figure concerns which stars are assumed to become black holes, not the maximum possible neutron-star mass. The local hilltop itself also depends on assumptions Smolin openly says remain to be demonstrated, and primordial black holes and coordinated multi-parameter changes are not resolved. The record should therefore remain QUALITATIVE-DIRECTIONAL.
