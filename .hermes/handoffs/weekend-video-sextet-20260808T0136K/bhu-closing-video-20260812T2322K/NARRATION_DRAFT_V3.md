## Card 01 — assertion heading

**This route closed because it had no calibrated, identifying sky test**

Could our universe be inside a black hole? This is Duho's personal interest, not a NebulaMind research frontier. We read the primary sources to ask what these ideas predict. Here is the verdict. One branch gives pulsars a number to check. But our galaxy-spin route cannot identify a black-hole origin: it has no calibrated target, and other rotating cosmologies can give the same broad signal. This closes one route. It does not say the idea is true or false.

## Card 02 — assertion heading

**“Black-hole universe” names different programmes, not one model**

The first surprise is structural. Lana found at least five different programmes under this label. One identifies a closed universe with a black hole. Another adds a torsion-driven bounce. Another inherits rotation. Cosmological natural selection asks whether black-hole production shapes physical constants. Other baby-universe work points to different observables. They do not share one forecast.

Source: C02; packet lines 143–167 and 368–384.

## Card 03 — assertion heading

**A test needs a target that can be wrong—and that rivals do not share**

To test an idea, the model has to risk being wrong. It needs a number, a range, or a distinctive pattern that a measurement can miss. And that target has to distinguish the model from its rivals. Lana found one clean numerical test in the family she surveyed. It does not use galaxy spins. It uses neutron stars.

Source: C04; packet lines 143–152, 259–278, and 305–317.

## Card 04 — assertion heading

**Cosmological natural selection puts a low ceiling on neutron-star mass**

In cosmological natural selection, universes reproduce through black holes with slightly changed constants. Those constants should sit near values that favour black-hole production. In the Brown–Lee–Rho chain Lana surveyed, the Brown–Bethe maximum neutron-star mass is about one point five solar masses. Their paper says a neutron star at approximately two solar masses or above would put that chain in “serious doubt or simply falsify” it. That is a real, falsifiable number.

Source: C04, C05; packet lines 259–278, 307–310, and 351–355.

## Card 05 — assertion heading

**Measured pulsars have entered the source’s approximately two-solar-mass regime**

Two pulsars reach the critical regime. Demorest's measurement is one point nine seven, plus or minus zero point zero four solar masses; its centre is below two. Fonseca's is two point zero eight, plus or minus zero point zero seven. The quoted sixty-eight point three percent interval stays above two. But at ninety-five point four percent credibility, the result does not clear two.

Source: C06, C07; packet lines 47, 91–97, and 268–277.

## Card 06 — assertion heading

**The evidence enters the test regime; the packet does not call it falsification**

This is where restraint matters. Brown, Lee, and Rho gave a disjunction: a heavy neutron star would put the chain in serious doubt, or simply falsify it. Lana's closing record does not decide which side applies, and neither will this video. The finding is narrower: observations have entered the regime named by the source.

## Card 07 — assertion heading

**The rotating-parent source explicitly says the two handedness counts should differ**

A different programme led to our sky work. If the parent black hole rotates, the child universe inherits its axis. The source says galaxies should tend to align with that axis. It then says clockwise- and counterclockwise-spinning galaxy counts should be different. The spin lane was not chasing a phantom. It followed an explicit, source-backed qualitative claim.

Source: C09, C13; packet lines 143–167, 219–257, 330–342, and 386–401.

## Card 08 — assertion heading

**The handedness claim came after the cited data and never became a numerical forecast**

But that sentence was added in 2025, after the galaxy studies cited as support. It was not a pre-data forecast. The paper gives rotating-frame mechanics, but not a numerical forecast for galaxy handedness: no expected size, no rule for how it changes with scale or redshift, no independently predicted direction, and no acceptance range for a new measurement.

## Card 09 — assertion heading

**A real spin difference would still not identify a black-hole origin**

Suppose a controlled survey found a real spin-handedness difference. That would be interesting. But a positive generic anisotropy would not identify BHU. Other rotating cosmologies can lead to the same broad effect. One observed number would have more than one possible cause. A successful measurement is not automatically a successful test of this model.

Source: C12; packet lines 159–167, 319–328, 351–366, and 386–401. No named rival appears because the packet says externally freezing that comparison would require primary citations.

## Card 10 — assertion heading

**The spin route fails twice: no numerical target, and no unique signature**

The route therefore fails in two independent ways. With no source-defined numerical range, neither a small result nor a finite-precision null can be scored. With no unique signature, a positive result cannot identify BHU. We could build a trustworthy measurement and still be unable to answer the BHU question. The hunt had a source. It did not have a target.

Source: C11, C12, C13, C14; packet lines 153–167, 330–366, and 386–401.

## Card 11 — assertion heading

**Reopen this route only for a calibrated target or a unique signature**

What would change the verdict? First, a published calculation connecting parent-black-hole parameters to a handedness amplitude, scale, or redshift dependence, with a numerical acceptance region. Or second, a signature unique to birth behind a parent horizon, rather than a generic effect of rotation or a bounce. A confirmed spin asymmetry alone would still not test BHU. This route reopens only when a BHU model supplies a calibrated target or a unique signature.

Source: C15, C16; packet lines 403–418.
