Phase 4 report: the confrontation, and what it answered.

The question, in plain words. One published model says our whole universe sits inside a black hole — that the Big Bang was an explosion behind a horizon, with a shock wave standing as a wall around everything we can see. If that is true, and we are not sitting at the exact center, then the sky should look different in the direction of the wall than away from it. So: does what we actually observe rule that out?

To answer it honestly we had to do two things. Work out what the model really predicts, from its own equations. And freeze what the sky actually says, from published measurements, before comparing.

What the model predicts turned out to be sharper, and stranger, than we assumed. First: inside the wall, the sky is exactly normal, from anywhere. The expansion looks the same in every direction no matter how far off center you stand — not slightly lopsided, exactly zero. That killed our own starting idea, which was that a lopsided expansion rate would be the giveaway.

Second: the model has exactly one fingerprint. If the wall is close enough that the oldest light we can see reaches past it, we would see one round patch of altered sky, tens of degrees wide, in one direction. One patch, one axis. Nothing else.

Third, and this is the new number the phase produced: a hiding rule. If our distance off center is less than about 20 percent of the distance to the wall, every ray of the oldest light stays inside, and the wall is invisible. That fraction shrinks toward zero the later the wall would come into view.

Now what the sky says. We froze 18 published measurements, each quote checked against its own source paper, carrying both sides of two arguments the field has not settled. The famous large-scale oddities are in there — the missing power, the low variance, the missing correlations at wide angles.

And here is the answer to whether both can be true. Yes — because they never actually collide. The shapes do not match. Every one of the 7 frozen large-angle measurements is a statistic of the whole sky at once: a deficit, a variance, an odds, a p-value. The model's fingerprint is a bounded patch around one direction. On the 4 criteria we wrote down in advance, being a single localized region fails on all 7 rows, and being a bounded patch against normal surroundings fails on all 7 rows. And the frozen record holds no angular-size number for any of them, so that criterion is an honest gap, not a pass.

So the verdict. The model is not ruled out, and it is not supported. It stays where it was — consistent with the sky — but now with something it never had: a quantified condition for staying hidden, and one named way to make it testable for real, which is to compute what light crossing that wall would actually look like. That work has not been done by anyone, including us, and the report says so.

Two things make this different from a study declined this morning. The judgment criteria were written down and locked before the comparison was made — and the brief has not changed since, verified byte for byte by the reviewing engine. And when our execution quietly bent one criterion, two independent engines caught it, separately, within an hour of each other. The finding survived the correction unchanged; only its stated basis got honest.

The review state, stated exactly. Both reviewing engines have now passed the amended verdict — the first at ten to two, the second just now, after I repaired the one documentation residue it named: the central finding was stated in the body but missing from the summary, which is the part people quote. Track C is closed, and with it Phase 4. What remains open is not a gate but a piece of physics nobody has done: the optics of that wall.
