What the black hole universe work is actually asking, and what is at stake. Read from the document, in plain words.

The question. Is the universe we live in the inside of a black hole? That sounds like a slogan, and most versions of it are. But one version is written in equations rather than words: a pair of mathematicians published an exact solution of Einstein's equations in which the Big Bang was an explosion that happened inside a black hole, and a shock wave — a real, sharp front, like the edge of a blast — surrounds everything we can see. We would be sitting inside that blast, looking outward at a wall we cannot see through.

The work of the last week has not been asking whether that story is true. It has been asking something narrower and more useful: could we ever tell?

Why anyone should care. Ideas about our universe being contained in something larger are cheap. Nearly all of them are unfalsifiable in the strict sense — nothing you could measure would come out differently. This one looked different, because it is a genuine solution with a genuine boundary, and a boundary is the kind of thing that might leave a mark on the sky. So the prize was never to prove we live in a black hole. The prize was to take one member of that family and either give it a real test, or show precisely why it cannot have one. Both outcomes convert an argument into knowledge.

What we found, in the order we found it.

First: from the inside, the model looks exactly like an ordinary universe. This was a surprise and it cost us our starting idea. We had assumed that if we sat off to one side of the wall, the universe would expand slightly faster in one direction than another, and that this lopsidedness would be the giveaway. It is not. The interior of this model is exactly the ordinary expanding universe of textbook cosmology — not approximately, exactly — so measuring expansion in different directions can never detect the wall, no matter how good the measurement gets or how far off centre we sit. That test is dead, permanently, and we killed it ourselves.

Second: the only possible fingerprint is a patch of sky. If the wall happens to be close enough that our view of the oldest light reaches past it, we would see one round region of sky, tens of degrees across, in one direction, that looks different from everywhere else. One patch, one direction. Nothing else about the model is visible at all.

Third, and this is where it got hard: nobody had ever worked out what crossing that wall would look like. The original papers construct the geometry and stop. So we could say where the patch would be, but not whether you could see it — which is the difference between a test and a picture. That question turned out to have a moving answer. My first attempt said the wall was effectively see-through. That was wrong, and wrong in an embarrassing way: inside a black hole the roles of space and time swap over, and I had used a time direction as though it were a distance. Corrected, the answer became — it depends on what the material beyond the wall is made of, and the equations do not tell us.

Fourth, and most important: there is a setting where the model produces no signal at all. Two effects fight each other. The wall's motion brightens the sky on one side, and the fact that different directions look through the wall at different moments in its history dims it on that same side. For one particular composition of the material beyond the wall, those two effects cancel exactly. Not approximately — exactly. In that case an observer could sit as far off centre as you like, and the sky would look perfectly uniform.

I did not find this. An adversarial reviewer did, and it found it because my own check was built in a way that could not have seen it: I took the size of the signal without its sign, and I looked at six settings rather than searching between them. A cancellation lives exactly in the gaps between samples.

So what is actually at stake.

If the model survives, we learn less than people expect. Surviving means not ruled out, and the price of survival is that we would have to be sitting very close to the centre of the blast — within a fraction of a percent of the way to the wall, on current numbers. That is a coincidence, not a confirmation.

If the model is excluded, one specific version of the black hole universe idea dies: the shock wave version, with its particular geometry. It would not kill the broader family.

And if the honest answer is that this cannot be tested by light at all — that is a real result, and it may well be where we land. It would not be a failure or a shrug. It would be the statement that a specific published cosmology, for specific and demonstrable reasons, makes no prediction any telescope can check. That converts an idea that sounds untestable into an idea proved untestable, here, by this argument. Very few speculative cosmologies have been given that dignity, and it is worth more than a weak bound would be.

What is genuinely riskable, stated plainly. The exclusion is not established: it holds across most of the range we can compute, and fails completely in a narrow band. Even outside that band, the strength of the constraint has moved three times as the calculation improved, and once by more than a factor of two after I swept properly something I had previously only sampled. Several of the biggest corrections came from my own errors, caught by review rather than by me. Two independent calculations do now agree on the central physics — that nothing reaches us from beyond the wall, and that the wall's own glow is the only thing we could ever see. That part I would defend.

And the one thing to know before anyone calls this a test. The model can produce no signal at all without being wrong. Any claim that this is a test of black hole cosmology has to survive that sentence first. It is the single most important finding of the phase, it was found by someone attacking my work rather than by me, and it is the reason I am reporting the state of play instead of a result.
