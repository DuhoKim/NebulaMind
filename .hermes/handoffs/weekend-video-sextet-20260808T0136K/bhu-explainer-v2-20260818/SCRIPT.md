# How neutron-star measurements test one black-hole-universe chain

## Panel 01 — This specific chain fails its own second neutron-star test

Could our universe exist inside a black hole? This is Duho's personal side-interest, not a NebulaMind research programme. We tested one chain linked to that idea. We sealed its rule before checking published pulsar measurements. The chain offered 2 neutron-star tests, joined by "or." Its heavy-star test reaches serious doubt. The chain fails decisively on its binary test. This does not falsify the wider black-hole-universe family.

## Panel 02 — Black-hole-universe cosmology is a family, not 1 theory

The starting idea is that a universe can exist inside a black hole. A 1972 paper described a closed universe inside one. Its accessible abstract allowed oscillation and bounded expansion under a radius condition. That is one proposal, not a single theory. Our review found at least 5 distinct black-hole-universe programmes. They disagree with each other.

## Panel 03 — Cosmological natural selection adds reproduction and selection

Cosmological natural selection, or CNS, adds reproduction and selection to this picture. In this proposal, universes reproduce through black holes. Physical constants are numbers that describe how nature behaves. CNS says those constants mutate slightly between generations. Universes producing more black holes would leave more descendants. The constants should therefore sit near values that favor black-hole production.

## Panel 04 — CNS made the cosmic idea checkable with a low mass ceiling

CNS then made a checkable claim. Its stated falsifiable consequence was a low maximum neutron-star mass. A neutron star is an ultra-dense object left after some stars collapse. The route used a proposed change of state in ultra-dense matter, called kaon condensation. It produced the Brown–Bethe ceiling: about 1.5 times the Sun's mass. These masses can be measured through pulsar timing.

## Panel 05 — The source offered 2 tests joined by or

Brown, Lee, and Rho stated 2 ways observations could break this chain. Test 1 was a massive neutron star around 2 times the Sun's mass. Test 2 was a well-measured double neutron star pair differing by more than 4% in mass. The paper joined these tests with "or." Either test was meant to count.

## Panel 06 — The heavy-star test reaches serious doubt

Test 1 gives a bounded result. PSR J0740+6620 measures 2.08 ± 0.07 times the Sun's mass. It clears 2.00 at 68.3%, but not at the stricter 95.4% level. So the heavy-star test reaches serious doubt, not strict falsification. Still, 3 qualifying systems exceed the literal 1.5 ceiling by at least 8 standard deviations each. That ceiling is contradicted.

## Panel 07 — The binary test fails the chain by a wide margin

Test 2 decides the chain. PSR J1913+1102 has measured masses of 1.599 ± 0.008 and 1.290 ± 0.008 times the Sun's mass. Relative to the heavier star, they differ by 19.3 ± 0.7%. The source's limit was 4%. The observed difference is nearly 5 times that threshold. Its 95.4% interval remains above 4% by a wide margin. The asymmetry was published in 2020 and sharpened in 2026.

## Panel 08 — A sealed rule keeps the threshold from following the answer

We wrote and hash-sealed the criterion before the evidence harvest. It admitted dynamical pulsar-timing masses. It also required an uncontested double neutron star system. This kept the thresholds from following the answer. After sealing, we found the source's own He-red-giant caveat. We did not narrow the rule. The source calls that shift small but gives no number. A 19.3% difference remains nearly 5 times the 4% threshold. The verdict is unchanged.

## Panel 09 — The chain loses links; the wider family survives

The verdict must stop at each link. The Brown–Bethe maximum near 1.5 times the Sun's mass is dead as a literal prediction. The earlier particle-physics links face serious doubt through this chain. The heavy-star test did not individually falsify them at our strict standard. CNS loses its flagship neutron-star prediction, but CNS itself is not refuted. The wider family of at least 5 disagreeing programmes also survives this test.

## Panel 10 — The source's second rule had already answered the test

These numbers belong to the pulsar community. We did not discover these stars or remeasure their masses. We applied a pre-registered rule to published measurements. The famous heavy-star test stopped at serious doubt. The less-watched binary test had cleared its threshold in published work since 2020. Neutron stars made this cosmic claim answerable. This specific Brown–Lee–Rho chain fails by the second rule its own authors wrote.
