## Panel 02 — The trial checked 23 claims and left 1 symmetry idea standing

We audited arXiv:1910.10819 version 2 claim by claim. ## Panel 03 — Dark energy puts the paper on both horns of a billion-fold dilemma

The paper identifies cosmic spin with observed dark energy. Follow that statement forward, and dark energy requires rotation at 0.828 times the cosmic expansion rate: 1.09 billion times above the generous Planck rotation bound, and 17.6 billion times above the tighter Saadeh bound. Follow it backward instead, enforcing the generous bound, and rotation supplies less than 1 billion-billionth of observed dark energy. The text provides no third reading.

## Panel 04 — Its conserved spin behaves like radiation, not dark energy

A second failure is independent of the first. The paper says the universe conserves angular momentum as it expands. That premise makes the spin fade with the square of cosmic growth, so its proposed rotational energy thins exactly like radiation. On the paper's own terms, the result cannot accelerate the universe. It also lies about 46 standard deviations from the dark-energy measurement the paper cites as support. The claims fail; the author is not the target.

## Panel 05 — We rebuilt only the survivor as a strict, frozen model

Rather than rescue the failed sectors, we discarded them. The strict model keeps 1 object: a small, axis-carrying cosmic vorticity on an expanding universe. It begins with a Kerr parent black hole, an inheritance knob from 0 to 1, and 10 numbered assumptions, A0 through A9. No rotating global frame, centrifugal dark energy, or unsupported metric correction returns through the back door. Unknown bounce transfer stays named, not hidden.

## Panel 06 — Cosmic expansion erases spin in 2 different stages

The spin-fade law was derived from a pinned vector-perturbation equation and checked by machine algebra. During radiation domination, rotation falls in inverse proportion to cosmic size. During matter and dark-energy domination, it falls with the inverse square. The inflation-era rate has no pinned source, so it remains an explicit parameter rather than a claimed result. Mapping today's microwave-background limit back to galaxy spin-up changes the allowance by only a factor of a few, not enough to rescue it.

## Panel 07 — The missing transfer coefficient is now derived and bracketed

Next came the missing bridge: how much galaxy handedness appears per unit of leftover cosmic spin. Combining tidal-torque growth, turnaround, halo spin statistics, and a coherent rotational push gives a transfer coefficient of about 7.2, with an honest bracket from 1.4 to 12.8. Machine algebra reproduced the coefficient and its bounds. Our local novelty check found no printed analytic transfer function of this kind. The strict model still awaits external theorist review before any publication claim.

## Panel 08 — The strict answer is 0.027 standard deviations across the whole sky

Now the confrontation is direct. At the generous rotation bound and the fiducial galaxy turnaround time, the predicted handedness imbalance is 1.9 parts in 100 million. Even counting all 2 trillion observable galaxies yields only 0.027 standard deviations. A 3-standard-deviation detection would need about 25 quadrillion galaxies: roughly 12,000 observable universes. Favorable choices across the full bracket reach at most 0.08 standard deviations. No survey lever closes that gap.

## Panel 09 — The strict calculation strengthens the earlier closure and constrains the parent

The earlier generous estimate needed 18 observable universes. The derived model needs about 12,000, landing inside the old declared bracket but 25 times below its generous edge. The same chain also turns the microwave-background limit around: inherited parent spin must be diluted by more than 10 to the power 22 for a supermassive parent, or 10 to the power 30 for a stellar parent. That constraint is real but weak: it rules out too little dilution, not parent spin itself.
