Abstract
 
A Universe with finite age also has a finite causal scale. Larger scales can not
affect our local measurements or modeling, but far away locations could have different cosmological parameters. The size of our causal Universe depends on the details of inflation and is usually assumed to be larger than our observable Universe today. To account for causality, we propose a new boundary condition, that can be fulfill by fixing the cosmological constant (a free geometric parameter of gravity). This forces a cancellation of vacuum energy with the cosmological constant. As a consequence, the measured cosmic acceleration can not be explained by a simple cosmological constant or constant vacuum energy. We need some additional odd properties such as the existence of evolving dark energy (DE) with energy-density fine tuned to be twice that of dark matter today.
We show here that we can instead explain cosmic acceleration without DE (or modified gravity) assuming that the causal scale is smaller than the observable Universe today. Such scale corresponds to half the sky at z=1 and 60 degrees at z=1100, which is consistent with the anomalous lack of correlations observed in the CMB.

 

Keywords: Cosmology: dark energy, cosmic background radiation, cosmological parameters, early Universe, inflation

At later times ΩΛ≃1\Omega_{\Lambda}\simeq 1 and galaxies will be torn apart by the new inflation.
Moreover, a§a_{\lx@sectionsign} has the largest Hubble radius (see Fig.2) with the highest chances to host observers like us.
There is nothing too special about this coincidence.
Ultimately, the reason why χ§∼3​c/H0\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\sim 3c/H_{0} reside in the details of inflation: when inflation begins aia_{i} and ends aea_{e} (see Fig.2).

(the larger the angle the smaller ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda}).
But note that this rough estimate does not take into account the foreground (late) ISW and lensing effects (Fosalba et al. 2003; Das &

This requires further investigation. Also note that this estimate for ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda}
corresponds to the size of disconnected regions at the location of the CMB, which might be slightly different to the value near us, as we see a different patch of the primordial Universe (see bellow).
Note also that there are temperature differences on scales larger θ§\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}, but they are not correlated, as expected in causality disconnected regions.
Nearby regions are connected which creates a smooth transition across disconnected regions.

5 Discussion and Conclusions

Λ\LambdaCDM in Eq.1-3 assumes that ρ\rho is constant everywhere at a fixed comoving time.
This requires acausal initial conditions (Brandenberger 2017) unless there is inflation, where a tiny homogeneous and causally connected patch, the Causal Universe χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}, was inflated to be very large today. Regions larger than χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} are out of causal contact. Here we require that
test particles become free (or the relativistic flux is zero)
as we approach χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}. No cause should produce no effect.
This leads to Eq.17, which is the main result in this paper.
If we ignore the vacuum, this condition requires:
Λ=8​π​G​ρ§\Lambda=8\pi G\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}, where ρ§\rho_{\lx@sectionsign} is the matter and radiation inside
χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} (Eq.20). For an infinite Universe
(χ§→∞\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\rightarrow\infty) we have ρ§⇒0\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}\Rightarrow 0 which requires Λ⇒0\Lambda\Rightarrow 0. This is also what we find in classical gravity with a Λ\Lambda term, because Hooke’s term diverges at infinity (see Eq.6). So the fact that Λ≠0\Lambda\neq 0 could indicate that χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} is not infinite. Adding vacuum ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} does not change this argument because ρΛ≡Λ/8​π​G+ρvac=ρ§\rho_{\Lambda}\equiv\Lambda/8\pi G+\rho_{\rm vac}=\rho_{\lx@sectionsign} turns out to be independent of ρvac\rho_{\rm vac}
(see Eq.19).
Thus, whether the causal size of the Universe is finite or not, ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} can not gravitate! The cancellation between Λ\Lambda and ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} is a direct consequence of the boundary condition and it also implies that deSitter Universe (empty with a cosmological constant) is not causal (it produces curvature even when empty) and therefore not a physical model.

For constant vacuum (ω≡p/ρ=−1\omega\equiv p/\rho=-1), we find χ§≃3​c/H0\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 3c/H_{0}
for Ω§=ΩΛ≃0.7\Omega_{\lx@sectionsign}=\Omega_{\Lambda}\simeq 0.7.
We can also estimate χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} as c/(ai​Hi)c/(a_{i}H_{i})
when inflation begins, see Eq.25. After inflation χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} freezes out until it re-enters causality at a§≃0.93a_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 0.93, close to now (a=1a=1). This starts a new inflation (as ρΛ=ρ§>ρm\rho_{\Lambda}=\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}>\rho_{m}) which keeps the causal boundary frozen.
Thus a finite χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} explains why ρΛ≃2​ρm\rho_{\Lambda}\simeq 2\rho_{m}.
It also predicts that CMB temperature should not be correlated above θ>θ§≃60\theta>\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 60 deg. A prediction that matches observations
(see Fig.3). This is one of the well known anomalies measured in the CMB.
One would also expect the CMB spectrum to be anisotropic on the largest scales, which is another well known measured anomaly (see Planck
Collaboration et al. 2014).
One can reverse this argument to use the lack of CMB correlations above
θ§≃60\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 60 deg, to estimate χ§≃θ§​χC​M​B\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\chi_{CMB}. Together with condition ρ§=ρΛ\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}=\rho_{\Lambda}, this provides a prediction of ΩΛ≃0.7±0.1\Omega_{\Lambda}\simeq 0.7\pm 0.1, which is independent of other measurements for ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda}.
More work is needed to account for the late ISW and lensing
and to interpret the CMB measurements with a metric that is not homogeneous (Gaztañaga 2019).

Note that because the Universe is not strictly homogeneous outside a causal region, the causal boundary for observers far away from us could be slightly different from ours, because they see a different patch of the Universe which could have slightly different energy content. Continuity across nearby disconnected regions forces these differences to be small, but it is impossible to quantify this without a model for the initial conditions and a better understanding of the process that generates the primordial homogeneity.
In general such differences could affect structure formation, galaxy evolution and CMB observations. The fact that we can measure a concordance picture from different observations with the Λ\LambdaCDM model indicates that these differences must be small. But tensions between measurements of cosmological parameters (or fundamental constants of nature) from very different redshifts (eg between CMB and local measuremnts) or different parts of the sky at high redhifts (such as dipolar variation of fine structure constant in Webb et al. 2011) could be related to such in-homogeneities, rather than to evolution of the Dark Energy (DE) equation of state or other more exotic explanations.

For χ§>>3​c/H0\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}>>3c/H_{0} we can not explain cosmic acceleration with ω=−1\omega=-1, because the resulting ρΛ\rho_{\Lambda} in Eq.20 would be very small. We need evolving DE with equation of state ωD​E>−1\omega_{DE}>-1 and ρΛ=ρD​E\rho_{\Lambda}=\rho_{DE} today (see Appendix B). But DE gives no clue as to why ρD​E≃2​ρm\rho_{DE}\simeq 2\rho_{m} today and can not explain the anomalous lack of CMB correlations at large scales.
We can apply Occam’s razor to argue that there is no need for DE or Modify Gravity. Comparing Fig.1 with Fig.2, we can see that the measured cosmic acceleration today can be explained by a finite causal scale
χ§≃3​c/H0\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 3c/H_{0}, which is expected for Universe with a finite age and a mechanism like inflation.

We have speculated that the finite causal size of our universe
could result from a quantum fluctuation at the Planck scale lPlanckl_{\rm Planck} which produces
an inflated expansion by a factor ≃e70\simeq e^{70}
leading to a reheating into matter and radiation today.
 This eventually results into late time cosmic acceleration.
The existence of such finite causal scale can be tested further with observations of the variation of cosmological or fundamental parameters over cosmological scales.

Appendix A Hooke’s law

In Newton’s gravity, a point mass mm generates a radial acceleration
g→=−∇→ϕ=−Gmr→/r3\vec{g}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi=-Gm\vec{r}/r^{3},
where ϕ\phi is the Newtonian potential.
The Λ\Lambda term in GR’s field equations, Eq.9, corresponds here to an additional term which is linear in rr, as in Hooke’s law:

g→=−∇→​ϕ=g→N​e​w​t​o​n+g→H​o​o​k​e=−(Gr3+G2)​m​r→\vec{g}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi=\vec{g}_{Newton}+\vec{g}_{Hooke}=-\left(\frac{G}{r^{3}}+G_{2}\right)~m~\vec{r}

(27)

Hooke’s constant, G2G_{2}, can be related to Λ\Lambda, as we will see below.
This Hooke’s term is unique in that it is the only distance dependence (other than the inverse square law) that has a key property for gravity: that a spherical mass shell of arbitrary density ρ⁡(R)\rho(R) produces a gravitational field which is identical to a point source of equal mass in its center:

g→H​o​o​k​e=−G2∫s​h​e​l​ld3R(r→−R→)ρ(R)=−G2mr→\vec{g}_{Hooke}=-G_{2}\int_{shell}d^{3}R~(\vec{r}-\vec{R})~\rho(R)=-G_{2}m~\vec{r}

(28)

where R→\vec{R} covers the shell and r→\vec{r} is some position outside. This is a key property of gravity, needed to treat the Universe as a whole
and sustain Gauss’s and Birkhoff’s theorems.
Thus in Classical Mechanics the above law of gravity Eq.(27) is consistent
with the symmetries and observations, as long as G2G_{2} is small
enough.
In fact Newton, and other scientist, had already noticed this, but did not consider
Hooke’s term for lack of observational evidence (Calder &
Lahav 2008, and references therein).

Notice how Eq.27 diverges for r⇒∞r\Rightarrow\infty. We expect instead that g→⇒0\vec{g}\Rightarrow 0, as particles should be free at infinity (as there could be no causal connection). This explains why on theoretical grounds we would expect G2=0G_{2}=0 even if this relation is allowed by the symmetries of the problem. On the other hand, if causality ends at some finite distance r§r_{\lx@sectionsign}, as the Universe has a finite age, then we see that Newton’s law alone can not describe a causal gravitational interaction. We need to also have Hooke’s law.
Requiring that gravitational forces g→\vec{g} in Eq.27 vanished at the causal boundary r§r_{\lx@sectionsign}:

g→​(r=r§)=0⇒G2=−4​π​G3​V§\vec{g}(r=r_{\lx@sectionsign})=0~~\Rightarrow~~G_{2}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3V_{\lx@sectionsign}}

(29)

so that Hooke’s gravitational force is repulsive and only becomes comparable to Newton’s gravity for separations comparable to r§r_{\lx@sectionsign}.

We can estimate the flux using Eq.(27) to find:

Φ=∮∂Vd​r→​g→=−4​π​G​mV−3​V​G2​m§\Phi=\oint_{\partial V}~d\vec{\mathrm{r}}~\vec{\mathrm{g}}=-4\pi Gm_{V}-3VG_{2}m_{\lx@sectionsign}

(30)

where mVm_{V} is the mass inside VV and
m§m_{\lx@sectionsign} is all the mass in all the (causally connected) universe. As expected, Φ=0\Phi=0 in Eq.30 reproduces Eq.29 for V=V§V=V_{\lx@sectionsign}.
For the Newtonian term,
masses outside VV do not contribute to Φ\Phi because for a small cone center outside and crossing VV the in going flux exactly cancels with the outgoing flux (as the inverse square law in g→\vec{g} compensates the increase in the area crossed then the cone goes inside to when is going outside). This is not the case for the Hooke’s law, where the difference in flux is just proportional to the volume inside VV.
As all the masses contribute equally, we have:

ΦH​o​o​k​e(V)=−G2∑mm(r→)∮∂V(R→−r→)dA→=−3VG2m§\Phi_{Hooke}(V)=-G_{2}\sum_{m}~m(\vec{r})~\oint_{\partial V}~(\vec{R}-\vec{r})~d\vec{A}=-3VG_{2}m_{\lx@sectionsign}

(31)

where the total mass is m§=∑mmm_{\lx@sectionsign}=\sum_{m}m.
This result is also true in arbitrary number of dimensions (see Wilkins 1986)).
This explains why Hooke’s law behaves like vacuum energy (i.e. like negative pressure: ω=−1\omega=-1) or Λ\Lambda in GR: the density
remains constant as we increase the volume!
Comparing Eq.30 with Eq.7 we have:

Λ=−3​G2​m§=4​π​G​ρ§\Lambda=-3G_{2}m_{\lx@sectionsign}=4\pi G\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}

(32)

where in the last step we have also used Eq.29, or equivalently Φ=0\Phi=0 in Eq.30. This of course agrees with Eq.8, which shows that
Eq.27 and Eq.6 are equivalent.

This result provides a Classical Physic’s interpretation of the
cosmological constant: it is just related to Hooke’s constant G2G_{2} and the total mass m§m_{\lx@sectionsign} in the (causal) universe. Note how Λ\Lambda can be zero if either G2=0G_{2}=0 or if m§=0m_{\lx@sectionsign}=0. Note also how G2=0G_{2}=0 for infinite volume and in this case Λ=0\Lambda=0 because m§/V§m_{\lx@sectionsign}/V_{\lx@sectionsign} also goes to zero. This relation establishes a clear connection between the value of Λ\Lambda and the matter-energy content in the Universe, as requiered by causality. It also gives new light into Mach’s Principle.
