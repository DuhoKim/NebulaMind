How the Big Bang Ends up Inside a Black Hole

Enrique Gaztanaga

Abstract
 
The standard model of cosmology assumes that our Universe began 14 Gyrs (billion years) ago from a singular Big Bang creation. This can explain a vast range of different astrophysical data from a handful of free cosmological parameters. However, we have no direct evidence or fundamental understanding of some key assumptions: Inflation, Dark Matter and Dark Energy. Here we review the idea that cosmic expansion originates instead from gravitational collapse and bounce.
The collapse generates a Black Hole (BH) of mass M≃5×1022​M⊙M\simeq 5\times 10^{22}M_{\odot} that formed 25 Gyrs ago. As there is no pressure support, the cold collapse can continue inside in free fall until it reaches atomic nuclear saturation (GeV), when is halted by Quantum Mechanics, as two particles cannot occupy the same quantum state.
The collapse then bounces like a core-collapse supernovae, producing the Big Bang expansion. Cosmic acceleration results from the BH event horizon. During collapse, perturbations exit the horizon to re-enter during expansion, giving rise to the observed universe without the need for Inflation or Dark Energy.
Using Ockham´s razor, this makes the BH Universe (BHU) model more compelling than the standard singular Big Bang creation.

1 Introduction

A cosmological model predicts the evolution of the observed Universe given some initial conditions.
The standard cosmological model Dodelson 2003; Weinberg 2008, called Λ\Lambda Cold Dark Matter (Λ\LambdaCDM), assumes that our Universe expansion began in a hot Big Bang creation at the very beginning of space-time. The Λ\LambdaCDM model explains the formation, composition and evolution of our Universe starting from a quantum fluctuation close to Planck scale. Planck scales are so small that space-time itself has to be treated as a quantum object. Such initial conditions violate energy conservation and are very unlikely as they have a low entropy ( Dyson et al. 2002; Penrose 2006). To address such initial conditions properly, we need a new quantum theory of space-time (Quantum Gravity), which opens the door to brane Cosmology Durrer et al. 2005; Nojiri et al. 2001 which is a very exciting idea, but it is hard to test with observations.

Despite these shortfalls, the Λ\LambdaCDM model is very successful. However, this is at the cost of introducing three more exotic ingredients or mathematical tricks: Inflation, Dark Matter and Dark Energy, for which we have no direct evidence or understanding at any fundamental level.
Are they windows for new discoveries, such as String Theory or new forms of matter/energy, or a signal that the paradigm needs to be replaced? Can we choose some different initial conditions and reproduce the success of the Λ\LambdaCDM model without those exotic fixes and within the known laws of Physics?

Here, we present a brief review that summarizes several recent papers Gaztañaga 2020; Gaztañaga 2021; Fosalba and Gaztañaga 2021; Gaztanaga 2021; Gaztanaga 2022; Camacho-Quevedo and Gaztañaga 2021; Gaztañaga and Fosalba 2022 that suggest a simpler explanation: the Black Hole Universe (BHU). This review also includes some new results and ideas. Some previous studies misinterpreted super horizon scales as scales that were outside the BHU. This is clarified here together with some new details regarding the Big Bounce and the observational interpretation of super horizon perturbations. In Section 2 we give a brief presentation of the Λ\LambdaCDM model and its observational support.
In Section 3 we present the BHU model using a Newtonian approach. In Cosmology, one can use Newtonian physics to model to a good approximation of both the background and its perturbations Bernardeau et al. 2002. A consistent Newtonian version of the BHU solution is a good indication that we understand the physics involved. Appendices A and B present a summary of the same BHU solution in the more rigorous GR approach.
Appendix C presents some new considerations of the possible effect of rotation in the BHU solution.
We end with a Summary and Discussion that includes a review of related literature and previous results and a comparison between models.

2.2 Nucleosynthesis and CMB

In 1964, Penzias and Wilson Penzias and Wilson 1965 accidentally found a uniform Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) radiation of temperature T0≃3​KT_{0}\simeq 3K. Robert Dicke, James Peebles, P. G. Roll, and D. T. Wilkinson in the companion publication Dicke et al. 1965 interpret this radiation as a signature from the hot Big Bang: the oldest light in the Universe.
This was first noticed in 1948 by R.Alpher and R.Herman Alpher and Herman 1948; Alpher 2015 who developed the theory of the primordial nucleosynthesis and predicted a leftover CMB radiation of T0≃5​KT_{0}\simeq 5K, closed to the observed value.
The idea behind it is simple. Because the universe is expanding, when you imagine going back in time the density must become higher and higher, atoms will break and the resulting plasma will be dominated by radiation, like the interior of a star. If you simulate an expansion from such initial conditions you can build a prediction for the primordial abundance of elements and radiation that we observed today. This is called primordial nucleosynthesis.

Hydrogen is the most abundant
element measured in our Universe.
Around ∼75\sim 75% of the total mass of the atoms (nucleons) in the Universe is in the form of hydrogen, the remaining 2525% is mostly Helium. The abundance predicted by nucleosynthesis
depends on the cross section of several Nuclear Physics reactions, such as neutron capture or decay. These are proportional to the ratio η=ρB/ρR\eta=\rho_{B}/\rho_{R} of the number density of baryons ρB\rho_{B} (protons and neutrons) to that of photons, ρR\rho_{R}, given by the CMB background temperature T=T0/aT=T_{0}/a. So a measurement of the primordial element abundance and ρB\rho_{B} can be used to predict T0T_{0}. Nowadays, we use
the more precise measured value T0=2.726​KT_{0}=2.726K and the observed abundance to predict ρB\rho_{B}.
In relative units: ΩB=ρB/ρ≃0.05\Omega_{B}=\rho_{B}/\rho\simeq 0.05 Steigman 2007. So that only ≃5%\simeq 5\% of the total energy-density in our Universe is made of regular matter (i.e., made of known baryons and leptons). The rest, according to Λ\LambdaCDM , is made of Dark Matter and Dark Energy. Where do these numbers come from? Why 5% and not 20%?

2.3 Cosmic Inflation and the Horizon Problem

Cosmic Inflation Starobinskiǐ 1979; Guth 1981; Linde 1982; Albrecht and Steinhardt 1982 consists of a period of exponential expansion that must have happened right after the beginning of time (τ=0\tau=0). There are over a hundred versions and variations Liddle 1999; Weinberg 2008, but generically the model requires some hypothetical new scalar field (the inflaton) with
negligible kinetic energy (ω=−1\omega=-1) to dominate the very early universe. After expanding by a factor of e60e^{60}, inflation leaves the universe empty and we need a mechanism to stop inflation and create the matter and radiation that we observe today. This is called re-heating. All these components require some fine tuning and free parameters that are hard to test because the physics involved is beyond reach by experiments Dodelson 2003. However, Inflation solves some important mysteries that we do not know how to fix otherwise within Λ\LambdaCDM .

As mentioned below Equation (3),
the structures that we observe today were not in causal contact in the past. We say that they are super horizon scales. Structures that are larger than rHr_{H} cannot evolve because the time a perturbation takes to travel that distance is larger than the expansion time. How can these structures form if they were not in causal contact? This is the horizon problem.
A clear evidence of this problem is the uniform CMB temperature across the full sky. The Hubble horizon rHr_{H} at CMB times only subtends about one degree in our sky. So causality cannot explain the observed all sky CMB uniformity.
The horizon problem is solved by inflation because, during inflation, structures of all scales become a super horizon. After inflation ends, they re-enter the horizon (ω=−1\omega=-1). Moreover, reheating provides a very uniform temperature background at the end of inflation.

2.4 Structure Formation and Dark Matter

In 1992, NASA’s Cosmic Background Explorer (COBE) satellite detected temperature variations of very small relative amplitude δT≃10−5\delta_{T}\simeq 10^{-5} in the CMB Smoot and etal 1992.
We believe those were the seeds that grew under gravitational collapse
from to form stars, galaxies and the cosmic web that we observe today. However, where do the seeds come from? Models of Inflation propose that these seeds come from super horizon quantum fluctuations that were exponentially inflated during inflation. Inflation predicts a power law (almost scale invariant) spectrum of fluctuations which agrees with the shape measured by later CMB missions Spergel and etal 2003; Planck Collaboration 2020; Aiola and etal 2020; Dutcher and etal 2021 and clustering in Galaxy Surveys Efstathiou et al. 1990; Gaztanaga and Baugh 1998; Gawiser and Silk 1998; DES Collaboration 2022. However, inflation does not provide a specific prediction for δT≃10−5\delta_{T}\simeq 10^{-5}: it is just a free parameter of the model.

The measured δT≃10−5\delta_{T}\simeq 10^{-5} is too small to explain the observed structure in galaxy surveys today Efstathiou et al. 1992; Tegmark and Rees 1998. Some fix is needed: galaxy bias Fry and Gaztanaga 1993; Gaztañaga and Juszkiewicz 2001 or a Λ\Lambda term Efstathiou et al. 1990.
The shape of the spectrum of fluctuations (including the Baryon Acoustic Oscillations, BAO Eisenstein and Hu 1998; Eisenstein and etal 2005; Gaztañaga et al. 2009a; Gaztañaga et al. 2009b) in the CMB and Galaxy Surveys, also required another free component to agree with the Λ\LambdaCDM model. They require a new type of matter, that we called Cold Dark Matter (CDM Davis et al. 1985), which is not made of regular matter (baryons) and interacts very weakly with matter or radiation (thus the name). CDM needs to be about four times more abundant than regular matter: ΩC​D​M≃4​ΩB\Omega_{CDM}\simeq 4\Omega_{B}. Such CDM is also needed to understand the motion of galaxies in clusters Zwicky 1937, the galaxy rotational curves Rubin and Ford 1970, gravitational lensing Clowe et al. 2004, galaxy evolution Davis et al. 1985, cosmic flows Feldman and etal 2003 and structure in galaxy maps Efstathiou et al. 1990; Gaztanaga and Baugh 1998; Gawiser and Silk 1998; DES Collaboration 2022. Despite enormous observational efforts in the last 30yrs, such Dark Matter component has never been directly detected as a real particle or object Bertone et al. 2005; Profumo et al. 2019.

2.5 Cosmic Acceleration, Dark Energy and the Static Universe

Usually, cosmic acceleration is defined by the adimensional coefficient q≡(a¨/a)​H−2q\equiv(\ddot{a}/a)H^{-2}. Taking a derivative of Equation (3) we find q=−12​(1+3​ω)q=-\frac{1}{2}(1+3\omega). For regular matter we have ω>0\omega>0 so we expect the expansion to decelerate (q<0q<0) because of gravity. However, the latest concordant measurements from a Type Ia supernova (SN) Perlmutter and etal 1999; Riess and etal 1998, galaxy clustering and CMB all agree with an expansion that tends to ω=−1.03±0.03\omega=-1.03\pm 0.03 DES Collaboration 2022 or q≃1q\simeq 1 in our future.

Dark Energy (DE) was introduced Huterer and Turner 1999
to account for ω<0\omega<0. However, there is no fundamental understanding of what DE is or why ω≃−1\omega\simeq-1. A natural candidate for DE is the cosmological constant Λ\Lambda Einstein 1917; Weinberg 1989; Carroll et al. 1992; Peebles and Ratra 2003,
which has ω=−1\omega=-1 and can also be thought of as the ground state of a scalar field (the DE), similar to the Inflaton. Λ\Lambda can also be a fundamental constant in GR, but this has some other complications Weinberg 1989; Carroll et al. 1992; Peebles and Ratra 2003. Including DE in the Λ\LambdaCDM model is also needed to complete the energy budget for our Universe: 5% baryons (ΩB≃0.05\Omega_{B}\simeq 0.05), 25% Dark Matter (ΩD​M≃0.25\Omega_{DM}\simeq 0.25) and 70% DE (ΩΛ≃0.7\Omega_{\Lambda}\simeq 0.7), so that ΩB+ΩD​M+ΩΛ=1\Omega_{B}+\Omega_{DM}+\Omega_{\Lambda}=1, as needed. DE is also important for understanding the Integrated Sachs–Wolfe (ISW) effect Crittenden and Turok 1996; Fosalba et al. 2003; Seife 2003; Gaztañaga et al. 2006,
and to have a longer age estimate of 14 Gyr, which is needed both to account for
the oldest stars and to have more time for structures to grow from the small CMB seeds δT≃10−5\delta_{T}\simeq 10^{-5} to the amplitude (and shape) we observe today in Galaxy Maps Efstathiou et al. 1990; Efstathiou et al. 1992.

Note how q=1q=1 means H˙=0\dot{H}=0, so that HH becomes constant and all structures become super horizon and freeze, as in Inflation. In the physical or rest frame (see Section A.1) this corresponds to a static (deSitter) metric. We are used to repeating that the universe accelerates, but in the limit q⇒1q\Rightarrow 1
it is more physical to say that the universe becomes static, as proposed by Einstein Einstein 1917 when he introduced Λ\Lambda. This can be understood with the Twin paradox analogy of Especial Relativity to explain time dilation. Time happens slower for the comoving observer according to the physical observer at rest. In the limit of exponential expansion, time freezers and the expansion stops. Something that is static for the rest frame observer
happens at constant velocity (HH constant) for the comoving observer.

3.4 How Did We End up Inside a BH?

Our Universe must have collapsed to form a BH.
Before it collapsed, the density is so small that there are no interactions other than gravity. Even radiation escapes the cloud. The density is still very low when MM approaches its corresponding event horizon R=rS=2​G​MR=r_{S}=2GM, but the gravitational pull is still that of a BH. Radial comoving shells of matter are in free fall collapse, so they do not feel that pull. This is the Equivalence Principle.
So the collapse continuous pass R=rSR=r_{S} inside the BH.
We take τ∗\tau_{*} in Equation (3) to correspond to the time τB​H\tau_{BH} when rH=−rSr_{H}=-r_{S}, i.e., FLRW cloud formed a BH:

where we have used Equation (9) and ω≃0\omega\simeq 0 (the latest stages of the collapse could have ω≃1/3\omega\simeq 1/3, but they last a negligible time compare to matter domination).
Thus the BH forms 11 Gyr before τ=0\tau=0 (the Big Bang) or 25Gyr ago.
The cold collapse continued after the BH formation.
In the last stages of the collapse atoms could ionized and part of the energy could transform into heat. This could slow down the collapse. Figure 4 shows the numerical BHU solution using Equations (3) and (11).

3.5 The Big Crunch

As mentioned before, there is a region outside the Hubble Horizon R>r>rHR>r>r_{H} which is dynamically frozen (yellow shading in Figures 3 and 4). This is the source for super horizon perturbations, which can be observed today in the CMB temperature maps.
Any small irregularities δ≡Δ​ρ/ρ\delta\equiv\Delta\rho/\rho (such as the particle composition of the fluid) will grow under gravity. This is the so-called
gravitational instability. The growth of δ\delta can start early on within the FLRW cloud, well before τB​H\tau_{BH}.
The amplitude of δ\delta from gravitational instability is scale invariant Zel’Dovich 1970; Harrison 1970; Peebles and Yu 1970.
In the linear regime, δ\delta follows a damped harmonic oscillator equation whose solutions
 Bernardeau et al. 2002
are D+∝aD_{+}\propto a and D−∝a−3/2D_{-}\propto a^{-3/2}, which correspond to the growing and decaying mode during expansion.
In the collapsing phase the damping term has a negative sign and fluctuations grow faster with time because D−D_{-} is the growing mode when aa goes to zero.
It is therefore likely that galaxy, stars, planets or life could also form during the collapsing phase. The details might depend on the original FLRW cloud composition. As the cloud collapses and the background density increases, the structures will disappear inside a hot Big Crunch, but the largest scale density perturbations will become super horizon scales (freeze out) and survive the Big Bounce, as they correspond to variations of the background over scales that are causally disconnected.

3.6 The Big Bounce

The energy density ρ\rho in Equation (4) is the same everywhere inside RR. By τ≃−10−4\tau\simeq-10^{-4} seconds, ρ\rho approaches nuclear saturation (GeV) in Equation (7).
The radius of our Universe RR is close to the distance between Earth and the Sun. However,
the Hubble radius is only few Km (containing a few solar masses). So the physical situation is similar to the interior of a regular collapsing star. We conjecture that this leads to a Big Bounce because of the Pauli exclusion principle of Quantum Mechanics.
Neutron density is the highest cold density observed in nature. The collapse is halted by neutron degeneracy pressure, causing the implosion to rebound Baym and Pethick 1979.
If the neutron material is elastic enough Bera et al. 2020 the collapse could just bounce into an expansion, pretty much like a bouncing of a ball. However, if the expansion rate is too high, the collapse could also led to a supernova (SN) explosion.
Global rotation of the FLRW cloud, could slow down the expansion rate (see Appendix C) and play some role in the bounce.

Stars explode as supernovas (SN) either because of runaway nuclear reactions or because of gravitational core-collapse. Protons and neutrons combine and form neutrinos by electron capture. The gravitational potential energy Φ\Phi of the collapse is converted into a neutrino burst. Neutrinos are reabsorbed by the infalling layers producing an SN explosion.
For example, the Crab Nebula pulsar in Figure 5 is thought to be a core collapse supernova that exploded releasing a total energy of 1051−105210^{51}-10^{52} ergs in the explosion. This energy is very similar to the FLRW collapsed energy of a M⊙M_{\odot} star within rH≃30r_{H}\simeq 30Km. Recall that the collapse speed is cc for rHr_{H}, so this is also closed to the internal (or rest) energy in Einstein’s most famous equation: E=M⊙​c2E=M_{\odot}c^{2}.

The bounce is synchronized at different locations because the background density is the same everywhere in the FLRW cloud. The collapse energy is converted into expansion energy (H>0H>0). Neutron stars, small primordial BHs
(PBHs) or quark stars Itoh 1970 could result as remnants of the SN explosions and they could contribute to the Dark Matter that we see today Carr and Kühnel 2020. The bounce happens at times and energy densities which are many orders of magnitudes away from Inflation or Planck times (τ≃10−35\tau\simeq 10^{-35} s or 101910^{19} GeV). So Quantum Gravity is not needed to understand cosmic expansion and there is no monopole problem Guth 1981.
This idea needs to be worked out and simulated.
Cold nuclear matter at neutron density is a major unsolved problem in modern physics Özel and Freire 2016,
but a Big Bang from a Big Bounce seems more plausible that one that comes out of nothing.

3.7 The Horizon Problem

The farther back we observe an image in the sky, the older it is.
The Big Bang, if we could see it, corresponds to a very distant spherical shell in the sky, represented by large red circles in Figure 6. The furthest we can actually see is the CMB shell (dashed circle), which is quite close to τ=0\tau=0. This means that rHr_{H} (or corresponding comoving particle horizon χ\chi) subtends a very small angle in the sky. So no physical mechanism can create the uniform CMB temperature that we see across the full sky. The initial conditions in the Big Bang have to be uniform to start with. This is very unlikely if the Big Bang came out of nothing Guth 1981; Dyson et al. 2002. However, this is exactly what we expect if the Big Bang originates from a uniform Big Bounce. This provides a solution to the Horizon problem without Inflation.

After the Big Bang, the resulting radiation and plasma fluids cool down following the standard FLRW evolution (nucleosynthesis and CMB recombination). The Big Bounce has super horizon irregularities from the collapsing phase which re-enter the horizon rHr_{H} as the expansion proceeds. These are the seeds for new structures (BAO and galaxies) that grow under gravitational instability, as illustrated in Figure 5.
The key difference with Inflation is that in the BHU the spectrum of incoming fluctuations have a cutoff for scales larger than λ>2​R\lambda>2R (k<π/Rk<\pi/R), while Inflation is scale invariant in all scales. This results in an anomalous lack of the largest structures in the CMB sky temperature TT with respect to the predictions of Inflation. This particular CMB anomaly is well known but is often interpreted in different ways Hinshaw and etal 1996; Spergel and etal 2003; Gaztañaga et al. 2003; Efstathiou et al. 2010; Schwarz et al. 2016.

3.8 Dark Energy

During the expanding phase we need to include ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda} in the dynamics because the BH Event Horizon rSr_{S} forbids anything to escape. This appears in the action of GR as a Gibbons-Hawking-York (GHY) boundary term York 1972; Gibbons and Hawking 1977; Hawking and Horowitz 1996, which is equivalent to
a Λ\Lambda term when the evolution happens inside an expanding BH (see Appendix B). The measured Λ\Lambda term is the Event Horizon of our BHU in Equation (9). In the standard Big Bang model there is no reason for cosmic acceleration. A new exotic ingredient, Dark Energy (DE), has to be added to account for this new evidence. Moreover,
there is no fundamental understanding as to why the DE equation of state ω≡ρ/p\omega\equiv\rho/p should be so close to ω=−1\omega=-1 as found by the latest data compilations DES Collaboration 2022. This is instead the natural outcome of the BHU because ω=−1\omega=-1 corresponds to a constant BH Event Horizon rS=rΛr_{S}=r_{\Lambda}.
The expansion freezes and becomes static in the physical frame. For a comoving observer this looks like exponential inflation, but both pictures are equivalent Mitra 2012; Gaztanaga 2021. As in the standard cosmological model, it takes 1414 Gyrs to reach now (or H0H_{0}) from the Big Bounce. So a total of 25 Gyrs from the BH formation in Equation (12).

4 Discusion and Conclusions

Inflation is believed to solve the flatness problem: why our universe has a flat global topology (or geometry) k=0k=0?
However, given some matter content, GR can not give us its topology. This is a global property of spacetime that is either assumed or directly measured. The same applies to an intrinsic Λ\Lambda term.
Equations (2) and (11) in the BHU are also exact solutions in GR for k≠Λ≠0k\neq\Lambda\neq 0 by just replacing rH−2≡H2+k/a2+Λ/3r_{H}^{-2}\equiv H^{2}+k/a^{2}+\Lambda/3. However, we use k=Λ=0k=\Lambda=0 here because these are the values in empty space (for Minkowski metric) and there is no reason, within GR, that they should be different in the presence of matter.
So we believe there is no flatness or Λ\Lambda problem that needs to be solved. Such problems only arise when you include other considerations outside GR, for example when you try to explanin that the Big Bang could have emerge out of nothing or from Quantum Gravity. Which is not needed in the BHU.

The Λ\LambdaCDM model interprets cosmic acceleration as evidence for Dark Energy (or an intrinsic Λ\Lambda term). We have shown instead that this is an indication that we live inside a BH of mass given by Equation (9). Such interpretation provides a fundamental explanation to the meaning of Λ\Lambda (see also Appendix B).
This results in the BHU solution to GR (see Appendix A), which we have reproduced here in Equation (11) just using simple Newtonian physics.
The idea that the universe might be generated from the inside of a BH is not new and has extensive literature Gonzalez-Diaz 1981; Easson and Brandenberger 2001; Daghigh et al. 2000; Firouzjahi 2016; Oshita and Yokoyama 2018; Dymnikova 2019 which mostly focused in deSitter metric for the BH interior. The formation mechanisms involve some modifications or extensions of GR, often motivated by Quantum Gravity or String Theory.
The BHU solution is also similar to the Bubble Universe and gravastar solutions Blau et al. 1987; Frolov et al. 1989; Aguirre and Johnson 2005; Mazur and Mottola 2015; Garriga et al. 2016; Kusenko 2020. However, there are no surface terms (or Bubble) in the BHU and there is regular matter and radiation inside (see Appendix A). Several authors before have proposed that the FLRW metric could be the interior of a BH Pathria 1972; Good 1972; Popławski 2016; Zhang 2018; Smolin 1997. However, these solutions were incompleted Knutsen 2009 or outside GR. Stuckey Stuckey 1994 showed that a dust filled FLRW metric can be joined to an outside BH metric. This is an independent precursor to the BHU model. Note that the BHU is located within a larger spacetime and is only homogeneous inside the event horizon. We do not know much about the larger spacetime, but it is in principle possible to observe light and matter that comes from outside.

How did our Universe end up inside a BH? If it collapsed to form one, why is it expanding now? As illustrated in Figure 4, the BHU has a mathematical singularity at τ=0\tau=0. As we approach that singularity, causal regions become small so the physics involved is similar to that of stars.
In nature we have never observed stable cold matter with densities larger than that of an atomic nuclei.
We propose here that when the collapsed reaches nuclear saturation density it stops, explodes and bounces back, like a supernova. This is due to the
same neutron degeneracy pressure that occurs in NS and atomic nuclei.
The time before τ=0\tau=0 represents a causal horizon which divides the BHU into smaller solar mass regions that explode and bounce in sync as long as the density is the same. Super-horizon perturbations, produced during the collapse, will be sync out of phase and generate some irregularities.
Further work is needed to understand the details and conditions for such Big Bounce to happen and to estimate the perturbations and composition and fraction of compact and difuse renmants that resulted from the SN explosions.

This provides a uniform start for the Big Bang, solving the horizon problem. The yellow shaded regions in Figure 3-4 show that the mass that collapsed into our BHU moved outside the horizon rHr_{H}.
super-horizon perturbations could seed structure (BAO and galaxies) as they re-enter rHr_{H} during expansion. The main differences with Inflation is the origin of those perturbations and the existence of a cutoff in the spectrum of fluctuations given by R⁡(τ)R(\tau). As illustrated in Figure 6-7, such cutoff has recently been measured in the CMB maps Fosalba and Gaztañaga 2021; Camacho-Quevedo and Gaztañaga 2021. Current and future galaxy surveys are also able to measure this signal Gaztanaga and Baugh 1998; Barriga et al. 2001 which
could also appear as a dipole Secrest and etal 2021. The existence of super horizon perturbations could also be related to the tension in measurements of cosmological parameters from different cosmic scaletimes Riess 2019; Di Valentino and etal 2021; Castelvecchi 2019 which have similar variations in cosmological parameters Camacho-Quevedo and Gaztañaga 2021.
The Big Bounce could also help us understand two remaining mysteries in the Λ\LambdaCDM paradigm: the origin for the amplitude δT≃10−5\delta_{T}\simeq 10^{-5} in the CMB and the nature of Dark Matter. Structure formation during the collapse and bounce could be key to understand them. Compact remnants such as BHs or neutron could also be detected and account for all Dark Matter.

A.3 Junction Conditions

We can arrive at the same BHU solution using Israel’s junction conditions ( Israel 1967; Barrabès and Israel 1991). We can combine two solutions with different energy content, as in Equation (A.2), to find a new solution. To do that we need to find
a hypersurface junction Σ\Sigma to match them well. In our case, this will be given by RR. The junction conditions require that the metric and its derivative (the extrinsic curvature KK) match at Σ\Sigma. The join metric then provides a new solution to GR. In many cases, like in the Bubble Universes or gravastar Blau et al. 1987; Frolov et al. 1989; Aguirre and Johnson 2005; Mazur and Mottola 2015; Garriga et al. 2016; Kusenko 2020, which match dS and SW metric, this does not work and the junction requires a surface term (the bubble) to glue both solutions together.
For the BHU there are no surface terms Gaztanaga 2021, which shows that this is an exact solution. In the limit where the FLRW has constant HH (i.e., our future), the BHU solution corresponds to match between dS and SW metric. So a Bubble Universe without bubble.

Appendix B The Action of GR and the Λ\Lambda term

Consider the Einstein–Hilbert action ( Hilbert 1915; Padmanabhan 2010):

S=∫V4d​V4​[R−2​Λ16​π​G+ℒ],S=\int_{V_{4}}d{V_{4}}\left[\frac{R-2\Lambda}{16\pi G}+{\cal L}\right],

where d​V4=−g​d4​xd{V_{4}}=\sqrt{-g}d^{4}x is the invariant volume element, V4{V_{4}} is the volume of the 4D spacetime manifold, R=Rμμ=gμ​ν​Rμ​νR=R^{\mu}_{\mu}=g^{\mu\nu}R_{\mu\nu} is the Ricci scalar curvature and ℒ{\cal L} the Lagrangian of the energy-matter content. We can obtain Einstein’s field equations (EFE) for the metric field gμ​νg_{\mu\nu} from this action by requiring SS to be stationary δ​S=0\delta S=0 under arbitrary variations of the metric δ​gμ​ν\delta g^{\mu\nu}. The solution is ( Einstein 1916; Padmanabhan 2010):

Gμ​ν+Λ​gμ​ν=8​π​G​Tμ​ν≡−16​π​G−g​δ⁡(−g​ℒ)δ​gμ​ν,G_{\mu\nu}+\Lambda g_{\mu\nu}=8\pi G\penalty\ T_{\mu\nu}\equiv-\frac{16\pi G}{\sqrt{-g}}\frac{\delta(\sqrt{-g}{\cal L})}{\delta g^{\mu\nu}},

(37)

where Gμ​ν≡Rμ​ν−12​gμ​ν​RG_{\mu\nu}\equiv R_{\mu\nu}-\frac{1}{2}g_{\mu\nu}R.
For perfect fluid in spherical coordinates:

Tμ​ν=(ρ+p)​uμ​uν+p​gμ​ν,T_{\mu\nu}=(\rho+p)u_{\mu}u_{\nu}+pg_{\mu\nu},

(38)

where ρ\rho,
and pp are the energy-matter density and pressure.
This fluid can be made of several components, each with a different equation of state p=ω​ρp=\omega\rho.

Equation (37) requires that boundary terms vanish (e.g. see Landau and Lifshitz 1971; Padmanabhan 2010). If there are boundaries to the dynamic equations, we need to add a Gibbons-Hawking-York (GHY) boundary term York 1972; Gibbons and Hawking 1977; Hawking and Horowitz 1996 to the action in Equation (36):

(39)

so that the total action is S+SG​H​YS+S_{GHY} and KK is the trace of the extrinsic curvature at the boundary ∂V4\partial{V_{4}} and hh is the induced metric.
The expansion that happens inside an isolated BH is bounded by its event horizon r<rSr<r_{S} and we need to add the GHY boundary term SG​H​YS_{GHY}.
The integral is over the induced metric at ∂V4{\partial{V_{4}}}, which for a time-like junction d​χ=0d\chi=0
corresponds to R=rSR=r_{S}:

d​s∂V42=hα​β​d​yα​d​yβ=−d​τ2+rS2​d​Ω2.ds^{2}_{{\partial{V_{4}}}}=h_{\alpha\beta}dy^{\alpha}dy^{\beta}=-d\tau^{2}+r_{S}^{2}{d\Omega}^{2}.

(40)

So the only remaining degrees of freedom in the action are time τ\tau and the angular coordinates.
We can use this metric and the trace of the extrinsic curvature at R=rSR=r_{S}
to estimate K=−2/rSK=-2/r_{S} from Equation (34).
This result is also valid for a null geodesic Gaztanaga 2021.
We then have:

SΛ=−ΛV4/(8πG)=−rS3Λτ/3G,S_{\Lambda}=-\Lambda{V_{4}}/(8\pi G)=-r_{S}^{3}\Lambda\tau/3G,
where we have estimated the total 4D volume V4{V_{4}} as that bounded by ∂V4\partial{V_{4}} inside r<rSr<r_{S}. i.e.,: V4=2​V3​τ{V_{4}}=2V_{3}\tau, where the factor 2 accounts for the fact that V3=4​π​rS3/3V_{3}=4\pi r_{S}^{3}/3 is covered twice, first during collapse and again during expansion.

Such event horizon becomes a boundary for outgoing geodesics, i.e., expanding solutions.

Appendix C Outside Our BHU: A Rotating Cloud

If the FLRW cloud is not totally isolated it could have some rotation. This could be a way to infer if there is something outside our BHU. Any rotation, no matter how small, could prevent or interfere with the cloud collapse. Can we detect such rotation? A rotating BH is a bit more difficult to model because spherical symmetry is lost and the BH becomes oblate (i.e., the Kerr metric Kerr 1963):

x=r2+rJ2​sin⁡θ​cos⁡Φ;y=r2+rJ2​sin⁡θ​cos⁡Φ;z=r​cos⁡θ,x=\sqrt{r^{2}+r_{J}^{2}}\sin{\theta}\cos{\Phi}\,\,\,\,;\,\,\,\,y=\sqrt{r^{2}+r_{J}^{2}}\sin{\theta}\cos{\Phi}\,\,\,\,;\,\,\,\,z=r\cos{\theta},

(42)

where rJ=J/Mr_{J}=J/M is the ratio between the angular momentum JJ and the BH mass. A detailed analysis of this case is outside the scope of this review, but we will make some energy considerations to understand the possible impact of such rotation on the Big Bounce.
We assume that both mass MM and angular momentum JJ are conserved, so rJr_{J} is constant.
We also assume that rJ≪rSr_{J}\ll r_{S} so during the collapse we can neglect deviations from spherical symmetry. If we start from the FLRW cloud of size RR and mass MM with some small initial rotation, θ˙\dot{\theta}, these products have to be constant:

JM=rJ=R2​θ˙=rS2​θ˙B​H.\frac{J}{M}=r_{J}=R^{2}\dot{\theta}=r_{S}^{2}\dot{\theta}_{BH}.

(43)

As RR gets smaller, θ˙\dot{\theta} will become larger.
The kinetic energy term in Equation (1) will have another contribution 2​K=R˙2+θ˙2​R22K=\dot{R}^{2}+\dot{\theta}^{2}R^{2}, so that Equation (2) becomes:

rH−2≡H2​(τ)=8​π​G3​ρ​(τ)−rJ2R4=rS−2​(aaB​H)−3​(1+ω)−rJ2rS4​(aaB​H)−4​(1+ω),r_{H}^{-2}\equiv H^{2}(\tau)=\frac{8\pi G}{3}\rho(\tau)-\frac{r_{J}^{2}}{R^{4}}=r_{S}^{-2}\left(\frac{a}{a_{BH}}\right)^{-3(1+\omega)}-\frac{r_{J}^{2}}{r_{S}^{4}}\left(\frac{a}{a_{BH}}\right)^{-4(1+\omega)},

(44)

where in the last step we have used Equations (11) and (3)
for a collapsing FLRW cloud with equation of state ω\omega. So, for ω=0\omega=0, rotation acts like a radiation term of negative energy density. Rotation is negligible, except
when a⇒0a\Rightarrow 0 when rotation tends to delay the collapse, as it reduces the expansion rate HH. Unless angular momentum is lost some other way, the rotation component will dominate (stop the collapse) for:

rJ≃rS​(aaB​H)(1+ω)/2.r_{J}\simeq r_{S}\left(\frac{a}{a_{BH}}\right)^{(1+\omega)/2}.

(45)

Close to the Big Bounce, if radiation dominates (ω=1/3\omega=1/3) with neutron energy densities (GeV), we have a≃10−12​aB​Ha\simeq 10^{-12}a_{BH}. So the condition for the rotation not to interfere with the collapse is:

rJ≪10−8​rS.r_{J}\ll 10^{-8}r_{S}.

(46)

Equivalently, as rS≃H0−1r_{S}\simeq H_{0}^{-1}, see Equation (9), θ˙B​H\dot{\theta}_{BH} in Equation (43) has to be:

θ˙B​H≪10−8​H0,\dot{\theta}_{BH}\ll 10^{-8}H_{0},

(47)

so less than 10−810^{-8} cycles per Hubble time.
Such a small contribution is undetectable in today’s expansion law: ΩJ≃10−16\Omega_{J}\simeq 10^{-16} in Equation (44), or during recombination, but it could be bounded using nucleosynthesis or by its affects on the Big Bounce.
