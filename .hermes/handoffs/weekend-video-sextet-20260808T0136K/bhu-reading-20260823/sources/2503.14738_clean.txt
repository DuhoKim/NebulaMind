Abstract
 
We present baryon acoustic oscillation (BAO) measurements from more than 14 million galaxies and quasars drawn from the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI) Data Release 2 (DR2), based on three years of operation. For cosmology inference, these galaxy measurements are combined with DESI Lyman-α\alpha forest BAO results presented in a companion paper. The DR2 BAO results are consistent with DESI DR1 and SDSS, and their distance-redshift relationship matches those from recent compilations of supernovae (SNe) over the same redshift range. The results are well described by a flat Λ\LambdaCDM model, but the parameters preferred by BAO are in mild, 2.3​σ2.3\sigma tension with those determined from the cosmic microwave background (CMB), although the DESI results are consistent with the acoustic angular scale θ∗\theta_{*} that is well-measured by Planck. This tension is alleviated by dark energy with a time-evolving equation of state parametrized by w0w_{0} and waw_{a}, which provides a better fit to the data, with a favored solution in the quadrant with w0>−1w_{0}>-1 and wa<0w_{a}<0. This solution is preferred over Λ\LambdaCDM at 3.1​σ3.1\sigma for the combination of DESI BAO and CMB data. When also including SNe, the preference for a dynamical dark energy model over Λ\LambdaCDM ranges from 2.8−4.2​σ2.8-4.2\sigma depending on which SNe sample is used. We present evidence from other data combinations which also favor the same behavior at high significance. From the combination of DESI and CMB we derive 95% upper limits on the sum of neutrino masses, finding ∑mν<0.064\sum m_{\nu}<0.064 eV assuming Λ\LambdaCDM and ∑mν<0.16\sum m_{\nu}<0.16 eV in the w0​waw_{0}w_{a} model. Unless there is an unknown systematic error associated with one or more datasets, it is clear that Λ\LambdaCDM is being challenged by the combination of DESI BAO with other measurements and that dynamical dark energy offers a possible solution.

I Introduction

Cosmic acceleration remains the most pressing problem in contemporary cosmology, implying a pervasive new form of energy with exotic physical properties, or a breakdown of Einstein gravity on cosmological scales, or perhaps both. To probe the physics of acceleration, cosmologists seek to measure the history of cosmic expansion and the history of gravitational clustering with the greatest achievable precision over a wide span of redshift. Baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO) provide a powerful tool for measuring the expansion history, using a characteristic scale that is imprinted on matter clustering by pressure waves that propagate in the coupled baryon-photon fluid of the pre-recombination Universe Eisenstein et al. 1998; Blake and Glazebrook 2003; Seo and Eisenstein 2003. This paper examines the cosmological implications of the BAO measurements from the second data release (DR2) of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument (DESI, Levi et al. 2013; DESI Collaboration et al. 2016a; DESI Collaboration et al. 2022), consisting of data from the first three years of operation.

Since the first clear detections of BAO in the Sloan Digital Sky Survey and the Two-Degree Field Galaxy Redshift Survey Eisenstein et al. 2005; Cole et al. 2005, BAO measurements have played a central role in observational cosmology. The key technical requirement is a large-volume spectroscopic survey with sufficient sampling density, and previous surveys designed with BAO measurements as a defining goal include WiggleZ Blake et al. 2011, the Baryon Oscillation Spectroscopic Survey (BOSS) Dawson et al. 2013 of SDSS-III Eisenstein et al. 2011, and its extension eBOSS Dawson et al. 2016 in SDSS-IV Blanton et al. 2017. In addition to galaxy and quasar redshifts, BOSS and eBOSS measured BAO in the Lyα\alpha forest absorption spectra of z>2z>2 quasars, an approach first proposed by White 2003; McDonald and Eisenstein 2007. Transverse BAO can also be measured in photometric surveys (e.g., Abbott et al. 2024), though the precision obtained for a given number of tracers is much higher with spectroscopic redshifts. DESI is designed specifically to enable a spectroscopic BAO survey of unprecedented power and efficiency DESI Collaboration et al. 2016b, as shown in its survey validation DESI Collaboration et al. 2024a based on the early data release DESI Collaboration et al. 2024b. In its first year of observations DESI Collaboration et al. 2025a, DESI already achieved BAO measurements competitive with those of all previous surveys combined DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c; DESI Collaboration et al. 2025b. Now, with redshifts of more than 30 million galaxies and quasars, and Lyα\alpha forest spectra of more than 820,000 quasars, the DESI DR2 sample is by far the largest spectroscopic galaxy sample to date.

Mayall Telescope at the Kitt Peak National Observatory. DESI collects the light for 5000 spectra per observation with a robotic focal plane assembly Silber et al. 2023 that can quickly align the positions of fiber optics cables Poppett et al. 2024 across the seven square degree field of view of the prime focus corrector Miller et al. 2024.
For each observation, there is a custom focal plane configuration or ‘tile’ that defines the DESI ‘target’ Myers et al. 2023 associated with each robotic positioner. When repeated observations of a tile are required to obtain the minimum effective observing time Schlafly et al. 2023, these maintain the same configuration. The light of each of these targets, along with calibration stars and sky spectra, are recorded from 360–980 nm with ten bench-mounted spectrographs that are located in a climate-controlled enclosure. This configuration helps to enable the superb wavelength and flux calibration of the DESI data.

The DESI main survey started observations on 14 May 2021 after a period of survey validation DESI Collaboration et al. 2024a. This paper presents the analysis of the main survey data that will be released with the second data release or DR2, which includes observations through 9 April 2024. The DESI spectroscopic reduction Guy et al. 2023 and redshift estimation (Redrock Bailey et al. 2025; Anand et al. 2024) pipelines were applied to the DR2 dataset in a homogeneous processing run denoted as ‘Kibo’. An error in the processing involved in the co-addition of spectra from separate exposures was subsequently identified and fixed, and the pipeline was rerun and denoted ‘Loa’. Approximately 0.1% of the measured redshifts change significantly between Kibo and Loa. The LSS catalogs for DR2 BAO measurements were produced for both Kibo and Loa. Decisions that affect the masking of the data (described further below) were made using the Kibo data and were not reconsidered with Loa. Both datasets will be released with DR2.

The DESI survey has two main observing programs, ‘bright’ and ‘dark,’ that are defined based on the nighttime sky conditions, and each of these programs has distinct target classes Schlafly et al. 2023; Hahn et al. 2023; Zhou et al. 2023; Raichoor et al. 2023; Chaussidon et al. 2023; Myers et al. 2023. The extragalactic sample for the bright program is the ‘bright galaxy sample’ (BGS).22
 2
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 There are also secondary targets that are observed at lower priority Myers et al. 2023. Luminous red galaxies (LRGs), emission line galaxies (ELGs), and quasars (QSOs) are all observed during dark conditions. Dark- and bright-time targets are processed separately. At z<2.1z<2.1, we measure BAO with the autocorrelation of the confirmed members of each target class, processed through ‘large-scale structure’ (LSS) catalogs as described in section II.1. At higher redshifts, we measure the auto-correlation of the Lyα\alpha forest absorption in the spectra of quasars and the cross-correlation of the forest absorption with quasar positions. The data samples used for these Lyα\alpha measurements are described further in section II.2. The analysis of the Lyα\alpha data and BAO results are presented in the companion key paper DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d. DR2 contains 6671 dark and 5171 bright tiles. These are respectively 2.4 times and 2.3 times the number released in DR1.

• 

The bad fiber list has been updated, applying the same methods as for DR1 Krolewski et al. 2025, but using the Kibo redshift results.

•

The area of the BGS sample has grown by 65% compared to DR1, and this is simply due to the footprint of the newly observed tiles.

The BGS sample we use is nearly 4×\times the size of the DR1 BGS sample used for BAO measurements. The increased area and completeness account for half of this increase. The rest of the increase is due to a relaxation of the absolute magnitude cut. First, we changed the definition of the absolute magnitudes used for the cut. In this analysis, we do not apply any kk or ee corrections and we instead determine the absolute rr-band magnitudes, MrM_{r} purely based on the apparent magnitude of the galaxy and the distance modulus to its redshift. Based on this new definition, we also changed the absolute magnitude cut to the value at which no galaxies would be removed from the sample at its maximum redshift of 0.4. This was determined to be Mr<−21.35M_{r}<-21.35, whereas the cut applied in DR1 was Mr<−21.5M_{r}<-21.5.

Prior to producing the results presented in this paper, the DESI BAO analysis team further processed the LSS catalogs to blind the results and apply BAO reconstruction. The blinding was removed only after the analysis choices were validated and fixed. We provide more detail on each process below.

II.1.1 Blinding

To minimize the risk of confirmation bias in our analysis, we used a blinding scheme to deliberately conceal the true position of the BAO peak observed from the data until all choices about our inference pipeline were finalized. This blinding was applied at the level of the LSS catalogs, using the same prescription as was applied to DR1 and detailed in Andrade et al. 2025b, but with a new random seed to produce unknown (but controlled) shifts to galaxy redshifts that conceal the true BAO peak position. A number of validation tests of both the data and the analysis pipeline were performed on the blinded data catalogs, as described in Andrade et al. 2025a. Once these tests were passed and the pipeline frozen, the blinding was removed and true catalogs were first processed during the DESI winter collaboration meeting in December 2024.

II.1.2 Reconstruction

Non-linear gravitational evolution induces large-scale bulk flows that can degrade the precision and accuracy of BAO measurements.
Density field reconstruction (Eisenstein et al. 2007) attempts to correct for this effect by estimating the displacement field from the observed galaxy distribution, using it to undo the gravitational flow and partially restore the acoustic peak to its linear regime shape. We ‘reconstructed’ the LSS catalogs using the IterativeFFT reconstruction algorithm (Burden et al. 2015) implemented in pyrecon44
 4
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 https://github.com/cosmodesi/pyrecon and using the fiducial DR1 settings from Paillas et al. 2025. This decision was informed by an extensive comparison of different reconstruction algorithms in the context of DESI (Chen et al. 2024a).
The reconstructed catalogs are used for the clustering and associated BAO measurements from galaxies and quasars presented throughout the rest of this paper.

II.2 Lyman-α\alpha forest catalog

The DESI DR2 quasar catalog for the Lyα\alpha forest analysis was constructed in a similar manner to the DR1 catalog described in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025b. The catalog contains high-redshift quasars and is built from the outputs of three automatic classifiers that analyze all quasar targets: the Redrock redshift estimator (Bailey et al. 2025) that classifies based on templates, including quasar templates optimized for DESI quasars (Brodzeller et al. 2023; P. Martini et al. 2025), a Mg II afterburner that searches for broad Mg II emission in quasar candidates classified as galaxies, and the QuasarNet neural network classifier developed by (Busca and Balland 2018) that has been updated D. Green et al. 2025 for DESI. The catalog also contains any other targets that are classified by Redrock as high-redshift quasars.

We also catalog two types of absorption systems that are masked by our analysis pipeline: Damped Lyα\alpha Absorption Systems (DLAs) and Broad Absorption Line (BAL) quasars. DLAs are systems with high (NH​I>2×1020N_{\mathrm{H\,I}}>2\times 10^{20} cm-2) column densities of neutral hydrogen in the intergalactic medium (IGM). These systems have somewhat higher clustering than typical Lyα\alpha forest absorption and have very broad damping wings that can compromise a significant fraction of a forest spectrum. We use three analysis tools to identify DLAs in the DR2 dataset; the performance of these methods are described in Brodzeller et al. 2025. Approximately 20% of the QSOs in the Lyα\alpha forest sample are BAL quasars. These absorption systems contaminate the forest by adding absorption that is uncorrelated with the large-scale structure of the IGM. We identify BALs with the method described in P. Martini et al. 2025. Further details about the quasar catalog for the Lyα\alpha forest are described in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d.

The DR2 quasar catalog has over 1.2 million quasars at z>1.77z>1.77, including over 820,000 quasars at z>2.09z>2.09. We use Lyα\alpha forests from the spectra of quasars at z>2.09z>2.09, while quasars at z>1.77z>1.77 are used as discrete tracers in the computation of the cross-correlation with Lyα\alpha. Both samples are close to a factor of two larger than the DR1 sample, which had 420,000 quasars at z>2.09z>2.09 and over 700,000 quasars at z>1.77z>1.77. In addition to the larger sample size, the average signal-to-noise per pixel is higher, because 67% of DR2 quasars have had at least two observations (out of the four planned for the Lyα\alpha quasar sample), while only 43% of DR1 quasars had been observed more than once.

III BAO Measurements

III.1 Clustering from Galaxies and Quasars

Our baseline BAO measurements from galaxies and quasars are derived from the two-point correlation function (2PCF) in redshift space. We measure it using the Landy-Szalay estimator (Landy and Szalay 1993) (adapted to post-reconstruction measurements, as in Padmanabhan et al. 2012) using pycorr,55
 5
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 https://github.com/cosmodesi/pycorr/ which implements a wrapper around a modified version of the Corrfunc pair-counting code (Sinha and Garrison 2020). We bin the correlation function in ss and μ=cos⁡θ\mu=\cos\theta, where ss is the redshift-space scalar separation and θ\theta is the angle between the galaxy pair and the line of sight, using ss-bins of 4​h−1​Mpc4\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc} width and 200 μ\mu-bins in [−1,1][-1,1]. We then decompose it into Legendre multipoles, focusing on the monopole and quadrupole moments for the analysis.

We estimate the errors of the correlation function measurements using the RascalC semi-analytical covariance matrix code66
 6
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 https://github.com/oliverphilcox/RascalC O’Connell et al. 2016; O’Connell and Eisenstein 2019; Philcox et al. 2020; Philcox and Eisenstein 2019; Rashkovetskyi et al. 2023, in the same way as for the DESI DR1 BAO analysis DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c. The computation gives the contributions of the measured (non-linear) two-point correlation function (including the disconnected four-point function) with survey geometry and selection effects, and shot-noise rescaling calibrated with jackknife resampling to account for missing non-Gaussian contributions. This procedure has been validated using DESI DR1 mocks in Rashkovetskyi et al. 2025, which also provides a more complete description of the algorithm. In general, covariance estimation for DR2 is less challenging than DR1 (e.g., the footprint is less complex and the samples have higher completeness). The DESI DR2 covariance pipeline is publicly available on GitHub.77
 7
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 https://github.com/cosmodesi/RascalC-scripts/tree/DESI-DR2-BAO/DESI/Y3/post

The BAO fitting procedure—described in detail in DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c—involves the use of a template of the correlation function multipoles in a fiducial cosmology (which are converted from Fourier space templates of the power spectrum, Chen et al. 2024b). During the parameter posterior sampling, the BAO features in these templates are shifted with respect to those seen in the data, and the amount of shifting is regulated by scaling parameters that are varied freely during the fit. The scaling parameters that shift the BAO features along and across the line of sight are related to the cosmology by

The templates are also allowed to vary in amplitude and are combined with a set of nuisance parameters that model the broadband shape of the correlation function. The reported BAO constraints are marginalized over these nuisance parameters so that the information coming from the position of the acoustic feature can be isolated. The BAO model and inference pipeline are largely the same as used for DR1 and described in DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c, with a few small modifications:

• 

Scale cut (smins_{\text{min}}): for the DR1 analysis, the minimum scale used in the BAO fits was smin=50​h−1​Mpcs_{\text{min}}=50~\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}. Reference Chen et al. 2024b showed through tests on mock catalogs that the recovered values of αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}} and αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} and their errors are very stable against changes to the minimum scale in the range 50​h−1​Mpc≲smin≲80​h−1​Mpc50\;\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}\lesssim s_{\rm min}\lesssim 80\;\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}. However, when fitting to the blinded DR2 data we found somewhat large χ2\chi^{2} values when using smin=50​h−1​Mpcs_{\rm min}=50\;\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc} for some tracers and redshift bins, which improved significantly when changing the scale cuts to smin=60​h−1​Mpcs_{\rm min}=60\;\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc} (with only very minor effects on the recovered α\alpha values). To guard against the possibility that this effect in the blinded data was due to some unknown systematic affecting the 2PCF in the range 50<s<60​h−1​Mpc50<s<60\;\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc}, we chose to set smin=60​h−1​Mpcs_{\text{min}}=60~\,h^{-1}{\rm Mpc} as our baseline for our BAO fits before unblinding.

• 

2D fits for ELG1 and QSO:
for tracers with a sufficiently large signal-to-noise ratio in the 2PCF, αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}} and αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} can be simultaneously determined, using anisotropic clustering information by fitting both the monopole and quadrupole moments of the correlation function (a ‘2D fit’). However, when the signal-to-noise of the quadrupole measurement is lower, robust determination of αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} is harder. In these cases, we only fit the monopole, which depends on αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}}, to avoid the complications of including a weak non-Gaussian constraint on αAP\alpha_{\rm AP}.
For the DR1 analysis, our baseline analysis for BGS, ELG1, and QSO used only the monopole for BAO fits. In DR2, the signal-to-noise ratio of the ELG1 and QSO samples has increased sufficiently to promote them to 2D fits for both αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}} and αAP\alpha_{\rm AP}, while for BGS we continue to use the monopole only to measure αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}}.88
 8
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 At the low effective redshift zeff=0.295z_{\rm eff}=0.295 of the BGS sample, measurement of αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} also carries very little cosmological information. This decision was made by verifying the stability of the constraints when fitting the blinded data and mock galaxy catalogs Andrade et al. 2025a.

• 

Systematic errors: the systematic error treatment in the DESI DR2 analysis incorporates refinements to improve the robustness and accuracy of the results. Fiducial cosmology-related systematic errors were adjusted, increasing the systematic uncertainty on αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} from 0.1% in DR1 to 0.18%, based on including an evolving dark energy model motivated by the DR1 results in the test Pérez-Fernández et al. 2025. Systematics related to unknown details of the small-scale galaxy-halo connection, which dominated the DR1 error budget Mena-Fernández et al. 2025; Garcia-Quintero et al. 2025, were refined to be tracer-specific. The contributions for LRG, ELG, QSO, and BGS tracers were updated to better capture effects dependent on redshift and galaxy type. The details of these choices are presented in Andrade et al. 2025a. The systematic error in αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}} increases the total error budget in this parameter by a fractional amount between 1%1\% (for BGS) and 9%9\% (LRG3++ELG1), while for αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} the increase ranges between 0.1%0.1\% (QSO) and 2%2\% (LRG3++ELG1). Thus, in all cases, our error budget remains dominated by statistical errors associated with finite survey volume and sampling density. Additionally, we assessed the impact of including theoretically motivated systematic correlations across redshift bins and found no significant effect on our main results.

The updated methods, systematic error estimates and the range of validation tests performed on blinded data before the pipeline was frozen are described in detail in the supporting publication Andrade et al. 2025a.

III.2 Clustering from the Lyman-α\alpha Forest

The DR2 Lyα\alpha forest BAO measurement DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d is based on the combination of four correlation functions: the auto-correlation function of the Lyα\alpha forest in region A (1040−12051040-1205 Å; Lyα\alpha(A)×\timesLyα\alpha(A)), the auto-correlation of the Lyα\alpha forest in region A with region B (920−1020920-1020 Å; Lyα\alpha(A)×\timesLyα\alpha(B)), the cross-correlation of the forest in region A with quasars (Lyα\alpha(A)×\timesQSO), and of region B with quasars (Lyα\alpha(B)×\timesQSO). We measure the Lyα\alpha forest in these two distinct regions because region B has additional astrophysical noise due to higher-order Lyman series absorption, and the spectra of region B are generally lower signal-to-noise ratio because this region is only observed in higher-redshift quasars. We do not measure the auto-correlation of region B nor the auto-correlation of the quasars because these have even lower signal-to-noise ratio. For regions A and B we measure the forest absorption relative to an estimate of the forest continuum with the picca code.99
 9
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 https://github.com/igmhub/picca This code fits a mean continuum model plus two diversity parameters for each quasar to derive the over or under-density of the forest absorption.

We measure these four correlation functions with the picca analysis code, along with the covariance and cross-covariance of the four correlation functions. The correlations are calculated from 0−200​h−10-200\,h^{-1} Mpc in 4​h−14\,h^{-1} Mpc bins in r∥r_{\|} and r⟂r_{\perp} that correspond to comoving distances along and transverse to the line of sight, respectively. We fit the correlation functions with the Vega package1010
 10
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 https://github.com/andreicuceu/vega to measure the two BAO parameters (α∥,α⟂\alpha_{\|},\alpha_{\perp}) and 15 nuisance parameters that include the Lyα\alpha forest bias, the Lyα\alpha redshift-space distortion (RSD) parameter, quasar bias, the biases of various metals in the IGM, and several others. All four correlation functions are fit in 2D from 30<r<180​h−130<r<180\,h^{-1} Mpc. The fit has 9306 data points and 17 parameters. Further details are given in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d and references therein. Figure 5 shows the Lyα\alpha autocorrelation function and the cross-correlation with quasars in the bottom right panel. This panel compresses these 2D measurements into a single correlation, while the analysis in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d uses the whole 2D information without this compression.

We finalized the Lyα\alpha forest analysis choices with blinded data and mock datasets before we measured the BAO parameters. Unlike for lower-redshift galaxies and quasars, the blinding procedure for the Lyα\alpha forest analysis shifts the BAO peak location in the correlation function (following the method developed for DR1, see DESI Collaboration et al. 2025b). Briefly, we calculated a correlation function model that sets nuisance parameters to the values in DR1 and then computed a blinding template with Vega for some shift Δ​α∥,Δ​α⟂\Delta\alpha_{\|},\Delta\alpha_{\perp} that our analysis pipeline (picca) applied to any calculation of the correlation function. The shift values were not stored anywhere, and instead we stored the random number seed and other information necessary to recover the shift. The virtue of blinding the correlation function, rather than the catalog-level blinding used at lower redshifts, is that it preserves the locations of features in the correlation function due to metal-line contamination, which otherwise would have revealed the magnitude and direction of the blinding.

There are two substantial improvements in the Lyα\alpha analysis relative to DESI DR1 DESI Collaboration et al. 2025b that we describe in the companion paper DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d and additional, important improvements that we describe in two supporting papers Casas et al. 2025; Brodzeller et al. 2025. The two substantial improvements are in how we account for the distortion of the correlation function due to the continuum fitting, and the modeling of metal contamination in the forest. Supporting paper Casas et al. 2025 describes improvements to the number and quality of mock catalogs used to validate our analysis pipeline. The most significant improvement in the mocks is the inclusion of non-linear broadening of the BAO peak. Another important improvement is the development of a new software package to identify DLAs based on template fitting, as well as a careful characterization of the purity and completeness of our DLA catalog with mock datasets. We now use the DLA template-fitting package, as well as two other codes based on a Convolutional Neural Network Wang et al. 2022 and a Gaussian Process method Ho et al. 2021, to produce the DLA catalog. The new template-fitting DLA finder, the performance of the three finders, and the construction of the final DLA catalog are described in another supporting paper Brodzeller et al. 2025. As shown in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d, the cumulative impact of all these improvements on the BAO results is very small.

The Lyα\alpha forest measures the BAO signal much better in the line-of-sight direction than in the transverse direction due to the large value of the Lyα\alpha RSD parameter. The optimal combination of α∥\alpha_{\|} and α⟂\alpha_{\perp} for the Lyα\alpha dataset is α∥0.55​α⟂0.45\alpha_{\|}^{0.55}\alpha_{\perp}^{0.45}, which differs from the best-measured αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}} quantity for the galaxy and quasar BAO in equation 12: we measure this optimal combination with a precision of 0.64% at an effective redshift of zeff=2.33z_{\rm eff}=2.33 with the DR2 Lyα\alpha sample, a significant improvement relative to even the 1.1% measurement from DESI DR1. Nevertheless, in this paper we still report Lyα\alpha BAO results in the conventional αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}}, αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} basis as for the other tracers.

Several recent theoretical studies have reported ∼3​σ\sim 3\sigma measurements of a shift in the BAO position in the Lyα\alpha forest due to non-linear evolution Sinigaglia et al. 2024; de Belsunce et al. 2024; Hadzhiyska et al. 2025. Each of these studies has various strengths and weaknesses, and do not agree on the magnitude and direction of the shift, so we have
adopted a theoretical systematic uncertainty of Δ​α∥=0.3%,Δ​α⟂=0.3%\Delta\alpha_{\|}=0.3\%,\,\Delta\alpha_{\perp}=0.3\%, which approximately encompasses the theoretical estimates. We add this systematic uncertainty to the statistical covariance matrix to obtain the total error budget. This systematic error reduces the precision of the isotropic BAO measurement to 0.7%. We discuss the theoretical studies and our choice of systematic error further in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d.

III.3.1 Internal consistency of DESI BAO measurements

As DR1 is a subset of DR2, and the data reduction and analysis pipelines are very similar, we conservatively assume a perfect correlation between the DR1 and DR2 measurements shown in figure 6 to assess their consistency. We find improvements in the statistical precision between 30% and 50% for DR2. We find that the largest discrepancies between results are 1.54​σ1.54\sigma in αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}} (for LRG3++ELG1) and 1.68​σ1.68\sigma in αAP\alpha_{\rm AP} (for LRG2), with the joint uncertainty defined as σ≡σDR2​NDR2/NDR1−1\sigma\equiv\sigma_{\rm DR2}\sqrt{N_{\rm DR2}/N_{\rm DR1}-1}, where NDR1N_{\rm DR1} and NDR2N_{\rm DR2} are the numbers of tracers in DR1 and DR2, respectively. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov (KS) statistic for the differences is 0.29 for 12 data points, corresponding to a pp-value of 0.22 and indicating high consistency between DR1 and DR2.

In order to check the internal consistency of the BAO measurements in different redshift bins, we interpret each within the flat Λ\LambdaCDM cosmological model, using the methods described below in section V. In this model, BAO measurements constrain the parameter Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} and the combination H0​rdH_{0}r_{\mathrm{d}}. figure 7 shows the results for each individual redshift bin and the combination: the posteriors all have significant overlap, indicating no internal inconsistency within a Λ\LambdaCDM model. The largest difference in the recovered parameters is 1.8​σ1.8\sigma (between LRG1 and LRG3++ELG1). The joint fit to all tracers returns a best-fit χ2/dof=10.2/(13−2)\chi^{2}/{\rm dof}=10.2/(13-2).

IV External Data

We use results from a number of external experiments together with our BAO measurements in order to obtain the most precise cosmological constraints. These are briefly described below.

IV.1 Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (BBN) prior on Ωb​h2\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}

Absent an external determination of rdr_{\mathrm{d}}, BAO measurements serve as an uncalibrated ruler and therefore measure the degenerate combination H0​rdH_{0}r_{\mathrm{d}} rather than H0H_{0} and rdr_{\mathrm{d}} individually. Given that BAO also measure Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}, from equation 2 we can see that, assuming standard neutrino content (Neff=3.044N_{\mathrm{eff}}=3.044 and ∑mν=0.06\sum m_{\nu}=0.06 eV), knowledge of Ωb​h2\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2} is sufficient to break this degeneracy. Careful observational determinations of the primordial deuterium abundance D/H Cooke et al. 2018 and the helium fraction YPY_{\rm P} Aver et al. 2022 in astrophysical systems can be connected to predictions from BBN for the abundances of D and 4He in the early Universe to determine the baryon-to-photon ratio and thus the physical baryon density Ωb​h2\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}, when combined with a measurement of the CMB temperature Workman et al. 2022a.

As in DR1, we use estimates of Ωb​h2\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2} determined by Schöneberg 2024 based on the PRyMordial code Burns et al. 2024 including marginalization over uncertainties in nuclear reaction rates. This corresponds to

Ωb​h2=0.02218±0.00055,\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}=0.02218\pm 0.00055,

(14)

in the standard Λ\LambdaCDM model, and

Ωb​h2=0.02196±0.00063\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2}=0.02196\pm 0.00063

IV.2 Cosmic microwave background (CMB)

The power spectra of anisotropies in the CMB contain a wealth of information on cosmological parameters that is complementary to that from BAO. The large-scale temperature and polarisation power spectra and CMB lensing power spectrum have been exquisitely measured by Planck Planck Collaboration et al. 2020b; Planck Collaboration et al. 2020a, with additional information from smaller scales and improved lensing reconstruction provided by the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT; Madhavacheril et al. 2024; Qu et al. 2024; MacCrann et al. 2024) and the South Pole Telescope (SPT; Carlstrom et al. 2011).

When using the full power of the CMB information, our baseline analysis makes use of the temperature (T​TTT), polarization (E​EEE) and cross (T​ETE) power spectra from Planck, specifically using the simall, Commander (for ℓ<30\ell<30) and CamSpec (for ℓ≥30\ell\geq 30) likelihoods, plus the combination of Planck and ACT DR6 CMB lensing from Madhavacheril et al. 2024.1111

This paper uses the updated version v1.2 likelihood. CamSpec Efstathiou and Gratton 2021; Rosenberg et al. 2022 is a new likelihood built on the latest NPIPE PR4 data release from the Planck collaboration, which replaces the original Plik likelihood based on the older PR3 release and includes some important differences in methodology. Another set of likelihoods based on PR4, known as LoLLiPoP (for low ℓ\ell) and HiLLiPoP (for high ℓ\ell) have also been independently released Tristram et al. 2024, which for brevity we will refer to as L-H in the following. However, certain aspects of the early Universe can be robustly constrained independently of any modifications of the late-time cosmological model. IV.3 Type Ia supernovae (SNe)

Type Ia SNe are luminous standardizable candles that provide another probe of the expansion history of the Universe, especially useful at low redshifts (0.01<z<0.30.01<z<0.3) where BAO measurements are limited by cosmic variance. There are three recent SNe datasets and likelihoods available for cosmology: the Pantheon+ Scolnic et al. 2022; Brout et al. 2022, Union3 Rubin et al. 2023 and Dark Energy Survey Year 5 (DESY5; DES Collaboration et al. 2024) samples.

The Pantheon+ and Union3 samples consist of compilations of 1,550 and 2,087 spectroscopically-classified SNe, respectively, drawn from multiple observational programs over the past few decades of observations. While 1,363 of the objects are in common between the two, there are differences in the calibration, modeling and treatment of systematic uncertainties. The DESY5 sample consists of 1,635 photometrically-classified z>0.1z>0.1 SNe with uniform calibration drawn from a single survey up to z=1.13z=1.13, together with a small set of 194 SNe at z<0.1z<0.1 drawn from historical sources, some of which are in common with the Pantheon+ and Union3 datasets. Despite the larger differences in the underlying data, the Pantheon+ and DESY5 analysis methodologies are quite similar in nature, although DESY5 uses the SALT3 Kenworthy et al. 2021 light-curve fitting model as opposed to SALT2 Guy et al. 2007, among other differences. Union3 shares a larger fraction of the data with Pantheon+ but uses a different approach based on the Bayesian hierarchical modeling framework Unity1.5 Rubin et al. 2015.

As in our DR1 analysis, we do not choose between these three options but instead present our headline results in combination with each SNe dataset independently, noting the differences in interpretation where appropriate. All three likelihoods are implemented in the Cobaya sampling code Torrado and Lewis 2019; Torrado and Lewis 2021 and the underlying data are publicly available.1414

In section VII, we illustrate the distance modulus residuals for the SNe datasets in redshift bins. For visual purposes only, we rebin the SNe in redshift using the same bins for all datasets, and then calculate the weighted average distance moduli and errors using the inverse of the covariance matrix (including both statistical and systematic errors and after removing the weighted mean of the distance moduli). All cosmological fits use the data and covariance as originally provided.

IV.4 Dark Energy Survey 3×\times2pt

Weak gravitational lensing of galaxies and its combination with galaxy clustering are local probes of structure formation. For models with an evolving dark energy equation of state, we use galaxy-galaxy, galaxy-shear, and shear-shear two-point correlation functions (referred to here as 3×\times2pt) measurements from the Dark Energy Survey Year-3 (DESY3) analysis Abbott et al. 2022. These data vectors were estimated from observations of over 10 million lens galaxies in the MagLim sample covering an area of approximately 4000 square degrees. We adopt the same modeling choices and scale cuts as the corresponding DESY3 analysis Abbott et al. 2023. We choose the same priors as DESY3 with the exception of the parameters w0w_{\rm 0}, waw_{a}, H0​rdH_{\rm 0}r_{\rm d} and Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}, where we impose priors that match those used for the DESI analysis in this paper. In particular, in post-processing we compute H0​rdH_{\rm 0}r_{\rm d} as a derived parameter and reweight the chains to impose the same prior for free parameters in the DESI chains. We sample the DESY3 likelihood using the publicly available DESY3 CosmoSIS pipeline Zuntz et al. 2015. To combine these constraints with DESI and SNe data at the posterior level, we use CombineHarvesterFlow Taylor et al. 2024 to fit normalizing flows to the DESY3 chains and reweight the DESI and DESI+SNe chains.

VII Dark Energy

Probing the behavior and nature of dark energy is the primary goal of DESI. The question of perhaps greatest interest, and the one that BAO measurements can best illuminate, is the value of the equation-of-state parameter w=P/(ρ​c2)w=P/(\rho c^{2}), and its possible evolution with time. To examine this we will primarily use the so-called Chevallier-Polarski-Linder (CPL) parametrization Chevallier and Polarski 2001; Linder 2003 of equation 9. While this form of w⁡(a)w(a) does not arise directly from an underlying physical model, it is a flexible parametrization that is capable of matching the predictions for observable quantities obtained in a wide range of models that are physically motivated de Putter and Linder 2008. The accompanying paper Lodha et al. 2025a explores various other parametrizations of w⁡(z)w(z), as well as non-parametric reconstruction methods.

For certain ranges of parameters w0w_{0} and waw_{a}, the parametrization of equation 9 allows so-called ‘phantom’ behavior of dark energy, in which the equation of state crosses to the regime w⁡(z)<−1w(z)<-1 Caldwell 2002 where the null energy condition (NEC)—which requires that the energy density of dark energy not increase with the expansion of the Universe—is violated. For single scalar-field models of dark energy, this phantom crossing presents severe theoretical difficulties (Carroll et al. 2003; Vikman 2005, e.g.,). However, more complex models of dark energy, with multiple fields, other dark energy internal degrees of freedom, or non-minimal coupling, can evade these difficulties, as can some modified gravity models, see, e.g., Hu 2005; Koussour et al. 2023; Wolf et al. 2024; Clifton et al. 2012; Ishak 2019.
We therefore adopt wide uniform priors on the parameters, w0∈𝒰⁡[−3,1]w_{0}\in\mathcal{U}[-3,1] and wa∈𝒰⁡[−3,2]w_{a}\in\mathcal{U}[-3,2], together with imposing the condition w0+wa<0w_{0}+w_{a}<0 to enforce early matter domination. While other justifiable choices are possible, and the values of Bayesian quantities such as the model evidence will always depend on the particular choice used, we consider this the minimal empirical approach. Whenever the equation of state crosses the w=−1w=-1 boundary we use the parametrized post-Friedmann (PPF) approach of Hu and Sawicki 2007; Fang et al. 2008 to include dark energy perturbations when calculating CMB power spectra—however, as shown below, the method of accounting for dark energy perturbations does not play a major role, since simply applying an early-Universe CMB prior on (θ∗,ωb,ωc)(\theta_{\ast},\omega_{\mathrm{b}},\omega_{\mathrm{c}}) largely reproduces the same results on w0w_{0} and waw_{a}.

Our primary measure of the statistical significance of preference for evolving dark energy from a given data combination is based on Δ​χMAP2\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}} between the best-fit Λ\LambdaCDM and w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM models for that combination. Because Λ\LambdaCDM is nested within w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM, corresponding to w0=−1w_{0}=-1, wa=0w_{a}=0, Wilks’ theorem Wilks 1938 implies that Δ​χMAP2\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}} should follow a χ2\chi^{2} distribution with two degrees of freedom under the assumption the null hypothesis (Λ\LambdaCDM model) holds, and assuming that errors are Gaussian and correctly estimated. To translate Δ​χMAP2\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}} into familiar terms, we quote the corresponding frequentist significance N​σN\sigma for a 1D Gaussian distribution,

CDFχ2(ΔχMAP2| 2dof)=12​π∫−NNe−t2/2dt,\mathrm{CDF}_{\chi^{2}}\left(\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}}|\,2\,\mathrm{dof}\right)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}}\int_{-N}^{N}e^{-t^{2}/2}dt~,

(22)

where the left hand side denotes the cumulative distribution of χ2\chi^{2}. We also compute the Deviance Information Criterion (DIC) Spiegelhalter et al. 2014; Trotta 2008; Grandis et al. 2016; Kass and Raftery 1995, which takes into account the Bayesian complexity of the model and penalizes including extra parameters.

In all cases, the favored w⁡(z)w(z) shows a phase of w>−1w>-1 at low redshifts and a phantom crossing to w<−1w<-1 above redshifts z≃0.4z\simeq 0.4. Within the w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM model, the necessity of such a crossing and the redshift at which it occurs is determined by the requirement to match the precise CMB measurement of θ∗\theta_{\ast} Linder 2007.
The details of the recovered form of w⁡(z)w(z) and the w0w_{0}, waw_{a} parameter values naturally depend on the choice of parametrization. The accompanying paper Lodha et al. 2025a explores various other parametrizations of w⁡(z)w(z) beyond equation 9, and non-parametric reconstruction methods, that exhibit a similar behavior. Reference Lodha et al. 2025a also performs binned reconstruction of w⁡(z)w(z) without assuming a functional form for the equation of state and finds a consistent picture, as shown in figure 12. The lowest redshift bin shown in the figure favors a value of w>−1w>-1 at high significance Lodha et al. 2025a, inconsistent with the Λ\LambdaCDM expectation w=−1w=-1.

The apparent preference for phantom crossing to w⁡(z)<−1w(z)<-1 at intermediate redshifts, and the consequent violation of the NEC, is thus rather independent of parametrization choices made in the analysis (see, e.g., Calderon et al. 2024 using DR1 data for non-parametric results). However, the equation of state is not directly observable, and we only observe quantities, such as distances, that depend indirectly on w⁡(z)w(z). In some circumstances it may therefore be possible to construct particular models that provide reasonable fits to the low-redshift data while still respecting w⁡(z)≥−1w(z)\geq-1 at all zz (Shlivko and Steinhardt 2024; Lodha et al. 2025b provide explicit examples, but Lewis and Chamberlain 2024 discusses the general limitations of such models in fitting DESI+CMB data).
The supporting paper Lodha et al. 2025a examines several NEC-respecting models of dark energy evolution and finds that while they can somewhat outperform Λ\LambdaCDM, they have low Δ⁡(DIC)\Delta(\text{DIC}) values relative to w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM for the combination of DESI, CMB and SNe data, indicating a preference for phantom crossing.

As is the case for BAO, there exist Λ\LambdaCDM models that can reasonably fit the SNe data (orange curves), but these have large Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} that do not well match CMB constraints and are also strongly inconsistent with DESI data, which prefer an Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} value that is lower than Planck, not higher. All these observations point to the fact that the Λ\LambdaCDM model struggles to consistently fit all three datasets: BAO, CMB, and SNe.

One way to address this tension is to adopt a model with more flexibility in the background expansion. However, the wwCDM model with a constant equation of state lacks sufficient flexibility. The cyan dotted curves in the top row and bottom left show a model with constant w=−0.971w=-0.971 and Ωm=0.310\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.310, which is the wwCDM model that best fits the DESI+CMB+DESY5 data combination; in the remaining two panels the cyan curves show the predictions of very similar models obtained by substituting Union3 or Pantheon+ for DESY5. If instead the wwCDM model were chosen to fit the DESI+DESY5 data, it would necessarily have an Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} value that would fail to match the CMB constraints on (θ∗,ωb,ωbc)(\theta_{\ast},\omega_{\mathrm{b}},\omega_{\mathrm{bc}}).

On the other hand, the w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM model does have sufficient flexibility to simultaneously achieve good fits to all three datasets. The green dashed and red dashed curves in the top panels show predictions for w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM models with parameters matching the best fits to DESI+DESY5 and DESI+CMB+DESY5 respectively. These are barely distinguishable in the plots, showing that the w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM model that best fits the BAO and SNe automatically also provides a good fit to the CMB. Over the range of redshifts covered by DESI BAO, these model predictions provide a better fit to BAO data than the best such Λ\LambdaCDM model—in particular, in the fit to the parallel BAO distances α|⁣|\alpha_{||}—while also simultaneously resolving the mismatch in Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} between DESI and CMB in the Λ\LambdaCDM framework. The bottom left panel also shows that they are also equally good at fitting the μ\mu offsets between low-zz and high-zz DESY5 data. These observations qualitatively help to explain why these models are strongly preferred over Λ\LambdaCDM.

In the equivalent curves in the center and right panels on the bottom row, DESY5 is replaced in all fits by Union3 and Pantheon+, respectively. While the qualitative picture is the same for Union3 as DESY5, the value of μ−μfid\mu-\mu_{\mathrm{fid}} at low redshift is smaller for Pantheon+ and so using this dataset does not strengthen the preference for evolving dark energy relative to that already provided by the joint fit to DESI and the CMB.

VII.3 Are alternative explanations possible?

Given the surprising results from our analysis—our best-fit evolving dark energy models apparently imply phantom dark energy at some redshifts, and order unity changes in ww over the redshift range spanned by the observations considered—it is worth considering alternative explanations for the data.

The simplest of these alternatives is simply a statistical fluctuation. Based on the Δ​χMAP2\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}} values presented in section VII.1 above, the statistical preference for the best-fit w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM model over Λ\LambdaCDM ranges from around 3​σ3\sigma to over 4​σ4\sigma, depending on the combination of datasets considered.
This level of significance is not trivially dismissed, even if it does not yet rise to the 5​σ5\sigma threshold commonly accepted for establishing new physics. The variations in the statistical significance within this range with the choice of SNe sample also highlight the importance of calibrating the low-to-intermediate redshift SNe distance scale.

The constraining power of SNe in measuring the equation of state comes primarily from the comparison of low-redshift (z<0.1z<0.1) and high redshift (z>0.1z>0.1) supernovae. For supernovae at z>0.1z>0.1, which partially overlap the redshift range of DESI, the Λ\LambdaCDM model that best fits the DESI data is also a good fit to the SNe data. Relative to models that best fit each of the DESY5, Union3 and Pantheon+ SNe samples alone, over the full redshift range, the DESI best-fit model gives only small shifts in the quality of the fit to the SNe data, with Δ​χ2=−1.2\Delta\chi^{2}=-1.2, 1.5 and 2.3 respectively. Unfortunately, no SNe compilation yet exists in which objects from both redshift regimes were collected as part of a uniform observational program. The Pantheon+ and Union3 datasets are compilations of SNe observed in many individual datasets, each with different calibrations and selection functions. Even for the DESY5 dataset, while 1635 SNe with z>0.1z>0.1 come from the DES survey program with homogeneous calibration, 194 SNe at z<0.1z<0.1 are drawn from a mix of historical observational programs with the best-controlled calibrations. The consistent calibration and processing of these data is therefore a delicate operation. Recently, Efstathiou 2024 noted an offset in the differences between the standardized brightnesses of low-zz and high-zz SNe for objects in common between the DESY5 and Pantheon+ samples; however, Vincenzi et al. 2025 explain the causes for this and argue that the differences are well justified.

If we exclude the low-z sample entirely and take only the DESY5 SNe, which is the most uniformly calibrated sample, naturally the constraining power and thus the statistical significance of the preference for evolution is reduced, but the best-fit values of w0w_{0} and waw_{a} remain far from Λ\LambdaCDM (central panel of figure 14). Even if SNe at all redshifts are excluded altogether, the statistical significance of the preference from DESI+CMB alone still exceeds 3​σ3\sigma. Future cosmology analyses of homogeneous SNe samples from ZTF Rigault et al. 2024; Bellm et al. 2019 and LSST Lochner et al. 2022; Gris et al. 2023 may shed further light on the relative calibration across redshifts.

Using the simple early-Universe prior on (θ∗,ωb,ωbc)(\theta_{\ast},\omega_{\mathrm{b}},\omega_{\mathrm{bc}}) that marginalizes over information dependent on late-time models in addition to DESI gives very similar posterior constraints on w0w_{0} and waw_{a} to the full DESI+CMB combination (left panel of figure 14), and the central w0w_{0}, waw_{a} values are very stable. However, the tension with Λ\LambdaCDM drops somewhat (from 3.1σ\sigma to 2.4​σ2.4\sigma) when using these simple priors instead of the full CMB: this is because the (Planck+ACT) CMB lensing likelihood makes a sizable contribution to the Δ​χMAP2\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}} calculation.

Although recent independent results from SPT Ge et al. 2024 and ACT Madhavacheril et al. 2024 find cosmological parameter values that are consistent with those from Planck, another possibility is that the CMB constraints used here suffer from some unknown systematic error. It is therefore interesting to consider the constraints that can be obtained entirely independent of the high-redshift CMB anchor. As argued in section VII.2, the primary role of this CMB anchor is that it limits the freedom to vary Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} when fitting to the low-redshift BAO and SNe distance-redshift measurements. This can be achieved instead by replacing the CMB with the DESY3 (3×\times2pt) information to obtain a constraint coming entirely from low-redshift cosmological probes. As shown in the right panel of figure 14, this combination favors the same region of parameter space, although with somewhat larger uncertainties.

We also consider the possibility of an undetected systematic error in our BAO measurements that would have evaded the many internal checks we have performed (cf. details in DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c; Andrade et al. 2025a; DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d). For instance, a coherent systematic shift of 1.5% to all the measured αiso\alpha_{\rm iso} values (equation 11), applied in a redshift-independent manner to all tracers, would decrease the tension between DESI and Planck in the Λ\LambdaCDM model, and thus decrease the evidence for evolving dark energy.1818
 18
 
 
 
 
 
 
 
 Such a shift would result in the DESI contours in figure 8 shifting ∼1.5%\sim 1.5\% to the left and thus overlapping the Planck CMB results in Λ\LambdaCDM. However, any such hypothetical shift could not be explained by a simple change in the physical value of rdr_{\mathrm{d}}, as that would also shift the CMB constraints. However, the required magnitude of such a hypothetical shift exceeds the total systematic error budget indicated by our tests by a factor of 6 (for QSO) to 10 (for BGS and LRGs), and we consider this highly unlikely.

Physical models that can satisfactorily explain the data without requiring dark energy evolution are more challenging. table 5 confirms the finding, previously reported in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025c, that allowing the curvature ΩK\Omega_{\mathrm{K}} to vary freely, while increasing uncertainties, does not shift the posterior in w0w_{0} and waw_{a} towards Λ\LambdaCDM. While we have fixed ∑mν=0.06\sum m_{\nu}=0.06 eV for our fiducial analysis, it is allowed to vary in section VIII below, and we find that this has almost no effect on the dark energy constraints. This is because fitting the CMB power spectrum imposes a positive correlation between ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} and Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}, thus given a physical prior that ∑mν>0\sum m_{\nu}>0 eV, in most of the available parameter space neutrino masses can only increase the CMB value of Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}, which exacerbates rather than relieves the tension with DESI BAO, and in the region where 0≤∑mν≤0.060\leq\sum m_{\nu}\leq 0.06 eV they have very limited effect. However, the more exotic scenario where ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} is treated as an ‘effective’ neutrino mass such that values ∑mν,eff<0\sum m_{\nu,{\rm eff}}<0 eV are allowed Green and Meyers 2024; Craig et al. 2024; Elbers et al. 2024 can allow CMB fits to be consistent with lower Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}. This has the effect of improving the Λ\LambdaCDM fit and thus reducing the preference for evolving dark energy from DESI+CMB+SNe, but not below the level that is found from the relevant DESI+SNe combination alone without any CMB information (which can be up to 3.3σ\sigma, table 6), as shown in section VIII. That is, allowing negative effective neutrino masses still does not fully remove the preference for non-cosmological constant dark energy. At the same time, such dark energy models do permit ∑mν,eff>0\sum m_{\nu,{\rm eff}}>0 values that are consistent with terrestrial experiments. More standard extensions, such as varying the number of effective neutrino species, NeffN_{\mathrm{eff}}, have no effect on the dark energy results.

Arguably, the least contorted way to reconcile our findings with Λ\LambdaCDM is to exclude all constraints from SNe data (on the grounds that the significance depends on the choice of SNe dataset and we have no a priori reason to choose one over the others), and then dismiss the remaining 3.1​σ3.1\sigma discrepancy with DESI+CMB (or 2.7​σ2.7\sigma when also not including CMB lensing) as a statistical fluke.

VIII Neutrinos

Cosmological observations are sensitive to the sum of the neutrino masses. However, this measurement depends on the choice of a cosmological model. So far, our baseline analysis has assumed the sum of neutrino masses to be fixed to the minimum value allowed by terrestrial experiments under a normal mass ordering, ∑mν=0.06\sum m_{\nu}=0.06 eV, modeled as a single massive and two massless states. In this section we allow ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} to vary. When doing so, we assume by default three degenerate mass eigenstates except when testing specific physical mass ordering scenarios. This model produces a good approximation of the observable effects of both normal and inverted physical mass ordering scenarios and, in the case of a positive detection of neutrino masses, would recover the correct ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} value without bias Lesgourgues and Pastor 2006; Di Valentino et al. 2018. We also impose a minimal physical (uniform) prior ∑mν>0\sum m_{\nu}>0 eV, while noting that scenarios with negative ‘effective’ neutrino mass have also recently been discussed (e.g., Green and Meyers 2024; Craig et al. 2024; Elbers et al. 2024). Supporting paper Elbers et al. 2025 explores the implications of different modelling assumptions in greater detail.

Primordial neutrinos were copiously produced before the nucleosynthesis era and decoupled at a temperature of about 1​MeV1\,\text{MeV}; see, e.g., Lesgourgues and Pastor 2006 for a review of neutrino cosmology. As the Universe expanded, neutrinos gradually lost kinetic energy, behaving as radiation in the early Universe and transitioning to non-relativistic matter around redshifts of z∼100z\sim 100 for realistic neutrino masses, thereafter influencing the late-time expansion history by contributing to the matter component. The main effect of massive neutrinos on the CMB is to impact the angular diameter distance to last scattering, which is degenerate with the effects of other cosmological parameters such as Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} and H0H_{0} (see, e.g., Loverde and Weiner 2024 for a recent discussion). Neutrinos also affect the lensing of CMB anisotropies by suppressing the growth of structure below the free-streaming scale. BAO are not sensitive to the latter effect at all, and only probe the background geometry by constraining the total matter density Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} and the parameter combination H0​rdH_{0}r_{\mathrm{d}}, so DESI BAO alone cannot constrain the neutrino masses. Nevertheless by breaking geometrical degeneracies, BAO significantly enhance the ability of the CMB to constrain this parameter.

The upper limits on ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} that we obtain from the combination of DESI and CMB depend on the particular choice of the CMB likelihood used, since the various likelihoods differ slightly in the amount of lensing power they infer from the lensed T​TTT, T​ETE and E​EEE power spectra. This can be incorporated into a phenomenological parameter ALA_{\rm L} that scales the model lensing power used to compute the lensed power spectra (but not the power reconstructed from the 4-point function), such that values AL>1A_{\rm L}>1 indicate an excess of lensing power, often referred to as the ‘ALA_{\rm L} anomaly’. Increasing ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} above 0.06 eV decreases the expected lensing power, while smaller values would boost it.

The baseline CMB likelihood we have adopted through this paper combines the CamSpec likelihood Efstathiou and Gratton 2021; Rosenberg et al. 2022 together with the combined CMB likelihood from Planck Carron et al. 2022 and ACT Madhavacheril et al. 2024, both of which are based on the PR4 data release. Combining these with the DESI DR2 BAO, and assuming a Λ\LambdaCDM background, we find the marginalized posterior limit

∑mν<0.064​eV(95%, DESI+CMB). \left.\sum m_{\nu}<0.064\,{\rm eV}\quad\text{(95\%,~DESI+CMB). }\right.

(29)

Although the CamSpec likelihood appears to prefer a lower lensing power and reduces the ALA_{\rm L} anomaly Rosenberg et al. 2022, the tighter constraints on other cosmological parameters degenerate with ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} mean that this upper bound is tighter than the one obtained with the original Plik likelihood:

∑mν<0.069​eV(95%, DESI+CMB[Plik]). \left.\sum m_{\nu}<0.069\,{\rm eV}\quad\text{(95\%,~DESI+CMB[{Plik}]). }\right.

(30)

The result of equation 30 is directly comparable to the result obtained from BAO and CMB in the DESI DR1 analysis DESI Collaboration et al. 2025c; DESI Collaboration et al. 2024e, which used the Plik likelihood as the default and obtained the corresponding constraint ∑mν<0.082\sum m_{\nu}<0.082 eV (95%).1919

Irrespective of which CMB likelihood is used, all three upper bounds are very close to the lower bound ∑mν>0.059\sum m_{\nu}>0.059 eV set by neutrino oscillation experiments Esteban et al. 2024. The posteriors shown in figure 15 all resemble the tails of distributions that would peak at negative neutrino masses if this were allowed by the prior. This behavior has been explored in a few recent studies Green and Meyers 2024; Noriega and Aviles 2024; Elbers et al. 2024; Naredo-Tuero et al. 2024, as well as in our companion work Elbers et al. 2025, which also shows that it holds for profile likelihoods based on extrapolating the χ2\chi^{2} likelihood surface to ∑mν<0\sum m_{\nu}<0 eV. In the context of a model with effective neutrino masses that are allowed to assume negative values, the DESI+CMB likelihood peaks at ∑mν,eff<0\sum m_{\nu,{\rm eff}}<0, and the tension with the minimal mass bound from terrestrial experiments increases to 3.0​σ3.0\sigma Elbers et al. 2025.

This discrepancy can be related to the tension between DESI and CMB results for the parameters Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} and H0​rdH_{0}r_{\mathrm{d}} in a Λ\LambdaCDM background discussed in section VI above, and particularly in figure 8. For fixed angular acoustic scale θ∗\theta_{\ast}, the effects on the CMB of changing ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} and changing Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} are positively correlated, as can be seen from the dashed black contour in figure 16 (similar degeneracies can also be observed between ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} and H0​rdH_{0}r_{\mathrm{d}}, but with a negative correlation). In Λ\LambdaCDM, DESI tightly constrains Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}, as shown by the vertical shaded contour, to a value close to the low-end tail of the marginalized posterior distribution for CMB measurements. Consequently, the combination of DESI and CMB rules out all but the smallest neutrino masses. This result is thus related to the preference of DESI for lower values of Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} compared to the CMB discussed in the previous Sections.

As the upper limits on ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} from cosmology approach the lower limits from neutrino oscillations, the distinction between the different mass orderings becomes increasingly relevant. The baseline constraint equation 29, obtained assuming a ∑mν>0\sum m_{\nu}>0 prior, already appears to rule out the inverted ordering. However, to determine the evidence in support of the normal ordering, one should also account for the fact that much of the posterior volume violates the constraints for both mass orderings. To do this we use a physical model for the total mass ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} in terms of lightest neutrino mass ml≥0m_{l}\geq 0, which includes information on the mass squared splittings Δ​m212\Delta m_{21}^{2} and |Δ​m312||\Delta m_{31}^{2}| and allows for either mass ordering, as described in Loureiro et al. 2019. We then determine the upper bounds on this model from a combined analysis of DESI, CMB (with the baseline CamSpec likelihood) and 3-flavor oscillation constraints from NuFit-6.0 Esteban et al. 2024, assigning equal prior probabilities to either mass ordering. The result obtained is

IX Conclusions

We have presented BAO measurements from over 14 million discrete galaxy and quasar tracers drawn from the first three years of operation of DESI and which will be included in the second data release (DR2). These results use samples of nearby bright galaxies, LRGs, ELGs and quasars over the redshift range 0.1<z<2.10.1<z<2.1, and cover a cumulative effective volume of over 42​Gpc342\;{\rm Gpc}^{3}. Complementary BAO measurements from correlations of the Lyα\alpha forest and high-redshift quasars at effective redshift z=2.33z=2.33 are presented in the companion paper DESI Collaboration et al. 2025d.

Our BAO analysis largely follows the methods used for the previous DESI DR1 analysis and presented in DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c, but with improved statistical precision as the effective volume of the data has increased by more than a factor of two. Some particular differences include the use of a fainter limiting magnitude cut to define the bright galaxy sample, resulting in a higher number density, and the inclusion of quadrupole information in BAO fits to the quasar sample. As for the DR1 analysis, we applied a strict catalog-level blinding to our data while initial data checks were carried out and the analysis pipeline was being finalized. The validation tests and the criteria that were required to be met before the data were unblinded are described in detail in the supporting publication Andrade et al. 2025a.

The final BAO results provide a precision on the isotropic distance scale measurement of DV/rdD_{\mathrm{V}}/r_{\mathrm{d}} that ranges from 1.54% (for the QSO) down to just 0.45% for our most constraining composite LRG3++ELG1 sample; other than for the QSO, the precision is sub-percent for every tracer and redshift range. We also obtained a precision of a few percent on the measurement of the Alcock-Paczyński distance ratio for every tracer except the lowest redshift BGS. Together these results are the most precise BAO measurements ever made at all redshifts covered, including at z<0.8z<0.8 where DESI DR2 now greatly exceeds the precision of the Sloan Digital Sky Survey (SDSS).

The DR2 results are very consistent with those previously reported from the DR1 data that form a subset of DR2. The pp-value determined from the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test for the distribution of the differences is 0.22. The DR2 results are also consistent with SDSS. DESI Collaboration et al. 2024c previously reported a ∼3​σ\sim 3\sigma discrepancy in the measurement of the transverse BAO scale DM/rdD_{\mathrm{M}}/r_{\mathrm{d}} in the LRG2 sample at effective redshift zeff=0.706z_{\rm eff}=0.706 compared to the equivalent result from SDSS. For DR2, the difference has decreased in significance, lying within the range 1.5​σ1.5\sigma to 2.6​σ2.6\sigma, depending on assumptions about the degree of correlation between the two samples.

The combination of all BAO measurements from DR2 is well fit by a flat Λ\LambdaCDM cosmological model with matter density parameter Ωm=0.2975±0.0086\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}=0.2975\pm 0.0086 and product of the scaled Hubble constant and sound horizon at the drag epoch h​rd=(101.54±0.73)hr_{\mathrm{d}}=(101.54\pm 0.73) Mpc. This represents a 40% improvement in precision compared to the equivalent results from DR1, but with excellent consistency between the two. However, while a Λ\LambdaCDM model provides a good fit to DESI data, the model parameters obtained from this fit are now in 2.3σ\sigma tension with those derived from the CMB, increased from 1.9σ\sigma in DR1. This tension is present despite DESI being consistent with the acoustic angular scale θ∗\theta_{*} measured by CMB.

When calibrated with an external prior on Ωb​h2\Omega_{\mathrm{b}}h^{2} from BBN, our BAO measurements correspond to a value H0=(68.50±0.58)​km​s−1​Mpc−1H_{0}=(68.50\pm 0.58)\;\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}} in Λ\LambdaCDM, a value that is independent of any information on CMB anisotropies. In the Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}-H0H_{0} plane, the results from BBN-calibrated BAO are now more discrepant with those from CMB; the offset of the results is again along the Ωm​h3=constant\Omega_{\mathrm{m}}h^{3}={\rm constant} degeneracy direction of the CMB that is determined by the very precisely measured acoustic angular scale θ∗\theta_{\ast}. Combining DESI BAO with BBN and the Planck θ∗\theta_{\ast} result gives H0=(68.45±0.47)​km​s−1​Mpc−1H_{0}=(68.45\pm 0.47)\;\,{\rm km\,s^{-1}\,Mpc^{-1}}, a 0.7% precision measurement competitive with that from the CMB itself, and in strong disagreement with SH0ES Riess et al. 2022.

Within the Λ\LambdaCDM framework, DESI results are also somewhat in tension with the high Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} values preferred by SNe datasets, which—contrary to DESI—prefer larger Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} than Planck. While not individually rising to the 3​σ3\sigma significance threshold, these results point to an incompatibility between different cosmological datasets when interpreted in the Λ\LambdaCDM model. Interestingly, the relative levels of Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} and H0H_{0} values currently measured by SN, DESI and CMB datasets match what would be expected if data from a true evolving dark energy model were analyzed in the restrictive Λ\LambdaCDM model, as pointed out recently by Tang et al. 2024.

Assuming a Λ\LambdaCDM background, the combination of DESI and CMB data give the tightest upper bound on the neutrino mass sum to date, ∑mν<0.064\sum m_{\nu}<0.064 eV (95% limit) in our baseline analysis. Although this relaxes to ∑mν<0.078\sum m_{\nu}<0.078 eV when using an alternative Planck likelihood Tristram et al. 2024, both results are approaching the lower bound set by terrestrial neutrino oscillation experiments, ∑mν≥0.059\sum m_{\nu}\geq 0.059 eV. Indeed, if the model is extended to allow for negative ‘effective’ neutrino masses, most of the posterior mass and the peak of the likelihood lie at ∑mν,eff<0\sum m_{\nu,{\rm eff}}<0 eV, another possible sign of growing tensions within the Λ\LambdaCDM model with DESI DR2 Elbers et al. 2025.

The evidence for a departure from Λ\LambdaCDM in the form of evolving dark energy has increased with the DR2 BAO data. Comparing the evolving dark energy model parametrized by w⁡(a)=w0+wa​(1−a)w(a)=w_{0}+w_{a}(1-a) to Λ\LambdaCDM, we find a 3.1​σ3.1\sigma evidence in favor of dynamical dark energy from DESI+CMB alone. When we add the recent Pantheon+, Union3 or DESY5 SNe datasets to this combination, the preference for w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM over Λ\LambdaCDM is 2.8​σ2.8\sigma, 3.8​σ3.8\sigma or 4.2​σ4.2\sigma respectively, with all three giving results for w0w_{0} and waw_{a} consistent with each other within their 68% credible regions. The preferences for w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM under these dataset combinations have increased compared to our DR1 results in DESI Collaboration et al. 2025c. All combinations of available datasets we study favor mutually consistent results with w0>−1w_{0}>-1 and wa<0w_{a}<0, apparently indicating a weakening dark energy today and a phantom crossing at some point in the past (in this parametrization). The supporting paper Lodha et al. 2025a examines this behavior using a wider range of models and non-parametric methods. We note that the degeneracy direction for the constraints in the w0w_{0}-waw_{a} plane approximately points towards the Λ\LambdaCDM solution, although this is neither exact nor consistent between fits to different combinations of datasets. This constitutes a weak coincidence as there is no a priori reason for the best-fit values of w0w_{0} and waw_{a} to have the values that lead to this observation.

In sections VII.2 and VII.3, we examined the contributions to the preference for evolving dark energy in detail. For DESI+CMB, the discrepancy in preferred Ωm\Omega_{\mathrm{m}} values in Λ\LambdaCDM is resolved in w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM, leading to the 3.1​σ3.1\sigma (Δ​χMAP2=−12.5\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}}=-12.5) preference for the latter. If we omit CMB lensing the best-fit w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM parameters are unchanged, though the significance drops to 2.7​σ2.7\sigma (Δ​χMAP2=−9.7\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}}=-9.7). If we also marginalize over any late-time dependence in the CMB and only impose a prior on (θ∗,ωb,ωbc)(\theta_{\ast},\omega_{\mathrm{b}},\omega_{\mathrm{bc}}), there is a further slight reduction to 2.4​σ2.4\sigma (Δ​χMAP2=−8.0\Delta\chi^{2}_{\mathrm{MAP}}=-8.0). The w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM and ‘compromise’ Λ\LambdaCDM models predict different evolution of the distance-redshift relation at z<0.4z<0.4, with a ∼2%\sim 2\% difference in distances at z<0.1z<0.1. BAO do not provide high precision in this regime, but supernovae do, and the DESY5 and Union3 samples clearly favor the w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM prediction. However, the distance-redshift evolution in the Pantheon+ analysis is closer to the Λ\LambdaCDM prediction, so adding Pantheon+ to DESI+CMB does not strengthen the evidence for w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM. appendix A shows that our results are robust to changes in the choice of CMB likelihood. We also show that replacing the CMB likelihood with the weak gravitational lensing constraints from the DESY3 (3×\times2pt) analysis retains a clear preference for evolving dark energy, with, e.g., a ≳3​σ\gtrsim 3\sigma preference for w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM from DESI+DESY3 (3×\times2pt)+DESY5 SNe. Removing the preference for w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM through changes to the DESI measurements themselves would require systematic errors to be far larger than any found in our tests.

In the w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM background model, when the neutrino mass scale is allowed to vary with physical non-negative prior bounds, the upper limit on ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} is significantly relaxed to ∑mν<0.16\sum m_{\nu}<0.16 eV (95%, from DESI+CMB) or ∑mν<0.13\sum m_{\nu}<0.13 eV (95%, from DESI+CMB+DESY5, the middle result from the three possible SNe samples), while the constraints on w0w_{0} and waw_{a} do not materially change from the case where ∑mν\sum m_{\nu} is fixed. These limits are entirely consistent with neutrino oscillation experiment results. The supporting paper Elbers et al. 2025 shows that even when allowing for effective masses ∑mν,eff\sum m_{\nu,{\rm eff}} with a wide prior that allows negative values, the marginalized posterior in w0​waw_{0}w_{a}CDM from DESI+CMB is consistent with the oscillation lower limit and its peak lies in the positive region.

In DESI Collaboration et al. 2025c we characterized the results from DESI DR1 and external datasets as providing “a tantalizing suggestion of deviations from the standard cosmological model”. The DR2 data presented here have sharpened this evidence, although significance levels still vary depending on the external data used—particularly the choice of SNe sample—and no combination exceeds 4.2​σ4.2\sigma. Nevertheless, it is becoming clear that unless some unidentified systematic error affects one or several of the different cosmological datasets used, the challenge to the Λ\LambdaCDM model has increased. Sharper measurements from future DESI analyses and from other experiments will show whether these challenges to the standard cosmological model herald yet another radical transformation in our understanding of the evolution and energy content of the Universe.

Appendix B Robustness to assumption about correlation in BAO systematic errors

Our baseline approach, described briefly in section III and in more detail in Andrade et al. 2025a, assumes that the systematic error contributions to BAO measurements are not correlated with each other across redshift bins. As a robustness test, we evaluated the impact of introducing off-diagonal terms in the systematics covariance matrix of the BAO measurements. To do this, we assumed that the theoretical modelling systematic error contributions, which are estimated to be 0.1% in αiso\alpha_{\mathrm{iso}} and 0.2% in αAP\alpha_{\rm AP}, are correlated across all redshift bins, with a correlation coefficient of 0.5. This leads to redshift-dependent correlations in the final covariance matrix. We found that including these correlations had no detectable effect on the cosmological constraints obtained in any model or combination of DESI with any external dataset. This result is unsurprising, since the systematic errors are subdominant to statistical errors in all redshift bins, so correlations in the systematic errors cannot play a large role.
