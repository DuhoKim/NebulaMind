Abstract

A Universe with finite age also has a finite causal scale. Larger scales can not affect our local measurements or modeling, but far away locations could have different cosmological parameters. The size of our causal Universe depends on the details of inflation and is usually assumed to be larger than our observable Universe today. To account for causality, we propose a new boundary condition, that can be fulfill by fixing the cosmological constant (a free geometric parameter of gravity). This forces a cancellation of vacuum energy with the cosmological constant. As a consequence, the measured cosmic acceleration can not be explained by a simple cosmological constant or constant vacuum energy. We need some additional odd properties such as the existence of evolving dark energy (DE) with energy-density fine tuned to be twice that of dark matter today. We show here that we can instead explain cosmic acceleration without DE (or modified gravity) assuming that the causal scale is smaller than the observable Universe today. Such scale corresponds to half the sky at z=1 and 60 degrees at z=1100, which is consistent with the anomalous lack of correlations observed in the CMB.

Cosmology: dark energy, cosmic background radiation, cosmological parameters, early Universe, inflation

1 Introduction

One of the most striking changes to Newton’s gravity proposed by Einstein is that energy gravitates. Scientists have since been wondering if vacuum energy ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} (vacuum fluctuations, zero-point fluctuations, quantum vacuum, dark energy or aether) could also gravitate. Measurements of cosmic acceleration (see e.g. Planck Collaboration et al. 2018; Abbott et al. 2019; Tutusaus et al. 2017) point to a model with Λ\Lambda, that we refer to as Λ\LambdaCDM and could be interpreted as a consequence of the gravity of ρvac\rho_{\rm vac}. Even while the accuracy and precision of measurements have greatly improved in the last years, the mean values of cosmological parameters have remained similar for well over a decade (see e.g. Gaztañaga et al. 2006; Gaztañaga et al. 2009). Eq.1-3 are a mathematical extrapolation. A physical model requires a mechanism to produce homogeneity that respects causality. Note that the Lorentz invariance or covariance of the field equations in General Relativity (GR) is not enough to warrant that a given solution has a causal structure (see Minguzzi & Sanchez 2006; Howard 2010 and references therein). This is not the case for the FLRW model, because ρ\rho and pp are the same everywhere at any fix cosmic time, and this can not have causal explanation for a Universe that has a finite age. If we want a causal explanation for our Universe we can not find the solution directly solving the Cauchy problem in GR, because we are not allowed to setup acausal initial conditions for such a problem. The only way to do this is to setup initial conditions that are random with no correlations. This is what happens in the realm of vacuum quantum fluctuations under Heisenberg uncertainty, which do not require a causal mechanism to exist. Inflation could them produce a large and homogeneous universe out of these initial quantum fluctuations. But even for such a case, there is a finite causal scale associated with the duration of inflation.

Regardless of the details of inflation, a Universe of finite age will only be causally homogeneous for scales smaller than some cut-off χ<χ§\chi<\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}. We need a boundary condition at χ=χ§\chi=\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} to account for the lack of causality (and therefore homogeneity) at larger scales. 2 Gauss’s law

A key property of Gravity is Gauss’s law. The acceleration g→\vec{\mathrm{g}} created by a point mass at distance r→\vec{\mathrm{r}} is such that a spherical shell of arbitrary radial density ρ⁡(r)\rho(r) produces a field which is identical to a point source of equal mass mm in its center. This condition alone can be used to define gravity in Classical Mechanics, but the solution is more general than Newton’s law (see Appendix A):

∇2ϕ\displaystyle\nabla^{2}\phi =\displaystyle= 4​π​G​ρm−Λ.\displaystyle 4\pi G\rho_{m}-\Lambda. (6)

Using Stokes Theorem, the gravitational field produces a flux around 2D closed surface ∂V\partial V:

Φ=∮∂Vdr→g→=−∫VdV(4πGρm−Λ)\Phi=\oint_{\partial V}~d\vec{\mathrm{r}}~\vec{\mathrm{g}}=-\int_{V}dV\left(4\pi G~\rho_{m}-\Lambda\right) (7)

so the flux only depends on the total mass (and Λ\Lambda) inside the boundary ∂V\partial V. Note the second term with Λ\Lambda in Eq.6-7 which corresponds to Hooke’s law, i.e. proportional to distance. These of course are the same equations that come from GR in the Newtonian limit (see below). One can then argue that Λ\Lambda is just part of the laws of gravity, as it is allowed by the symmetries of both GR and Classical Gravity. Current cosmological observations clearly indicate that Λ≠0\Lambda\neq 0. There is therefore no need for a search for some more exotic ’Dark Energy” explanation. For this to be confirmed it would good if this interpretation can be used to predict the actual value measured for the cosmic acceleration, which is something that we do in section 4.2.

Physicist assume that particles should be free at infinity, because of lack of causality. This is why boundary terms are usually neglected at infinity. In the same spirit, we will require here that test particles should be free (g→=0\vec{\mathrm{g}}=0) or more relevant for a fluid: that boundary terms should be zero (i.e. the flux Φ=0\Phi=0), when outside causal contact, r>r§r>r_{\lx@sectionsign}. For r§⇒∞r_{\lx@sectionsign}\Rightarrow\infty this condition requires Λ⇒0\Lambda\Rightarrow 0, as otherwise g→\vec{\mathrm{g}} and Φ\Phi diverge. Observational evidence that Λ≠0\Lambda\neq 0 may then indicate that r§r_{\lx@sectionsign} is finite. This agrees with the finite age of the Universe. From Eq.7 the boundary condition Φ⁡(r>r§)=0\Phi(r>r_{\lx@sectionsign})=0, implies:

Λ=4​π​G​ρm​(r<r§)\Lambda=4\pi G\rho_{m}(r<r_{\lx@sectionsign}) (8)

which is clearly related to the coincidence problem: ρΛ∼2​ρm\rho_{\Lambda}\sim 2\rho_{m}. Lets next explore this same argument in GR. For this we first need to see what is the relativistic version of Eq.6-7.

3.2 Causal Boundary condition

As mentioned in the Introduction, scales larger that χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} can have no effect on the metric or the curvature of the universe around us. Mathematically, this appears as retarder Green functions (ϕ⁡(χ,t)=ϕ⁡(χ−c​t)\phi(\chi,t)=\phi(\chi-ct)) as solutions to the wave equation Eq.14 (or Eq.11) with appropriate boundary conditions (typically χ§→∞\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\rightarrow\infty). This could result in a non-homogeneous solution for the metric of the Universe on very large scales (see Gaztañaga 2019). An observer situated at the edge of our causal boundary will find a similar solution, but could measure different cosmological parameters, because she sees a different patch of the initial conditions. There should be a smooth background across disconnected regions with an infrared cutoff in the spectrum of inhomogeneities for χ>χ§\chi>\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}. Solutions in different regions could be matched as in Sanghai & Clifton 2015.

We usually assume that particles should be free at infinity, because of lack of causality: if there is no cause there should not be any effect. This is why boundary terms are usually set to zero at infinity. For example, to reproduce the field equations Eq.9 in GR, from an action principle we need to neglect these boundary terms (e.g. Landau & Lifshitz 1971; Weinberg 1972). 4.1 Inflation and the coincidence problem

Do the results in previous section, e.g. Eq.23, depend on the observer? She will conclude that χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} is larger than the observable universe at z=9z=9 and will predict that that cosmic acceleration will happen in the future when the horizon reaches χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}. This shows how aia_{i} determines χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}, while aea_{e} determines when it re-enters the horizon (see Fig.2), and therefore how large ρ§\rho_{\lx@sectionsign} is. The Hubble rate during inflation HIH_{I} is proportional to the energy of inflation. During reheating this energy is converted into radiation: HI2≃Ωr​H02​ae−4H_{I}^{2}\simeq\Omega_{r}~H_{0}^{2}~a_{e}^{-4}, with ae≡eN​aia_{e}\equiv e^{N}a_{i}. We can combine with Eq.25 to find:

ai​χ§=HiHI​e−2​N​Ωr1/2​(χ§2​H0/c)≃4×108​lPlancka_{i}\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}=\frac{H_{i}}{H_{I}}e^{-2N}~\Omega_{r}^{1/2}~(\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}^{2}H_{0}/c)\simeq 4\times 10^{8}~l_{\rm Planck} (26)

where for the second equality we have used the canonical value of N≃60N\simeq 60 and Hi≃HIH_{i}\simeq H_{I}, which also yields ai≃1.56×10−53a_{i}\simeq 1.56\times 10^{-53} and HI∼1010H_{I}\sim 10^{10} GeV. The condition ai​χ§>lPlancka_{i}\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}>l_{\rm Planck} requires N<70N<70, close to the value found in Dodelson & Hui 2003. Thus, the whole causal size of our Universe χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} could result from a quantum fluctuation at the Planck scale lPlanckl_{\rm Planck}. Such vacuum fluctuation could generate an inflationary expansion. After N≃70N\simeq 70 e-folds inflation ends with reheating, which results into matter and radiation today. Thus, this model links the cosmological constant scale to the Planck scale via inflation.

4.2 Implications for CMB

The (look-back) comoving distance to the surface of last scattering a∗≃9.2×10−4a_{*}\simeq 9.2\times 10^{-4} (Planck Collaboration et al. 2018) is χC​M​B=η⁡(1)−η⁡(a∗)≃3.145​cH0\chi_{CMB}=\eta(1)-\eta(a_{*})\simeq 3.145~\frac{c}{H_{0}}. This is similar to our estimate for χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} in Eq.23. Thus, we would expect to see no correlations in the CMB on angular scales θ>θ§≡χ§χC​M​B≃60\theta>\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\equiv\frac{\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}}{\chi_{CMB}}\simeq 60 degrees for ΩΛ=Ω§≃0.7\Omega_{\Lambda}=\Omega_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 0.7. The lack of structure seen in the CMB on these large scales is one of the well known anomalies in the CMB data, see Schwarz et al. 2016 and references therein. This lack of correlations above 60 deg. has been interpreted as a universe with non trivial topology (Luminet et al. 2003). Fig.3 shows a comparison of the measured CMB temperature correlations (points with error-bars) with the Λ\LambdaCDM prediction for an infinite Universe (continuous line). There is a very clear discrepancy which Copi et al. 2009 estimates to happen in only 0.025 per cent of the realizations of the infinite Λ\LambdaCDM model. The significance of this discrepancy is model dependent. If errors are estimated from the data (and not from the model) Λ\LambdaCDM is strongly ruled out by this measurement (Gaztañaga et al. 2003). Even assuming a Λ\LambdaCDM model, the lack of large scale correlations in w2​(θ)w_{2}(\theta) represent an odd alignment of lower order multipoles of the angular power spectrum clc_{l} (Schwarz et al. 2016).

A small causal universe also predicts similar lack of correlation for CMB polarization measurements. We also expect variations of clc_{l} on smaller scales (e.g. around the first acoustic peak l≃200l\simeq 200) for clc_{l} estimated over different regions of the sky (separated by θ>60\theta>60 deg.), which is another known CMB anomaly (Planck Collaboration et al. 2014). Early evidence for these variations (Gaztanaga et al. 1998) were interpreted as non-Gaussian initial conditions.

We can also predict ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda} from the lack of CMB correlations. From Fig.3 we roughly estimate θ§≃60±3\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 60\pm 3 deg. to find (using Eq.22) ΩΛ=0.7±0.1\Omega_{\Lambda}=0.7\pm 0.1. (the larger the angle the smaller ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda}). But note that this rough estimate does not take into account the foreground (late) ISW and lensing effects (Fosalba et al. 2003; Das & Souradeep 2014), which add non primordial correlations to the largest scales. This requires further investigation. Also note that this estimate for ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda} corresponds to the size of disconnected regions at the location of the CMB, which might be slightly different to the value near us, as we see a different patch of the primordial Universe (see bellow). Note also that there are temperature differences on scales larger θ§\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}, but they are not correlated, as expected in causality disconnected regions. Nearby regions are connected which creates a smooth transition across disconnected regions.

5 Discussion and Conclusions

Λ\LambdaCDM in Eq.1-3 assumes that ρ\rho is constant everywhere at a fixed comoving time. This requires acausal initial conditions (Brandenberger 2017) unless there is inflation, where a tiny homogeneous and causally connected patch, the Causal Universe χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}, was inflated to be very large today. Regions larger than χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} are out of causal contact. Here we require that test particles become free (or the relativistic flux is zero) as we approach χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}. No cause should produce no effect. This leads to Eq.17, which is the main result in this paper. If we ignore the vacuum, this condition requires: Λ=8​π​G​ρ§\Lambda=8\pi G\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}, where ρ§\rho_{\lx@sectionsign} is the matter and radiation inside χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} (Eq.20). For an infinite Universe (χ§→∞\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\rightarrow\infty) we have ρ§⇒0\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}\Rightarrow 0 which requires Λ⇒0\Lambda\Rightarrow 0. This is also what we find in classical gravity with a Λ\Lambda term, because Hooke’s term diverges at infinity (see Eq.6). So the fact that Λ≠0\Lambda\neq 0 could indicate that χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} is not infinite. Adding vacuum ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} does not change this argument because ρΛ≡Λ/8​π​G+ρvac=ρ§\rho_{\Lambda}\equiv\Lambda/8\pi G+\rho_{\rm vac}=\rho_{\lx@sectionsign} turns out to be independent of ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} (see Eq.19). Thus, whether the causal size of the Universe is finite or not, ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} can not gravitate! The cancellation between Λ\Lambda and ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} is a direct consequence of the boundary condition and it also implies that deSitter Universe (empty with a cosmological constant) is not causal (it produces curvature even when empty) and therefore not a physical model.

For constant vacuum (ω≡p/ρ=−1\omega\equiv p/\rho=-1), we find χ§≃3​c/H0\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 3c/H_{0} for Ω§=ΩΛ≃0.7\Omega_{\lx@sectionsign}=\Omega_{\Lambda}\simeq 0.7. We can also estimate χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} as c/(ai​Hi)c/(a_{i}H_{i}) when inflation begins, see Eq.25. After inflation χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} freezes out until it re-enters causality at a§≃0.93a_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 0.93, close to now (a=1a=1). This starts a new inflation (as ρΛ=ρ§>ρm\rho_{\Lambda}=\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}>\rho_{m}) which keeps the causal boundary frozen. Thus a finite χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} explains why ρΛ≃2​ρm\rho_{\Lambda}\simeq 2\rho_{m}. It also predicts that CMB temperature should not be correlated above θ>θ§≃60\theta>\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 60 deg. A prediction that matches observations (see Fig.3). This is one of the well known anomalies measured in the CMB. One would also expect the CMB spectrum to be anisotropic on the largest scales, which is another well known measured anomaly (see Planck Collaboration et al. 2014). One can reverse this argument to use the lack of CMB correlations above θ§≃60\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 60 deg, to estimate χ§≃θ§​χC​M​B\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq\theta_{\lx@sectionsign}\chi_{CMB}. Together with condition ρ§=ρΛ\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}=\rho_{\Lambda}, this provides a prediction of ΩΛ≃0.7±0.1\Omega_{\Lambda}\simeq 0.7\pm 0.1, which is independent of other measurements for ΩΛ\Omega_{\Lambda}. More work is needed to account for the late ISW and lensing and to interpret the CMB measurements with a metric that is not homogeneous (Gaztañaga 2019).

Note that because the Universe is not strictly homogeneous outside a causal region, the causal boundary for observers far away from us could be slightly different from ours, because they see a different patch of the Universe which could have slightly different energy content. Continuity across nearby disconnected regions forces these differences to be small, but it is impossible to quantify this without a model for the initial conditions and a better understanding of the process that generates the primordial homogeneity. In general such differences could affect structure formation, galaxy evolution and CMB observations. The fact that we can measure a concordance picture from different observations with the Λ\LambdaCDM model indicates that these differences must be small. But tensions between measurements of cosmological parameters (or fundamental constants of nature) from very different redshifts (eg between CMB and local measuremnts) or different parts of the sky at high redhifts (such as dipolar variation of fine structure constant in Webb et al. 2011) could be related to such in-homogeneities, rather than to evolution of the Dark Energy (DE) equation of state or other more exotic explanations.

For χ§>>3​c/H0\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}>>3c/H_{0} we can not explain cosmic acceleration with ω=−1\omega=-1, because the resulting ρΛ\rho_{\Lambda} in Eq.20 would be very small. We need evolving DE with equation of state ωD​E>−1\omega_{DE}>-1 and ρΛ=ρD​E\rho_{\Lambda}=\rho_{DE} today (see Appendix B). But DE gives no clue as to why ρD​E≃2​ρm\rho_{DE}\simeq 2\rho_{m} today and can not explain the anomalous lack of CMB correlations at large scales. We can apply Occam’s razor to argue that there is no need for DE or Modify Gravity. Comparing Fig.1 with Fig.2, we can see that the measured cosmic acceleration today can be explained by a finite causal scale χ§≃3​c/H0\chi_{\lx@sectionsign}\simeq 3c/H_{0}, which is expected for Universe with a finite age and a mechanism like inflation.

We have speculated that the finite causal size of our universe could result from a quantum fluctuation at the Planck scale lPlanckl_{\rm Planck} which produces an inflated expansion by a factor ≃e70\simeq e^{70} leading to a reheating into matter and radiation today. This eventually results into late time cosmic acceleration. The existence of such finite causal scale can be tested further with observations of the variation of cosmological or fundamental parameters over cosmological scales.

Appendix A Hooke’s law

In Newton’s gravity, a point mass mm generates a radial acceleration g→=−∇→ϕ=−Gmr→/r3\vec{g}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi=-Gm\vec{r}/r^{3}, where ϕ\phi is the Newtonian potential. The Λ\Lambda term in GR’s field equations, Eq.9, corresponds here to an additional term which is linear in rr, as in Hooke’s law:

g→=−∇→​ϕ=g→N​e​w​t​o​n+g→H​o​o​k​e=−(Gr3+G2)​m​r→\vec{g}=-\vec{\nabla}\phi=\vec{g}_{Newton}+\vec{g}_{Hooke}=-\left(\frac{G}{r^{3}}+G_{2}\right)~m~\vec{r} (27)

Hooke’s constant, G2G_{2}, can be related to Λ\Lambda, as we will see below. This Hooke’s term is unique in that it is the only distance dependence (other than the inverse square law) that has a key property for gravity: that a spherical mass shell of arbitrary density ρ⁡(R)\rho(R) produces a gravitational field which is identical to a point source of equal mass in its center:

g→H​o​o​k​e=−G2∫s​h​e​l​ld3R(r→−R→)ρ(R)=−G2mr→\vec{g}_{Hooke}=-G_{2}\int_{shell}d^{3}R~(\vec{r}-\vec{R})~\rho(R)=-G_{2}m~\vec{r} (28)

where R→\vec{R} covers the shell and r→\vec{r} is some position outside. This is a key property of gravity, needed to treat the Universe as a whole and sustain Gauss’s and Birkhoff’s theorems. Thus in Classical Mechanics the above law of gravity Eq.(27) is consistent with the symmetries and observations, as long as G2G_{2} is small enough. In fact Newton, and other scientist, had already noticed this, but did not consider Hooke’s term for lack of observational evidence (Calder & Lahav 2008, and references therein).

Notice how Eq.27 diverges for r⇒∞r\Rightarrow\infty. We expect instead that g→⇒0\vec{g}\Rightarrow 0, as particles should be free at infinity (as there could be no causal connection). This explains why on theoretical grounds we would expect G2=0G_{2}=0 even if this relation is allowed by the symmetries of the problem. On the other hand, if causality ends at some finite distance r§r_{\lx@sectionsign}, as the Universe has a finite age, then we see that Newton’s law alone can not describe a causal gravitational interaction. We need to also have Hooke’s law. Requiring that gravitational forces g→\vec{g} in Eq.27 vanished at the causal boundary r§r_{\lx@sectionsign}:

g→​(r=r§)=0⇒G2=−4​π​G3​V§\vec{g}(r=r_{\lx@sectionsign})=0~~\Rightarrow~~G_{2}=-\frac{4\pi G}{3V_{\lx@sectionsign}} (29)

so that Hooke’s gravitational force is repulsive and only becomes comparable to Newton’s gravity for separations comparable to r§r_{\lx@sectionsign}.

We can estimate the flux using Eq.(27) to find:

Φ=∮∂Vd​r→​g→=−4​π​G​mV−3​V​G2​m§\Phi=\oint_{\partial V}~d\vec{\mathrm{r}}~\vec{\mathrm{g}}=-4\pi Gm_{V}-3VG_{2}m_{\lx@sectionsign} (30)

where mVm_{V} is the mass inside VV and m§m_{\lx@sectionsign} is all the mass in all the (causally connected) universe. As expected, Φ=0\Phi=0 in Eq.30 reproduces Eq.29 for V=V§V=V_{\lx@sectionsign}. For the Newtonian term, masses outside VV do not contribute to Φ\Phi because for a small cone center outside and crossing VV the in going flux exactly cancels with the outgoing flux (as the inverse square law in g→\vec{g} compensates the increase in the area crossed then the cone goes inside to when is going outside). This is not the case for the Hooke’s law, where the difference in flux is just proportional to the volume inside VV. As all the masses contribute equally, we have:

ΦH​o​o​k​e(V)=−G2∑mm(r→)∮∂V(R→−r→)dA→=−3VG2m§\Phi_{Hooke}(V)=-G_{2}\sum_{m}~m(\vec{r})~\oint_{\partial V}~(\vec{R}-\vec{r})~d\vec{A}=-3VG_{2}m_{\lx@sectionsign} (31)

where the total mass is m§=∑mmm_{\lx@sectionsign}=\sum_{m}m. This result is also true in arbitrary number of dimensions (see Wilkins 1986)). This explains why Hooke’s law behaves like vacuum energy (i.e. like negative pressure: ω=−1\omega=-1) or Λ\Lambda in GR: the density remains constant as we increase the volume! Comparing Eq.30 with Eq.7 we have:

Λ=−3​G2​m§=4​π​G​ρ§\Lambda=-3G_{2}m_{\lx@sectionsign}=4\pi G\rho_{\lx@sectionsign} (32)

where in the last step we have also used Eq.29, or equivalently Φ=0\Phi=0 in Eq.30. This of course agrees with Eq.8, which shows that Eq.27 and Eq.6 are equivalent.

This result provides a Classical Physic’s interpretation of the cosmological constant: it is just related to Hooke’s constant G2G_{2} and the total mass m§m_{\lx@sectionsign} in the (causal) universe. Note how Λ\Lambda can be zero if either G2=0G_{2}=0 or if m§=0m_{\lx@sectionsign}=0. Note also how G2=0G_{2}=0 for infinite volume and in this case Λ=0\Lambda=0 because m§/V§m_{\lx@sectionsign}/V_{\lx@sectionsign} also goes to zero. This relation establishes a clear connection between the value of Λ\Lambda and the matter-energy content in the Universe, as requiered by causality. It also gives new light into Mach’s Principle.

Appendix B Effective Dark Energy (DE)

Here we generalize the results of section 4 to the case with DE. If vacuum energy suffers a phase transition or changes with time, as could have happened during inflation, the cancellation presented in section 3.3 will not happen (because ω≠−1\omega\neq-1) and an evolving ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} (which we usually call Dark Energy) could contribute to the effective value of ρΛ\rho_{\Lambda}. Consider the general case of DE after inflation:

ρD​E​(a)\displaystyle\rho_{DE}(a) =\displaystyle= ρvac+ρD​E​a−3​(1+ω)\displaystyle\rho_{\rm vac}+\rho_{DE}~a^{-3(1+\omega)} (33)
pD​E​(a)\displaystyle p_{DE}(a) =\displaystyle= −ρvac+ω​ρD​E​a−3​(1+ω),\displaystyle-\rho_{\rm vac}+\omega~\rho_{DE}~a^{-3(1+\omega)},

where (by definition) only one component of DE is evolving. We then have from Eq.17 and Eq.4:

ρΛ=ρ§+ρD​E​[1+1+3​ω2​a^§−3​(1+ω)].\rho_{\Lambda}={\rho}_{\lx@sectionsign}+\rho_{DE}[1+\frac{1+3\omega}{2}\hat{a}_{\lx@sectionsign}^{-3(1+\omega)}]. (34)

where a^§\hat{a}_{\lx@sectionsign} is some mean value of aa in the past light-cone of a§a_{\lx@sectionsign} in Eq.22. This reduces to ρΛ=ρ§\rho_{\Lambda}=\rho_{\lx@sectionsign} for ω=−1\omega=-1. For a§⇒∞a_{\lx@sectionsign}\Rightarrow\infty we have ρ§⇒0\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}\Rightarrow 0 because ρm​(a)\rho_{m}(a) and ρr​(a)\rho_{r}(a) tend to zero as we increase a§a_{\lx@sectionsign}. The same happens with a^§−3​(1+ω)\hat{a}_{\lx@sectionsign}^{-3(1+\omega)} for ω>−1\omega>-1, so that:

ρΛ=ρD​E​for​a§⇒∞&ω>−1.\rho_{\Lambda}=\rho_{DE}~~\rm{for}~~{a}_{\lx@sectionsign}\Rightarrow\infty~~\rm{\&}~~\omega>-1. (35)

So evolving DE could produce the observed cosmic acceleration in an infinitely large Universe. This solution does not explain why ρΛ=ρD​E≃2​ρm\rho_{\Lambda}=\rho_{DE}\simeq 2\rho_{m}. The original motivation to introduce DE was to explain how vacuum energy ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} could be as small as the measured ρΛ\rho_{\Lambda} (Weinberg 1989; Huterer & Turner 1999). But we have shown in Section 3.3 that the causal boundary condition explains why ρvac\rho_{\rm vac} does not contribute to ρΛ\rho_{\Lambda} and also results in ρΛ≃2​ρm\rho_{\Lambda}\simeq 2\rho_{m}. This removes the motivation to have DE, as it represents an unnecessary complication of the model (Occam’s razor).

If observations find ω\omega to be significantly larger than ω=−1\omega=-1 this will indicate that something like DE exist. The actual measured value of ω\omega will not directly tell us the size of χ§\chi_{\lx@sectionsign} unless we also have some model for ρD​E\rho_{DE}. But very accurate measurements for the evolution of ρΛ\rho_{\Lambda} might actually be able to separate a component that is constant (i.e. ρ§\rho_{\lx@sectionsign}) from a component that is evolving (i.e. DE).
