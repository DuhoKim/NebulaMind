IS IT POSSIBLE TO CREATE 
A UNIVERSE IN THE LABORATORY 
BY QUANTUM TUNNELING? 
Edward Farhi. Alan H. Guth. and Jemal Guven 
ABSTRACT 
We explore the possibility that a new universe can be created by producing a small 
bubble of false vacuum. The initial bubble is small enough to be produced without 
an initial singularity. but classically it could not become a universe- instead it 
would reach a maximum radius and then collapse. We investigate the possibil-
ity that quantum effects allow the bubble to tunnel into a larger bubble. of the 
same mass. which would then classically evolve to become a new universe. The 
calculation of the tunneling amplitude is attempted. in lowest order semiclassical 
approximation (in the thin-wall limit), using both a canonical and a functional inte-
gral approach. The canonical approach is found to have flaws. attributable to our 
method of spacetime slicing. The functional integral approach ~ds to a Euclidean 
interpolating solution that is not a manifold. To describe it. we define an object 
which we call a "pseudomanifold". and give a prescription to define its action. 
We conjecture that the tunneling probability to produce a new universe can be 
approximated using this action. and we show that this leads to a plausible result. 
I. 
INTRODUCTION 
In this paper we address the question of whether the laws of physics. as we know them. 
allow for the possibility that human beings could create a new universe.1 
This question may at first seem absurd. and it would be absurd to ask this question 
in the context of the standard big bang model. In that picture the visible universe began. 
say at t ~ 1 sec. with about 1032 solar masses of material. Inflationary cosmology. on the 
other hand. gives a radically different answer to the question of what it takes to produce 
a universe. For inflation at a typical grand unified theory scale of 1014 GeV. for example. 
the universe emerges at t ~ 10-35 sec from a region of false vacuum with a radius of only 
,..... 10-24 em and a mass of only """ 10 kg. This list of ingredients is so modest that the 
notion of a human-made universe can be seriously entertained. 
The achievement of a mass density of ,.... 1076 gfcm 3 is admittedly somewhat beyond 
our technology- it is in fact well beyond the range of any foreseeable technology. 
For 
purposes of discussion. however. we will assume that some civilization in the distant future 
may have the necessary capabilities. We will focus only on the question of principle, the 
question of whether the possibility of creating a new universe is excluded by the laws of 
physics . 
While the question is interesting in its own right. it is also a useful device to focus 
attention on the minimal conditions necessary for a universe to be created. either in the

In addition. the problem is a very interesting test of our 
understanding of quantum gravity. Since there is no well-formulated theory of quantum 
gravity it is useful to have specific models in which calculations can be attempted. We look 
at a semiclassical approximation to quantum gravity in two ways: canonically and through 
the functional integral. In both cases we encounter results which we do not fully understand. 
The functional integral approach. however, gives a result that we feel is plausible. and likely 
to be correct. 
Specifically, we study the possibility that a new universe can be created by producing 
a spherically symmetric bubble of false vacuum (i.e .. a metastable state in which a scalar 
field has an expectation value that differs from its vacuum value). which will then undergo 
inflation. After the bubble has settled into the true ground state phase. the new universe 
would evolve much like our own universe is believed to have evolved after its period of 
inflationary growth. For simplicity. we will assume throughout the paper that the wall of the 
bubble is small compared to any other relevant dimension (thin-wall approximation). so that 
it can be described by a single coordinate. its radius. 
We will not discuss the details of how a false vacuum bubble might be produced, except 
to mention here that there are at least two possible mechanisms: one involves supercooling. 
and the other involves the compression of fermions. The supercooling mechanism would 
work in a particle theory in which at high temperatures the scalar field associated with 
the false vacuum undergoes a phase transition. acquiring an expectation value at or near 
its expectation value in the false vacuum. If the false vacuum is sufficiently stable. then 
the scalar field could be trapped in the false vacuum state if a region of space in the high 
temperature phase was cooled rapidly enough. The fermion compression mechanism would 
work in a particle theory in which the vacuum expectation value of the scalar field gives rise 
to a fermion mass. A high density of fermions would then exert a ~force" on the scalar field 
expectation value. driving it near the false vacuum value where the fermion mass is reduced. 
Due to the presence of the fermions. however, such a state is not yet a false vacuum- it 
would in fact have a positive pressure. A false vacuum. with its characteristically negative 
pressure. could be obtained if the fermions were rapidly swept away. 
Classically, i.e. in the absence of quantum mechanics. a variety of authors2- 1 have 
solved Einstein's equations for a spherical region of false vacuum sitting in an asymptotically 
flat empty space. (Our discussion follows most closely that of Ref. [6].) The true vacuum 
region is described by the Schwarzschild geometry. with a mass M which is by definition 
equal to the total energy of the bubble. Inside the bubble the geometry is de Sitter space. 
with a Hubble constant x given by x2 = 87rGptf3. where Pt is the false vacuum energy 
density. The false vacuum is separated from the true vacuum by a thin wall with energy per 
unit area a. (Pt and a are taken as fixed constants.) Einstein's equations at the wall imply 
a set of junction conditions.s which in turn lead to the equation of motion for the wall. 
The behavior of the bubble wall trajectory depends on the mass M of the bubble. If 
M is larger than a critical mass Mer (which depends on Pf and a). then the radius of the 
bubble wall (and. accordingly, the volume of the bubble) starts from zero and then grows 
without bound. The bubble does not. however. expand into the external true vacuum region. 
In fact, from the point of view of an external observer. the expanding bubble always remains

The exact solution referred to above has the undesirable feature of beginning with 
an initial singularity. An initial singularity is a point in spacetime at which at least one 
backward-going null geodesic terminates. Such a geodesic has no history whatever prior 1 
to the singularity, and therefore the presence or absence of a light ray traveling along the 
geodesic cannot be determined by initial conditions set prior to the singularity. For the exact I 
solutions. the singularity takes the form of an initial spacelike boundary of the manifold. t 
marking the terminal point for any backward-going timelike or null trajectory that intersects til 
it. The singularity in this case is also a place of infinite gravitational force, and clearly 
represents a singular initial value configuration. We therefore conclude that any plausible 
scheme to create a universe in the laboratory must avoid an initial singularity. 
Thus. we are lead to ask whether it is possible for a system with nonsingular initial 
data to evolve so that its late-time behavior could closely resemble the late-time behavior 
of the exact (M > Mer) solutions discussed above. As an analogy. note that an eternal 
black hole begins with an initial singularity. but that a black hole solution with very similar 
late-time behavior can be obtained from the nonsingular initial conditions of stellar collapse. 
For the false vacuum bubble, however. the initial singularity appears to be essential, at least 
at the level of classical physics. Making certain weak assumptions. it has been shown9 that 
if the bubble radius grows large (greater than 1/x). then the Penrose theorem1° implies that 
the spacetime must have emerged from an initial singularity. The proof assumes that the 
spacetime geometry is governed fully by classical general relativity. 
In this paper we will discuss whether this obstacle might be overcome by quantum 
tunneling. We will consider bubbles of mass M <Mer: for any such M there are two types 
of solutions. which we call types (a) and (b). For solutions of type (a), the radius starts 
at zero. reaches a maximum value. and then decreases again to zero. These solutions are 
influenced mainly by the pressure gradient. which points inward from the zero pressure true 
vacuum toward the negative pressure false vacuum. A solution of type (b) can be described 
roughly. at a fixed time. as more than half of a closed universe. connected to the asymptotic 
space by a neck. This solution begins at an asymptotically large radius in the asymptotic 
past. shrinks to a minimum radius, and then grows again without bound. At large times this 
solution closely resembles the solutions forM> Mer that were discussed earlier, and would 
grow to become a universe. The Penrose theorem. however, implies that these solutions 
must also begin from a singular spacetime. For solutions of type (a). the Penrose theorem 
may or may not apply. depending on the mass and on the stage of evolution of the bubble. 
We are primarily interested in creating a bubble during its period of expansion. We will show 
in Sec. II that the Penrose theorem applies to an expanding type (a) solution if and only if 
M >Ms. where Ms is the mass of a (type (a)) solution for which the maximum radius 
is precisely equal to the Schwarzschild radius. (Ms depends on Pf and u. and in all cases 
Ms <Mer.) 
We therefore consider an initial false vacuum bubble with M < Ms. which can in 
principle be created without an initial singularity. (Note that as M gets smaller and smaller. 
the role of gravity becomes increasingly irrelevant. Thus the creation of the bubble reduces 
to a problem involving only quantum field theory in flat space.) While classically such a

bubble would reach a maximum radius and then contract. we would expect that there is a 
nonzero probability that it might instead tunnel to a solution of type (b)- the two states 
have the same energy. and are not distinguished by any other conserved quantity. From 
the outside. the bubble would appear to have vanished. leaving behind a black hole of the 
same mass. On the inside. the growing universe would again be susceptible to a classical 
description. just as a particle can be described classically after it has tunneled through a 
barrier and is again in a classically allowed region. The new universe would grow large. but 
the classical argument implying an initial singularity would be invalid. since the spacetime 
took a quantum leap during which it was not governed by Einstein"s equations. 
In this paper we attempt to calculate the tunneling probability. using a semiclassical 
approximation to quantum gravity. However before quantizing a system it is important to 
understand its classical motions. In the next section we review the salient features of the 
classical trajectories of the thin-wall bubble problem. In particular we focus on the ap-
plication of the singularity theorem. identifying those cases where the singularities are a 
necessary part of the spacetime. We then discuss the classical action whose variation gives 
rise to the classical equations of motion. Knowledge of the classical action. and its associ-
ated Lagrangian. is a prerequisite to both the canonical and functional integral quantization 
methods. In Sec. IV we review the semiclassical formalism for quantum tunneling. showing 
that the amplitude is determined to lowest order in h by the action of a Euclidean solution 
that interpolates between the initial and final classical states: in the course of this calculation 
we find a formula for the quantity that must be subtracted from the action to properly define 
its zero point. In Sec. V we estimate the tunneling rate using a functional integral approach. 
Here we assume that the functional integral is dominated by a configuration which is a so-
lution to the Euclidean field equations. In applying this technique to the thin-wall bubble 
problem. we calculate the imaginary-time solution for the trajectory of the bubble wall. We 
find. however. that it turns back on itself in a way that does not allow the construction of an 
interpolating four-dimensional manifold. Nonetheless we present a prescription for evaluating 
the subtracted action for this interpolating object. and we obtain what we feel is a sensible 
answer. This is the main result of our paper. In Sec. VI we discuss the purely canonical ap-
proach to the thin-wall bubble problem. This approach founders almost immediately. but we 
carry through the calculations just to see what we get. In contrast to the functional integral 
result, the answer in this case does not seem sensible. Finally in Sec. VII we summarize our 
conclusions. 
The quantum behavior of false vacuum bubbles has also been discussed in several earlier 
papers. Frolov. Markov. and Mukhanov11 have conjectured that quantum effects give rise to a 
limiting spacetime curvature. and in this context they have discussed a scenario in which black 
hole collapse leads to the production of a new universe. Berezin. Kuzmin. and Tkachev12 
have discussed the tunneling of false vacuum bubbles. using Euclidean semiclassical methods 
similar to the functional integral approach we present in Sec. V. They assumed. however. 
that the Euclidean bubble wall trajectory forms a dosed loop. and they therefore obtained 
results very different from ours. Berezin. Kozimirov. Kuzmin. and Tkachev13 have developed 
a quantum theory of thin-wall bubbles. constructing a Hamiltonian by using the classical 
equations of motion and the criterion that it should numerically equal the mass of the 
false vacuum bubble. Their Hamiltonian is different from the one that we find. however.

Their formalism seems to also have the shortcoming that the quantities O'in and D'out-

treated as parameters and not dynamical variables. Thus. the formalism apparently cannot 
describe motion for which the u"s change sign. whether the motion is classically allowed or 
classically forbidden. This would prevent. for example. a description of tunneling for false 
vacuum bubbles with mass less than Ms. In addition. Goncharov. Linde. and Mukhanov14 
have used stochastic methods to estimate the rate for the nucleation of false vacuum bubbles 
in empty Minkowski space. Unlike semiclassical techniques which attempt to calculate the 
exponent B of the tunneling amplitude Ae~B/h-. the stochastic approach attempts only to 
estimate the magnitude of B. Finally, as we were completing this work we received a preprint 
by Fischler. Morgan. and Polchinski15 which addresses exactly the same question addressed 
here. We will discuss the results of this paper in our conclusion. Sec. VII.

THE SUBTRACTED TUNNELING ACTION 
We would like to quantize the thin-wall bubble whose classical motion we have described 
above. Of course this problem involves gravity. and no tractable theory of quantum gravity 
exists. However. since we have reduced the problem to a single degree of freedom. r. that is 
governed by a known Lagrangian (3.47). the natural place to begin is the quantization of this 
system. Fortunately. we will not be interested in all aspects of this problem; we only wish 
to calculate the first term in the WKB expansion of a particular tunneling rate. Although 
the quantization will be ambiguous due to uncertainties in operator orderings, we will argue 
that these ambiguities do not affect the leading WKB approximation.

call a subtracted action. It is the action of a Euclidean solution that evolves from the initial

a solution) that remains static at the initial state of the tunneling. enduring for the same 
interval of Euclidean time as the solution. In Sees. V and VI we apply this general formalism 
to the thin-wall false vacuum bubble tunneling problem. using a functional integral approach 
(Sec. V) and a purely canonical approach (Sec. VI). We find that each of these approaches

The possibility of a multi-sheeted covering immediately comes to mind. but the pos-
sibility of a nonsingular multi-sheeted covering is excluded by the fact that the underlying

allows us to determine. for any closed path. whether the path returns to the original sheet 
or a different one. If all closed paths can be continuously deformed into a trivial path. as is 
the case here. then all closed paths must return to the original sheet. 
Although a nonsingular multi-sheeted covering is excluded. a new coordinate system

Sitter manifolds. Then. as a path is continuously deformed in the Schwarzschild or de Sitter 
manifold. the image of the path in the new coordinates can jump discontinuously from one 
sheet to another.

possible that they arise as saddle points which are obtained by the distortion of integration

VII. 
CONCLUSIONS 
The inflationary universe model proposes that our universe grew from a tiny inflating 
region of false vacuum. We know. however. that the laws of classical general relativity imply 
that a bubble that grows large enough to become a new universe cannot be produced without 
an initial singularity. In this paper we have asked whether this requirement can be avoided 
by quantum tunneling. Unfortunately we do not have a definitive answer to this question. 
but we have obtained an expression for the tunneling amplitude that seems highly plausible. 
and we conjecture that it is a valid approximation. Thus. we are suggesting that quantum 
effects can very likely avoid the implications of the classical singularity theorems. and that 
the laws of physics as we know them permit in principle the creation of a new universe by 
human initiative. In addressing this question. however. we have uncovered a situation in 
the semiclassical formulation of quantum gravity that appears very novel: we have found 
a tunneling process for which the interpolating Euclidean solution folds back on itself in a 
manner that cannot be interpreted as a manifold. To describe this object we have introduced 
what we call a pseudomanifold. a generalization of a manifold in which each point is assigned 
an integer-valued covering number. The pseudomanifold can also be described by a multi-
sheeted covering that is singular in the sense that .;g. the square root of the determinant 
of the metric. can vanish and change sign. 
The starting point of our investigation was the observation that in the classical thin-wall 
bubble problem there can be two very different types of trajectories with the same mass 1.\1. 
provided that M is less than a critical mass Mer· One type corresponds to a bubble which. 
with some fancy engineering. could in principle be constructed. In fact for .\f very small. 
gravity plays no role and the bubble can be viewed as a conventional flat space false vacuum 
bubble. According to classical physics. this bubble would expand to a maximum radius and 
then collapse. The second corresponds to a bubble which grows arbitrarily large, but which. 
50 
according to classical general relativity. could not be produced without an initial singularity. 
Since the two configurations have the same mass. it is natural to consider the possibility 
that the first configuration could tunnel to become the second. 
Our experience with the WKB method has taught us that Euclidean solutions to classical 
equations of motion can be used to estimate tunneling rates. If we reduce our problem to 
just one degree of freedom, the radius of the bubble wall, we can then find an imaginary time 
solution which goes through the classically forbidden region. This solution starts from the 
maximum radius of the initial classical trajectory and ends at the minimum radius of the final 
classical trajectory. From this point of view the problem looks very much like an ordinary 
one-dimensional tunneling problem. with the Euclidean solution interpolating between the 
turning points. At this level it appears as if the problem is straightforward. 
When the calculation is continued. however, complications become apparent. We have 
pursued both a canonical and a functional integral approach. finding unexpected results in 
both cases. We believe that our canonical approach is dearly invalid. but we discuss it 
anyway for its pedagogical value. The functional integral approach also has complications 
that we do not fully understand. but it nonetheless leads to a result which is plausibly correct. 
In order to construct a canonical description for the one-degree-of-freedom problem. it is 
necessary to reformulate the action of the full field theory. which is expressed as an integral 
over the four-dimensional spacetime. To do this we adopted a recipe for slicing spacetime. 
Each segment of the trajectory was then associated with a region of spacetime. and the 
action for the segment was taken as the field theory action for the corresponding region. In 
order to maintain explicit time-translation invariance. we chose to slice along hypersurfaces 
of constant Schwarzschild time or static de Sitter time. This prescription. however. works 
only for trajectories that lie in the left or right quadrants of the Kruskal diagram and in the 
left or right quadrants of the de Sitter diagram. 
Pursuing the canonical approach. we found that the expression for the canonical mo-
mentum p(r,T) is noninvertible. so r cannot be expressed as a function of rand p. This 
means that it is impossible to define a Hamiltonian H(r,p). and the canonical formulation 
cannot be completed. It is nonetheless possible to evaluate the action for the solution. and 
we found that the action varies non-monotonically with the bubble mass M. which seems 
very hard to accept. We also found that. for a range of bubble masses (Mv < M < Ms ). 
the canonical momentum p cannot be defined so that it vanishes at both ends of the tunneling 
trajectory. 
The alternative. and in this case more successful approach. is the sum over histories. 
While we cannot even attempt a calculation of the full functional integral. we assume that to 
leading order in h the amplitude can be approximated by semiclassical methods. That is. we 
assume that the amplitude to go from one three-geometry to another is well-approximated 
by e11·d"'- where Je1 is the action of the classical solution to the field equations which 
interpolates between the two three-geometries. If no real-time solution exists then we seek a 
Euclidean four-geometry that solves the imaginary time field equation and whose boundary is 
the two three-geometries of interest. The tunneling amplitude is then estimated as e~lgfh, 
where IE is the properly subtracted classical action of the Euclidean solution- that is. it 
is the action of the solution. minus the action of a configuration that remains static at the 
51

initial state of the tunneling process for the same Euclidean time as the solution requires for 
its transit. 
We have found. however. that no true Euclidean interpolating manifold exists. There 
is no difficulty or ambiguity in analytically continuing the bubble wall trajectory into the 
Euclidean regime. but when this trajectory is plotted on a Euclidean spacetime diagram it is 
found to cross both the initial and final surfaces ofthe tunneling problem. These intersection 
points prevent a conventional manifold interpretation. 
We admit that we are not sure what the absence of a true interpolating manifold implies 
about the tunneling problem. Perhaps it indicates that the stationary phase method has 
failed. perhaps it indicates that one cannot extrapolate the thin-wall approximation into the 
Euclidean regime. or perhaps it is a suggestion that tunneling is for some reason forbidden. 
We find it difficult to believe. however. that the tunneling process is forbidden, since 
there is no barrier to constructing a well-defined manifold (with either Lorentzian or Euclidean 
signature) that interpolates between the initial and final states. Such a manifold is not a 
solution. but it would constitute a path contributing to the functional integral. Furthermore. 
since any small variation about such a path would also contribute. the measure of these 
paths appears naively to be nonzero. The amplitude would then be nonzero unless the 
various paths conspire to cancel each other. as they do for an amplitude that violates a 
conservation principle associated with a symmetry. In the present case. however. there is no 
apparent symmetry or conservation law at work. We therefore conjecture that the tunneling 
process is allowed. and that the semiclassical approximation is valid. 
To characterize the Euclidean interpolation we define a pseudomanifold. which we de-
scribe in two alternative ways. In either case. it is specified in terms of the behavior of a 
hypersurface that evolves continuously through the Euclidean Schwarzschild and de Sitter 
manifolds. beginning at the initial hypersurface of the tunneling process and ending at the fi-
nal hypersurface. In one description each point is assigned a covering number. defined as the 
number of times the point is crossed by the evolving hypersurface. with crossings counting 
positively in one direction and negatively in the other. In the other description we introduce 
new coordinates that give a singular multi-sheeted covering of the original space. In these 
new coordinates it looks very much like a true manifold. except that ..;g is allowed to vanish 
and to change sign. We have given a prescription to define the action for a pseudomanifold. 
which in the singular multi-sheeted description has the same form as the usual expression 
for the action. It is then clear that the variation of this action reproduces the (analytically 
continued) classical equations of motion. 
We have used our definition of the action to estimate the tunneling amplitude as a 
function of the various parameters in the problem. and we have found that it behaves very 
reasonably: the tunneling action decreases monotonically to zero as the bubble mass M 
approaches the critical mass Mer at which tunneling would not be necessary. and it diverges 
monotonically as the gravitational constant G -
0. The action is negative definite by the 
usual sign conventions. but we argue that, regardless of the sign of the action. the tunneling 
probability is always exponentially suppressed.

Even with this small probability. however. there might still be a large probability of an event of 
this sort occurring somewhere in a universe that has undergone a large amount of inflation. 
Thus. the possibility of a chain reaction by which one universe produces more than one 
universe is not obviously ruled out by this estimate. On the other hand. if we are talking 
about human-made universes. then a probability this small must be considered equivalent to 
zero. Thus the production of a universe at the GUT scale seems prohibitively unlikely. but 
it might be possible at energy scales approaching the Planck scale. 
If our functional integral result is correct. then it seems to raise an important issue in 
quantum gravity: how does a pseudomanifold arise in a quantum gravity path integral? It 
might mean that such objects occur in the physical definition of the path integral. or it might 
mean that they arise as saddle points which are obtained by the distortion of integration 
contours in the complex plane. 
Finally we would like to discuss what the production of a new universe would look 
like to us. the creators of the new spacetime. Recall that to produce a new universe it is 
necessary to build very many false vacuum bubbles. each in its expanding phase. Almost 
all of these bubbles will follow their classical trajectories- that is. they will expand to a 
maximum radius and then collapse. To an observer riding along with the wall. the wall is 
seen to pass through the Schwarzschild radius of the bubble in a finite time. However to 
an observer who sits at a fixed radius. beyond the trajectory. the wall takes an infinite time 
to reach the horizon. At late times the wall appears frozen at the horizon. If the observer 
sends in light rays to probe the bubble wall. then the reflected waves are received for all 
time. although they become highly redshifted. 
A few of the bubbles. roughly e- 21• times the number we start with. reach their 
maximum classical radius and then quantum tunnel to a larger radius on the other side of 
the horizon. These are the seeds of the new universes. The time-energy uncertainty relation 
prevents us from saying exactly what this event looks like at the instant it occurs. After 
the tunneling event. however. the bubble trajectory closely resembles that of the classical 
trajectory shown in Fig. 5. Here the horizon is exposed: there is no wall frozen just outside 
of the horizon. If an observer at fixed radius has been illuminating the bubble wall with light 
rays. the reflected rays would cease during the tunneling process. When the bubble tunnels 
it vanishes from your radar screen. 
We must ask. however. whether the vanishing of the bubble is an unmistakable signal 
that a new universe has been produced. Are there other processes that would look the same 
from the outside, but which do not lead to the creation of a new universe? To answer the 
question we need to go beyond the approximations used in this paper. in which the bubble 
motion before and after the tunneling is treated classically. We need instead a full quantum 
description. with the bubble motion represented by the evolution of a wave packet. The 
packet might be highly peaked at the classical trajectory. but the spread of the packet would 
53

nonetheless allow the possibility of evolution very different from classical expectations. Thus. 
there is some probability that reflected photons would cease after a certain time. even if a 
new universe is not created. We have not yet attempted to estimate this probability. 
The lesson is that even if a new universe can be created. we still do not know if this 
spectacular event could be verified.
