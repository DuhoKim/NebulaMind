                                      On the expected anisotropy of radio source counts




                                      G. F. R. Ellis* and J. E. Baldwint Orthodox Academy of Crete,
                                      Kolymbari, Crete



                                      Received 1983 May 31 ; in original form 1983 March 31




                                      Summary. If the standard interpretation of the dipole anisotropy in the
                                      microwave background radiation as being due to our peculiar velocity in a
                                      homogeneous isotropic universe is correct, then radio-source number counts
                                      must show a similar anisotropy. Conversely, determination of a dipole aniso-
                                      tropy in those counts determines our velocity relative to their rest frame;
                                      this velocity must agree with that determined from the microwave back-
                                      ground radiation anisotropy. Present limits show reasonable agreement
                                      between these velocities.



                        1 Introduction

                        The uniformity of the microwave background radiation (MBR) is generally accepted as the
                        most sensitive test of the isotropy of the Universe on very large scales (z « 1500), and
                        the dipole anisotropy of amplitude AT/T ^ 10"3 is widely believed to be due to the
                        combined peculiar motions of the Sun, Galaxy and the Local Group. An independent
                        recent determination of the peculiar velocity of the Local Group with respect to a
                        sample of spiral galaxies with radial velocities less than SOOkrns-1 has been made by
                        Hart & Davies (1982) who obtain a value of Vlg = 436 ±55 km s 1 in a direction 1 = 264°,
                        £ = +45°, in reasonable agreement with the best determination from measurements of
                        the MBR anisotropy of ^LG = 610 ± 50 km s 1 in a direction differing by only 17° (Wilkinson
                        1982). Thus most, and perhaps all, of the MBR anisotropy may be attributed to our
                        motion in a Universe that is at present highly isotropic. The high degree of isotropy
                        of the MBR remaining after correcting for this effect is, however, no guarantee of
                        the isotropy of the Universe at other epochs. In particular, relative motions between
                        different regions of the Universe can arise, giving large observed anisotropies in
                        some model universes. Furthermore, confirmation of the isotropy of the Universe at all
                        epochs is the basis on which we may have confidence in the standard homogeneous and
                        isotropic (Friedmann—Robertson—Walker, FRW) Universe models. Observational tests

                        of the isotropy of the Universe at other epochs are therefore important, even if of lower

                            One observational test of the isotropy which has already been studied is that of the
                        2—20keV X-ray background (Warwick, Pye & Fabian 1980; Protheroe, Wolfendale &
                        Wdowczyk 1.980; Shafer & Fabian 1982). Their analyses of the data provide values of the
                        component of dipole anisotropy measured in the direction of the microwave background
                        anisotropy of 0.23 ± 0.20, 0.61 ± 0.26 and 0.52 ± 0.18 per cent respectively, corresponding
                        to velocities of translation relative to the microwave background of 200 ± 180, 560 ± 230
                        and 475 ± 165 km s_1. There are, however, two difficulties in the interpretation of the
                        X-ray data. The first is the problem of the subtraction of a galactic component. In the
                        models fitted to the data, the contributions are as large as 2.5—7.2 per cent (Warwick et al
                        1980) and 1.1—1.3 per cent (Protheroe et al. 1980) of the total background over the
                        latitude range | h | = 90-20°. Since the distribution of the galactic radiation is unlikely to
                        be symmetric, it is difficult to separate from a true extragalactic dipole anisotropy. The
                        second difficulty is that we do not know what are the constituents of the extragalactic
                        X-ray background and cannot give a specific range of redshifts over which it originates.
                            Radio source number counts, which also provide evidence on the anisotropy, do not
                        suffer from these two difficulties of interpretation.

                        3 Observational limits

                        It is of particular interest to see whether the velocity responsible for the anisotropy in the
                        MBR can be detected in existing or foreseeable measurements of the radio source counts.
                        The most recent estimate of our net velocity relative to the MBR is 372 ± 25 km s_1 in a
                        direction RA = 1 lh.2 ± 0h2, dec = 6° ± 3° (Wilkinson 1982). A detection of the anisotropy
                        in the source counts due to this motion evidently requires observations of large numbers
                        of faint sources to achieve sufficient statistical accuracy; at present such observations exist
                        only at low frequencies. Taking a mean spectral index a of 0.75 and the slope, x, of the
                        integral source counts as 1 (somewhat below the Euclidean value of 1.5), the amplitude of
                        the dipole anisotropy is [2+x(l+ a)](u/c) « 4.6 x 10-3. To measure this at the 3a
                        significance level requires observations of at least n sources in directions along the axis of
                        anisotropy where

                        3(2ln)1/2 = 9.3 x 10'3,       i.e. n~2x 105.

                            Such numbers of sources have not yet been catalogued, but lie within the capabilities
                        of existing telescopes. For instance, such numbers per steradian are reached at S ~ 15 mJy
                        at 150 MHz and ~3 mJy at 1420 MHz. The main instrumental problem lies in maintaining
                        uniformity of calibration to much better than 1 per cent over large angles in the sky.
                           Searches for large-scale anisotropy in the 4C catalogue at 178 MHz (Pilkington &
                        Scott 1965; Gower, Scott & Wills 1967) giving negative results have been published
                        by Holden (1966) and Webster (1976), but without setting any numerical limit on a
                        possible dipole anisotropy. The catalogue is restricted to declinations > —07°. Further-
                        more, Webster’s analysis revealed declination-dependent instrumental effects so that
                        it is possible to make a sensitive analysis only for dipole anisotropies with axes aligned
                        towards Dec 0°. Fortunately the observed MBR anisotropy has its maximum close
                        to this at Dec 6°. Our analysis of the 4C data as tabulated by Holden (1967) gives a
                        dipole term of amplitude (5.6±3.2)x 10“2 in a direction RA 21h. The quoted error
                        is the expected statistical error for the number of sources involved. The corresponding
                        velocity, adopting a = 0.75 and x = 1.5 (appropriate for sources of 2 Jy at the limit of the 4C
                        catalogue), is 3630 ± 2074 km s"1.

                        catalogue was constructed. Scheuer (1967) showed that the probability distribution of the

                        4 Conclusion

                        Anisotropies in radio-source number counts can be used to determine a cosmological
                        standard of rest. Current observations determine it to about ±500 km s"1, but accurate
                        counts of fainter sources will reduce the error to a level comparable to that set by obser-
                        vations of the microwave background radiation. If the standards of rest determined by the
                        MBR and the number counts were to be in serious disagreement, one would have to abandon
                        either

                           (a) the idea that the radio sources are at cosmological distances, or
                           (b) the interpretation of the cosmic microwave radiation as relic radiation from the big
                        bang, or
                           (c) the standard FRW Universe models.

                        Thus comparison of these standards of rest provides a powerful consistency test of our
                        understanding of the Universe.
