# Independent primary-source citation custody: neutron-star mass / Brown–Bethe / CNS chain

## Custody verdict

- **Brown, Lee & Rho’s corrected relation is \(M\gtrsim2M_\odot\), rendered \(M\,≳\,2M_\odot\), not \(M\ge2M_\odot\) and not a strict \(M>2M_\odot\).**[1][7]
- **Their paper directly calls such an observation a “serious obstacle to the BB and CNS scenarios,” while its abstract says it would “put in serious doubt or simply falsify” a four-link chain ending in CNS.**[1][2]
- **Demorest et al.’s \(1.97\pm0.04M_\odot\) central value is below strict 2.00; its 1-σ interval is 1.93–2.01, so it is near/consistent with 2.00 rather than strictly at or above it.**[3][4]
- **Fonseca et al.’s exact notation is \(2.08^{+0.07}_{-0.07}M_\odot\) at 68.3% credibility; its 2.01–2.15 interval clears 2.00 at that level, but the paper’s 95.4% lower bound is 1.95.**[5][6]

## 1. Brown, Lee & Rho, PRL 101, 091101 (2008)

**Bibliography:** G. E. Brown, Chang-Hwan Lee, and Mannque Rho, “Kaon Condensation, Black Holes, and Cosmological Natural Selection,” *Physical Review Letters* **101**, 091101 (2008), DOI **10.1103/PhysRevLett.101.091101**.[2]

**Primary full text:** the accepted/final author manuscript is arXiv:0802.2997v2; APS supplies the version-of-record full text at the DOI.[1][2]

**Relation-sign correction:** APS issued a Publisher’s Note, *PRL* **101**, 119901 (2008), DOI **10.1103/PhysRevLett.101.119901**, saying the original online abstract had a “relation sign misprint.”[7]

### Exact abstract wording

> “It is argued that a well-measured double neutron-star binary in which the two neutron stars are more than 4% different from each other in mass or a massive neutron star with mass \(M\gtrsim2M_\odot\) would put in serious doubt or simply falsify the following chain of predictions: (1) a nearly vanishing vector meson mass at chiral restoration, (2) kaon condensation at a density \(n\sim3n_0\), (3) the Brown-Bethe maximum neutron-star mass \(M_{\max}\approx1.5M_\odot\), and (4) Smolin’s ‘cosmological natural selection’ hypothesis.”[1][2]

The source uses `$M\gsim 2\msun$` and locally defines `\gsim` as “greater than or approx.”[1]

The APS Publisher’s Note visually prints a greater-than sign with an approximation tilde below it, i.e. \(\gtrsim\) / ≳.[7]

Accordingly, `M >= 2`, `M > 2`, and `M ≳ 2` are not exact textual equivalents; only the last preserves the corrected relation.[1][7]

### Direct CNS applicability and exact strength

The body directly states:

> “A firm observation of any type of a neutron star whose mass is greater than \(M_{\max}^{BB}\) or to be safe \(\gtrsim2M_\odot\) would present a serious obstacle to the BB and CNS scenarios.”[1]

Its CNS-specific criterion is a star whose mass “appreciably exceeds” \(M_{\max}^{BB}\), which “would count against the CNS scenario.”[1]

That CNS criterion is not given as a sharp 2.00-solar-mass inequality; \(\gtrsim2M_\odot\) is introduced “to be safe” relative to \(M_{\max}^{BB}\approx1.5M_\odot\).[1]

The more local microphysics claim is:

> “Find a neutron star of mass \(\gtrsim2M_\odot\), whether in binary or otherwise, then it falsifies the VM of HLS theory, which in turn falsifies the kaon condensation at \(\sim3n_0\).”[1]

Therefore the paper itself makes three distinguishable claims: direct falsification of its HLS/low-density-kaon prediction, a “serious obstacle” to BB and CNS, and abstract-level “serious doubt or simply falsify” language for the complete chain.[1]

The unqualified sentence “a 2-solar-mass neutron star falsifies CNS” is too strong because it erases \(\gtrsim\), the disjunctive hedge, the body’s “serious obstacle” wording, and the model-dependent links to CNS.[1]

## 2. Demorest et al. (2010), PSR J1614−2230

**Bibliography:** P. B. Demorest et al., “A two-solar-mass neutron star measured using Shapiro delay,” *Nature* **467**, 1081–1083 (2010), DOI **10.1038/nature09466**.[4]

**Primary full text:** arXiv:1010.5788v1 is the authors’ submitted manuscript; the final Nature abstract is available at the DOI page.[3][4]

Final Nature abstract:

> “We calculate the pulsar mass to be \((1.97\pm0.04)\,M_\odot\), which rules out almost all currently proposed hyperon or boson condensate equations of state.”[4]

Author full text:

> “The implied pulsar mass of \(1.97\pm0.04\,M_\odot\) is by far the highest yet measured with such certainty …”[3]

The uncertainty convention is explicit:

> “Timing values in parentheses represent 1-σ uncertainty in the final digit, as determined by Markov chain Monte Carlo error analysis.”[3]

The central estimate 1.97 is 0.03 below 2.00, and 2.00 lies 0.75σ above that central value.[3][4]

The reported 1-σ interval is [1.93, 2.01], so the measurement does not strictly clear \(M\ge2.00\) by its central value or throughout its quoted interval.[3]

It is nevertheless a “two-solar-mass” star in the paper’s title and fits Brown et al.’s approximate \(\gtrsim2\) language, as well as Brown et al.’s broader alternative condition of exceeding \(M_{\max}^{BB}\).[1][3][4]

## 3. Fonseca et al. (2021), PSR J0740+6620

**Bibliography:** E. Fonseca et al., “Refined Mass and Geometric Measurements of the High-mass PSR J0740+6620,” *The Astrophysical Journal Letters* **915**, L12 (2021), DOI **10.3847/2041-8213/ac03b8**.[6]

**Primary full text:** arXiv:2104.00880v2 is explicitly the final version after referee corrections; the journal article is open at the DOI page.[5][6]

Exact abstract result:

> “…with the pulsar mass \(m_{\rm p}=2.08^{+0.07}_{-0.07}\,M_\odot\) (68.3% credibility) determined by the relativistic Shapiro time delay.”[5]

This is a model-averaged posterior derived from chi-squared-grid likelihoods, uniform priors on the physical parameters, and Bayesian model averaging over three dispersion-measure models.[5]

The paper identifies the reported center as the posterior median and the quoted bounds as a 68.3% credible interval.[5]

The discussion states:

> “Our 68.3% credible interval of the model-averaged \(m_{\rm p}=2.08^{+0.07}_{-0.07}\,M_\odot\) …” and “The 95.4% lower bound on the model-averaged \(m_{\rm p}\), 1.95 \(M_\odot\) …”[5]

The 68.3% interval is [2.01, 2.15], entirely above 2.00, so it clears a strict 2.00 threshold at that quoted credibility level.[5]

It does not clear 2.00 at 95.4% credibility because the paper’s own lower bound at that level is 1.95.[5]

Writing \(2.08\pm0.07\) is numerically equivalent at the rounded precision, but the exact published convention is Bayesian \(2.08^{+0.07}_{-0.07}\) at 68.3% credibility.[5]

## Custody cautions / unresolved context

- Brown et al. test a theoretical chain, not universe reproduction directly; the inference to CNS depends on their Brown–Bethe, kaon-condensation, and maximized-black-hole links.[1]
- Brown et al. limit their CNS discussion to the neutron-star upper-mass prediction and explicitly do not settle the broader merits or foundations of CNS.[1]
- The symbol \(\gtrsim\) provides no formal confidence-level cutoff, so whether a measurement “meets” it cannot be decided like a strict \(\ge\) relation without an added convention.[1][7]
- Demorest’s final abstract says “almost all currently proposed” hyperon or boson-condensate equations of state, not every possible kaon-condensation model.[4]
- No Lana/Kun summaries were used; this report relies on the three primary papers, their author full texts, journal records, and the APS relation-sign correction.
