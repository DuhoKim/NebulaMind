6. Human calibration capacity as the binding constraint

The design, instrument, and sign conventions reported in this paper were frozen under preregistration, and every stage of the pre-image machinery (sample definition, selection, presentation schedule, controls, and validation checks) was executed and passed. The study then halted before image analysis, as the preregistered protocol required once its calibration condition could not be met. This section states why, and reports the reason as a result in its own right: for this class of measurement, the binding constraint is neither sky coverage nor sample size but human calibration capacity.

The frozen estimator is A_L = beta/(2a − 1), where a is the accuracy with which a human observer labels spiral winding direction on real objects drawn properly from the accepted population. Because the denominator rescales observed label fractions into an intrinsic asymmetry, it must be calibrated on the same population and under the same sign conventions as the measurement itself. Two apparently cheap sources for a are excluded by construction. Machine predictions cannot enter it: the estimator exists to test what automated catalogues assert, so calibrating it against their output would be circular. Known-answer synthetic images cannot supply it either: those estimate epsilon, the error term that corrects the measurement for instrument and presentation effects, not the human accuracy on real galaxies that the denominator requires, and substituting one for the other would silently change the estimand. The quantity a must come from people looking at real survey images, and we costed every route to it.

The first route is a single dedicated checker. The frozen presentation schedule contains 850 objects; one calibrated observer working through them would yield a with the full stratified design intact. No such person was available, and the preregistration correctly forbids substituting an uncalibrated one.

The second route is a distributed panel. To protect label quality, no panelist may contribute more than roughly 50 decisions, and each object requires a three-person majority. The floor budget's schedule therefore trebles to 1,860 decisions, which at the per-panelist ceiling demands a minimum of 38 panelists (51 at the inherited budget), each trained and individually calibrated against the frozen conventions before their decisions may count. The panel does not relax the constraint; it re-expresses it, converting one unavailable checker into 38, and adds a recruitment and calibration overhead that the single-checker design was intended to avoid.

The third route is external labels. The modern Galaxy Zoo releases (GZ2, DECaLS, DESI) publish spiral winding tightness (tight, medium, or loose: a pitch-angle classification), not winding direction. Only Galaxy Zoo 1 ever asked its volunteers for clockwise versus anticlockwise, and it fails on three independent grounds: it is SDSS-selected and bright, so it neither covers nor probability-samples the DR10.1-south population; it publishes no known-answer controls from which its labellers' error could be bounded; and its screen-relative sign convention has no publishable anchor to a celestial East-of-North frame, so transferring its labels onto the frozen convention would itself require a new calibration exercise. The 8.67-million-row DESI catalogue, the largest available, consists of model predictions and is excluded from a by the circularity constraint above. No published label set transfers.

The fourth route is to loosen the frozen floors. The design requires at least 10 labels per cell and at least 30 per stratum across 9 strata by 3 bins, forcing at least 270 real labels before controls. Budgets below this are achievable only by deleting strata and bins, that is, by shrinking the population the study covers, which is categorically different from accepting a noisier answer on the same population. Below 120 total decisions nothing is publishable at all, and 120 itself yields only a restricted two-stratum upper limit, not the preregistered claim.

We state the general result plainly. For population-level asymmetries estimated through a human-calibrated denominator, the scarce resource is not area, depth, or objects; the source catalogue holds more galaxies than any plausible budget could label, and the machinery to sample, present, and correct them is validated and waiting. The scarce resource is verified human judgment: of order a thousand calibrated decisions, deliverable either as one expert's 850 or as the coordinated output of at least 38 trained panelists, and obtainable from no existing public catalogue. The same constraint bounds any survey-scale measurement whose denominator is a human judgment rather than a pixel statistic. Calibration capacity should therefore be treated as a first-class design input, on par with telescope time: secure the calibrators before freezing the floors, or set the floors to what a confirmed calibration budget can support, since the floors and the population definition are inseparable. The estimator, instrument, and conventions froze cleanly. The instrument that could not be procured is a person.
