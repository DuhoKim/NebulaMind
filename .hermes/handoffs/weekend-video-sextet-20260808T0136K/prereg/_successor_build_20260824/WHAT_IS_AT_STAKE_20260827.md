# What is at stake

2026-08-27, Hwao, for Duho. Written in plain words on purpose. No digests, no slot names, no
gate vocabulary — those live in the receipts, and this is the thing the receipts are in service
of.

## The question

Galaxies are not smooth blobs. Most of the ones we can see clearly are spirals, and a spiral has
a direction: its arms wind one way or the other. From where we sit, some appear to turn
clockwise and some counter-clockwise.

If you picked a thousand spiral galaxies at random, you would expect roughly five hundred of
each. Nothing in physics as we currently understand it says the universe should prefer one
handedness over the other, and nothing says the preference should depend on which way you look.

**In 2011 Michael Longo said it does.** He reported that in one direction of the sky there is a
slight excess of one handedness, and in the opposite direction a slight excess of the other —
about a four percent lean, along one particular axis. Not a large effect. But not zero either.

That is the question this study asks, and only that: **is Longo's specific claim true?**

## Why anyone cares

Modern cosmology rests on an assumption so basic it is easy to forget it is an assumption: that
the universe has no preferred direction. Rotate it, and it looks the same. Every map, every
model, every age and distance we quote is built on top of that.

A four percent handedness lean along a fixed axis would be a preferred direction. It would mean
either that the universe carries some large-scale rotation or twist left over from its
beginning, or that something in how structure formed lined up the spins of galaxies across
distances far larger than anyone expects them to talk to each other.

Either would matter. Not "textbooks rewritten tomorrow" — one measurement never does that — but
it would be a genuine crack in an assumption everything else stands on, and it would be
falsifiable, which is rarer in cosmology than it should be.

There is also a reason to be sceptical, and it is the interesting part. **Effects of exactly
this size are the natural home of mistakes.** Which way a galaxy appears to spin is a judgement
call made from a small, faint image. If the people or programs making that judgement lean even
slightly one way, and the sky is not evenly surveyed, you can manufacture a four percent signal
out of nothing at all. Several claimed detections of this kind have died that way. So the claim
is worth testing precisely because it sits where truth and artefact are hardest to tell apart.

## What "yes" would mean, and what "no" would mean

**If the answer is yes** — we see the lean, at the size and the axis Longo named — that is a
published claim reproduced by an independent sample, announced in advance. It would not settle
the matter. It would make the matter urgent: other groups would test it, and the first question
everyone would ask is whether our classification of spin direction is biased. We would have to
survive that question. But a preregistered confirmation is a strong thing, and it would put a
real crack in the assumption of no preferred direction.

**If the answer is no** — and this is the more likely outcome, because most claims of this kind
do not reproduce — then a fifteen-year-old claim has been tested by a sample chosen to be
capable of seeing it, and it was not there. That is a real result. It is not a discovery, and it
will not make anyone's day. But "we looked properly and it is not there" is exactly what stops a
field chasing a ghost, and it is only worth anything if the looking was done properly, which is
what all the machinery has been about.

What a no would **not** mean: it would not prove the universe is isotropic, it would not rule
out a smaller lean than the one we can see, and it would not settle the separate claims made by
other researchers using different methods. It answers one question, cleanly. That is the whole
design.

## Why this attempt, when the last one was stopped

The previous version of this study was stopped, and the reason is worth understanding because it
is the reason this one exists.

To detect a lean that depends on direction, you need galaxies spread out along the direction
you are testing — ideally, plenty at both ends of that axis. The previous sample had two hundred
thousand galaxies, which sounds like plenty, but they were **all bunched near one end**. Every
one of them sat at nearly the same angle to the axis in question. It is the difference between
surveying a room from every corner and surveying it from one corner: more measurements do not
help if they are all in the same place.

There was a check meant to catch exactly that, and it had been recorded as passed — but the
number used came from a different, much larger group of galaxies, not from the sample actually
collected. Measured properly, the sample scored about a third of what it needed. Nobody lied.
Two documents were each correct and nobody put them side by side.

So that study was declined. Not paused, not amended — a study like this makes its promises
before it looks at the data, and once the data exist you cannot edit the promise, only end it
and write a new one.

**The new sample is chosen for the thing being measured.** Sixty-five thousand galaxies —
fewer, deliberately — picked so that roughly half sit at one end of the tested axis and half at
the other. On the measure that matters it scores about thirteen times better than the old
sample, and it clears the threshold the design requires with about twenty percent margin.
Fewer galaxies, better placed, and a fifth of the data to download.

Crucially, the redesign was driven by **where the galaxies sit**, not by any look at what they
show. Nobody has seen the answer. That is what makes reusing the old material legitimate, and it
is the one thing that must not be quietly compromised later.

## What is actually being risked

**About 148 gigabytes of images and something on the order of a month.** That is the concrete
cost: the download, the processing, the measurement, the checking.

**The sensitivity is real but conditional.** The calculation says that if the effect is there at
the claimed size, this sample would see it about ninety-nine and a half times out of a hundred.
That number is now measured properly rather than assumed — it was overstated once and had to be
withdrawn and re-done. But it is a statement about the *geometry* of the sample. It assumes the
measurement itself is clean.

**And the measurement is the hard part, and it is still ahead of us.** Deciding which way sixty-
five thousand galaxies spin, from small faint images, without leaning even slightly one way, is
the step where this kind of study usually goes wrong. Everything built so far — the checking,
the sealing, the independent review — is aimed at that step. None of it has been tested against
the step itself yet.

**One thing has not been checked by anyone.** The written promise this study runs on — what will
be measured, how, and what counts as a yes or a no — has never been reviewed by an outside
referee. The machinery around it has been reviewed five times. The promise itself has not. That
is the largest untested piece and I would not want it discovered late.

**And the honest bottom line:** we may spend 148 gigabytes and a month to learn that a
fifteen-year-old claim does not reproduce in a well-chosen sample. That is the most likely
single outcome. It is worth knowing, it is publishable, and it is not a discovery. Anyone
deciding to spend the month should decide it with that in front of them, not with the four
percent lean in front of them.

## Where it stands right now

Nothing has been downloaded. The part that computes exactly which images are needed — the piece
that took five rounds of review and one outside reviewer to settle — is finished and locked. The
sensitivity calculation is restored and passes. The written promise is not finished, has eleven
of twelve required pieces still missing, and has not been refereed.

So: the instrument is close to ready and better aimed than the last one. The question it is
aimed at is small, specific, and worth answering either way. And the thing most likely to
decide whether the answer means anything is the part nobody has reviewed yet.
