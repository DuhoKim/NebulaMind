# Galaxy Evolution

## Overview: Galaxy Evolution As A Regulated Baryon Cycle

Galaxy evolution is the study of how dark matter halos, gas accretion, star formation, feedback, environment, and cosmic time combine to build the observed galaxy population. A useful way to read the field is as a regulated baryon cycle: halos assemble and accrete gas, galaxies convert only part of that gas into stars, feedback returns energy and material to the circumgalactic medium, and quenching processes eventually reduce or shut down star formation.

The strongest compact picture is not a single formation path but a set of coupled reservoirs. Large-scale cosmological simulations supply the hierarchical structure-formation frame, while galaxy surveys test those predictions against observed satellite kinematics, clustering, gas fractions, and stellar populations. The same evidence base also keeps the field honest about efficiency: even where gas is available, the conversion of baryons into long-lived stars is globally incomplete rather than automatic.

## Dark Matter Halos And Baryon Conversion Efficiency

Dark matter halos provide the gravitational scaffolding for galaxy formation, but halo mass alone does not determine how efficiently baryons become stars. At the low-mass end, shallow potential wells make galaxies sensitive to stellar feedback: <!--claim:2905-->Stellar feedback suppresses star formation in lower mass halos.<!--/claim:2905--> In the same regime, feedback is not only an after-the-fact correction to star formation, because <!--claim:2906-->in low-mass halos, stellar feedback processes regulate the baryon cycle.<!--/claim:2906-->

The relationship between halo history and galaxy properties is less settled than a one-parameter halo-mass model would suggest. Current evidence leaves assembly history as a contested secondary axis: <!--claim:2912-->halo assembly history shapes galaxy properties.<!--/claim:2912--> Similarly, <!--claim:2918-->the properties of a galaxy are shaped by its host dark matter halo.<!--/claim:2918--> That statement is best read conditionally rather than deterministically, because metallicity, large-scale environment, feedback timing, and gas supply can redirect similar halos into different visible outcomes. Low-surface-brightness galaxy studies sharpen this point by treating halo assembly and multiscale environment as coupled variables rather than interchangeable labels for halo mass.

Baryonic cooling is the entry point from halo growth to star formation, but it is not a guarantee of efficient galaxy growth. Cooling gas can collapse into dense stellar systems, while gas that remains hot is pressure-supported and can delay further accretion. This is why host-halo properties help separate star-forming and quiescent central galaxies without reducing quenching to halo mass alone. Some central-galaxy analyses identify a transition near $M_h\sim10^{12.1}\,M_\odot$, but that threshold is best read as dataset- and model-dependent context rather than a universal switch.

At the massive end and at cosmic noon, quiescent systems probe the coupling between stellar shutdown and halo growth. <!--claim:2920-->Massive quiescent galaxies at $z\sim2-3$ are hosted by dark matter halos with masses of approximately $10^{12.5}$-$10^{13}\,M_\odot$.<!--/claim:2920--> That scale sits near the regime where virial heating, reduced cold accretion, and maintenance feedback become central to the interpretation.

## Gas Supply, Star Formation, And Feedback

The gas cycle links accretion to the interstellar medium and back out to the circumgalactic medium. Stellar feedback is the clearest bridge between local star formation and galaxy-scale regulation: <!--claim:2909-->stellar feedback drives galactic outflows.<!--/claim:2909--> Those outflows can remove low-entropy gas, stir the circumgalactic medium, regulate metal retention, and change the later supply available for star formation.

Feedback also operates below galaxy scale, where young stars can compress cloud edges, disperse parent molecular clouds, and regulate the local efficiency of star formation. Those cloud-scale links should be treated as context for the larger baryon cycle rather than as a single universal recipe, because radiation fields, supernovae, winds, and cosmic-ray transport couple differently across environments.

Not every proposed feedback channel is equally settled. In dusty, optically thick star-forming environments, radiation pressure remains a live point of tension: <!--claim:2916-->radiation pressure is a significant feedback mechanism in environments with high optical depth to UV photons.<!--/claim:2916--> That mechanism should be weighed beside supernovae, stellar winds, cosmic rays, and radiation-hydrodynamic effects rather than treated as universally dominant.

Gas depletion time is one way to connect the instantaneous star-formation rate with the available molecular reservoir. In old stellar systems, gas can persist without rapidly forming stars: <!--claim:2911-->early-type galaxies can exhibit molecular gas depletion timescales exceeding 10 Gyr.<!--/claim:2911--> That long-timescale boundary helps separate the presence of molecular gas from the efficiency with which a galaxy turns it into new stars.

## Quenching And Maintenance Feedback

Quenching is not a single event. It can be rapid or slow, central or environmental, preventive or ejective, and it can leave different signatures in stellar populations, gas reservoirs, and morphology. At cosmic noon, AGN-linked shutdown is often discussed as a fast channel: <!--claim:2913-->the quenching of star formation in massive galaxies by AGN feedback at $z\sim2$ is a rapid process.<!--/claim:2913-->

The strongest AGN-feedback synthesis is kinetic and maintenance-oriented rather than a claim that every AGN episode quenches a galaxy. <!--claim:2915-->Active galactic nuclei can drive feedback through a kinetic mode, where powerful jets and outflows mechanically interact with the surrounding medium.<!--/claim:2915--> Nearby systems such as the Whirlpool provide resolved examples of jet-ISM coupling, while accretion-flow models emphasize that black-hole feeding, spin, jet power, and feedback geometry can regulate one another. Ultra-fast outflow detections and compact jets from weakly accreting black holes broaden the phenomenology, but they are better used as evidence that AGN energy can leave the nucleus through several channels than as proof of a single self-regulating equilibrium. Over longer timescales, <!--claim:2924-->AGN feedback heats the gas reservoirs of massive galaxies.<!--/claim:2924--> Together these claims support a picture in which jets, winds, and hot-halo heating can reduce the cooling flow that would otherwise refuel star formation.

Central structure is another route into quenching. <!--claim:2917-->Central galaxy quenching is influenced by properties such as velocity dispersion, bulge mass, and central black hole mass.<!--/claim:2917--> The empirical M-sigma relation is one reason these central observables are not independent bookkeeping variables: black-hole mass and bulge kinematics track one another, though with morphology-dependent scatter. In the same family of observables, <!--claim:2921-->the growth of central stellar mass density is linked to mass quenching.<!--/claim:2921-->

Single-galaxy studies add texture without settling the whole causal chain. Circumnuclear observations of Centaurus A, for example, connect multiphase gas, warm molecular material, and black-hole activity in a nearby host; they support a coevolutionary reading, but not a universal proof that every black hole episode sets the fate of its galaxy.

## Environment, Morphology, And Structural Growth

Environment changes galaxies by stripping, starving, perturbing, and merging them. In clusters, environmental processing can transform star-forming disks into passive lenticular systems: <!--claim:2914-->the morphological transformation of quenched disks in clusters results in the formation of S0 galaxies.<!--/claim:2914--> That is a structural statement as much as a star-formation statement, because it links a galaxy's orbit and gas loss to its final morphology.

Ram-pressure stripping is the cleanest environmental gas-removal channel in the current source set. Long stripped tails in Abell 1367 show how intracluster-medium shocks can reshape the atomic gas content of infalling spirals, and a post-starburst system at $z=3.06$ shows that direct gas removal can operate early in cosmic time. In some dwarf-satellite settings, the immediate suppression of star formation is better described as direct ISM removal than slow starvation, though that balance remains environment-dependent.

Environment can also leave structural signals that are harder to translate into a single quenching clock. Simulations and cluster-region studies connect tidal interactions, lopsided stellar and HI components, group preprocessing, cluster infall, and late-type alignments in cosmic-web filaments to morphological change. These links are best used as context around the main gas-removal picture rather than as a claim that environment acts identically in every density regime.

Mergers are a second structural channel, but different merger regimes should be separated. The minor-merger route remains contested: <!--claim:2922-->mass growth from minor mergers is consistent with the observed size evolution of massive elliptical galaxies.<!--/claim:2922--> Major mergers are more direct events in this section's narrative: <!--claim:2923-->major mergers can increase galaxy size by a factor of approximately two while doubling the stellar mass.<!--/claim:2923--> Both channels can matter without either one explaining all size growth.

## Chemical Enrichment And Cosmic Timing

Chemical enrichment records the integrated history of star formation, inflow, and outflow. Metallicity scaling relations therefore act as clocks and regulators: they encode how much gas has been processed into stars, how much enriched material has been lost, and how much low-metallicity gas has diluted the interstellar medium. <!--claim:2910-->The fundamental metallicity relation is consistent to within approximately 0.1 dex in oxygen abundance from redshift $z=0$ to $z\sim2.3$.<!--/claim:2910--> That stability constrains baryon-cycle models across a large fraction of cosmic time.

This timing perspective also explains why galaxy evolution is not simply a sequence from blue disks to red spheroids. Different galaxies can enter the same observed class by different routes: some through early gas exhaustion, some through central quenching, some through environmental processing, and some through late structural assembly. Compact star-forming dwarfs such as Blueberry and Green Pea analogs add a useful caution: low-density environments can host chemically active, low-mass systems without making environment a one-directional predictor of gas content or future quenching.

## The High-Redshift And Reionization Frontier

At high redshift, galaxy evolution connects directly to reionization and to the first dense stellar systems. Environmental effects can appear early in the low-mass population: <!--claim:2919-->environmental quenching can suppress star formation in low-mass galaxies at high redshift.<!--/claim:2919--> The same era also raises the question of which sources supplied enough ionizing photons to reionize the intergalactic medium.

Globular-cluster progenitors are one candidate source population. <!--claim:2925-->Globular clusters are sources of ionizing photons during the epoch of reionization ($z\gt 6$).<!--/claim:2925--> The relative contribution of those systems remains unsettled rather than a settled replacement for faint galaxies: <!--claim:2926-->the ionizing photon contribution from faint galaxies during the reionization epoch is secondary to that of proto-globular clusters.<!--/claim:2926--> Current evidence is mixed, so the competing source populations should remain side by side.

## Open Tensions And Evidence Gaps

Some boundary claims are useful because they define what future evidence needs to resolve, but they should not anchor the main conclusions. In compact starbursts and mergers, <!--claim:2907-->the molecular gas depletion timescale in starbursts and galaxy mergers can be as short as about 100 Myr.<!--/claim:2907--> That is a short-timescale boundary to inspect, not a settled universal starburst rule.

The role of environment in massive isolated galaxies is another boundary case. <!--claim:2908-->Environmental effects are not a primary quenching mechanism in isolated massive galaxies.<!--/claim:2908--> That statement is useful because it separates isolated systems from clusters and groups, but it should remain caveated until its evidence is reconciled with the broader environmental-quenching literature.
