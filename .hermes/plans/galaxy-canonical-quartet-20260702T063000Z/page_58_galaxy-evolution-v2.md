## Overview & Historical Framework

Galaxy evolution connects the growth of dark-matter halos to the visible history of stars, gas, dust, black holes, and environment. The modern picture is not a single sequence from blue spirals to red ellipticals; it is a set of coupled channels. Halos assemble hierarchically, gas accretes and cools, star formation consumes and enriches that gas, feedback redistributes energy and metals, and dense environments can remove or heat reservoirs before galaxies would have quenched on their own.

The red sequence, green valley, and blue cloud remain useful observational landmarks, but they are outcomes rather than mechanisms. A reader-facing synthesis should therefore start with causal structure: what sets the baryon supply, what regulates star formation, what shuts galaxies down, and which observations distinguish internal feedback from external processing.

## Physical Mechanisms

The basic mechanisms are mass assembly, gas supply, angular-momentum redistribution, star formation, chemical enrichment, feedback, and dynamical transformation. Gas-rich disks can grow gradually through accretion and mergers; compact spheroids and quenched systems require either rapid gas consumption, suppressed inflow, heating, stripping, or morphological stabilization that makes remaining gas less efficient at forming stars.

This page should avoid pretending that every mechanism maps cleanly to one observational label. The same outflow can remove cold gas, stir turbulence, heat circumgalactic material, enrich the halo, or compress gas locally. The same dense environment can drive ram-pressure stripping, tidal heating, starvation, harassment, pre-processing in groups, and morphology-density correlations. The useful synthesis is the one that keeps these channels distinct while showing how they combine.

## Dark Matter & Structure Formation

Dark matter sets the gravitational scaffold. Halo mass and assembly history regulate the baryon supply, merger rate, angular momentum, and the depth of the potential well that feedback must overcome. The live page already captures early-galaxy and halo-assembly evidence, but the stronger article structure should integrate high-redshift tensions into this section instead of isolating them as a generic “frontier.”

High-redshift massive-galaxy claims should be written cautiously: JWST has sharpened the tension between inferred stellar masses and expected halo abundances, but stellar-population modeling, dust, emission-line contamination, AGN light, IMF assumptions, and spectroscopic completeness all affect the interpretation. The best current prose is therefore conditional: the observations are powerful tests of galaxy-formation efficiency and modeling assumptions, not automatically a falsification of ΛCDM.

## Star Formation & Quenching

Star formation follows the available cold gas supply, the density and turbulence of that gas, and the timescale over which inflow replenishes it. Quenching can occur when gas is consumed, removed, heated, stabilized, or prevented from cooling back onto the disk. That means “gas depletion” is real but too broad as a standalone explanation; it should be linked to the mechanism that changes the reservoir.

<!-- claim:2930-->Gas removal and depletion can suppress star formation by reducing the usable cold-gas reservoir, but the evidence should distinguish true reservoir loss from morphological quenching, turbulent regulation, and environment-specific stripping.<!--/claim:2930-->

Cold-gas loss is best treated as a family of pathways rather than a single mechanism. Reservoir removal, suppressed inflow, turbulent regulation, morphological stabilization, and cluster-specific stripping can all reduce star formation, but they leave different observational signatures and should not be collapsed into one generic “depletion” label.

## AGN Feedback

AGN feedback is a central but conditional regulator of galaxy growth. Observations and simulations connect jets, outflows, turbulence, and hot-gas coupling to reduced star formation in some systems, while other cases show that outflows can compress gas and trigger star formation locally. The useful synthesis is therefore not “AGN feedback always quenches”; it is “AGN feedback is mode-, phase-, scale-, and environment-dependent.”

<!-- claim:2929-->Internal AGN feedback can regulate or quench star formation through jets, outflows, turbulence, circumgalactic heating, and starvation, but the sign and strength of the effect depend on feedback mode and gas phase, and positive feedback can occur locally.<!--/claim:2929-->

Evidence snapshot: radio-jet simulations and observations show that jets can inflate bubbles and stir the interstellar or circumgalactic medium even when radio luminosity is modest. Molecular-gas studies of nearby AGN hosts show that outflow-driven turbulence can make cold gas less efficient at forming stars, while population simulations often require AGN feedback to reproduce observed galaxy demographics. High-redshift QSO observations add direct evidence for fast, galaxy-scale ionized outflows that avoid the brightest star-forming regions. The same evidence base also motivates the positive-feedback caveat: compressed gas can form stars locally even when the galaxy-wide effect is suppressive.

## Environmental Effects

Environment changes the boundary conditions of galaxy evolution. Infall into groups and clusters exposes galaxies to ram pressure, tidal effects, starvation, high-speed encounters, group pre-processing, and the thermodynamic state of the intracluster or intragroup medium. These effects are strongest for satellites and low-mass galaxies, but the observational signature can be mixed because mass, morphology, orbit, and gas fraction covary.

Evidence snapshot: simulations used to interpret high-redshift JWST quenched low-mass galaxies show that many plausible analogues are satellites, making environmental quenching a viable explanation in those cases. Cosmic-web studies also support the broader idea that filaments, sheets, and nodes can torque protogalactic gas and influence later accretion geometry. Satellite quenching still needs careful timing language: a delayed-then-rapid pathway depends on orbit, mass, gas fraction, redshift, and how the shutdown timescale is measured.

## Observational Evidence

The evidence base is multi-wavelength and redshift-dependent. Rest-frame optical and near-infrared spectra constrain stellar populations, nebular lines, metallicities, and dust. Sub-millimetre and radio observations constrain hidden star formation and molecular or atomic gas. X-ray and radio signatures identify AGN power and hot atmospheres. Integral-field spectroscopy maps outflows, rotation, turbulence, and spatially resolved quenching. Large imaging and spectroscopic surveys connect individual mechanisms to population statistics.

The main editorial improvement is to stop presenting “debates” as separate article furniture. Disputed points should be absorbed into the topical section where they belong: high-z mass budgets under structure formation, dusty cosmic star formation under star formation history, AGN coupling under feedback, and satellite/cosmic-web processing under environmental effects.

## Current Surveys & Missions

Current and near-future facilities should be framed by what uncertainty they reduce. JWST constrains rest-frame optical diagnostics at high redshift; ALMA and radio facilities trace molecular gas, dust-obscured star formation, and cold reservoirs; Euclid, Rubin, DESI, and related wide surveys connect galaxy populations to large-scale environment, weak lensing, clustering, and cosmic-web structure. The section should not become a mission list. It should explain which physical ambiguity each observing mode can adjudicate.

## Synthesis: What Regulates Galaxy Evolution?

Galaxy evolution is regulated by the supply of baryons, the conversion of gas into stars, the return of energy and metals through feedback, and the environments that reshape gas reservoirs after infall. The clearest picture is causal rather than linear: halo growth creates the gravitational setting, gas accretion and recycling provide fuel, stellar and AGN feedback regulate how efficiently that fuel forms stars, and groups, clusters, and cosmic-web geometry alter the boundary conditions. The open frontier is not whether any one mechanism matters, but when each dominates, how strongly it couples to observable gas phases, and how those answers change with mass, redshift, and environment.
